# Value-Based Pricing

Value-based pricing sets price according to the value customers believe they will receive, not according to what it costs you to build the product. In [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-A-Bonus-Material]], Ash Maurya treats pricing as a core viability assumption in the business model, not a late-stage detail. In the introduction and design sections of the book, he further reinforces that price is part of the product, and that the business model-not just the solution-is the real object being designed and tested [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp.11-58].

The validation section adds an important practical reminder: pricing should be treated as part of the same learning system as activation, retention, and referral. In other words, pricing is not an isolated finance decision; it is one of the assumptions that must be tested as the customer factory evolves [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp.229-237]. The 90-day cycle chapter extends that idea further: pricing assumptions should be reviewed and refined inside the regular model-prioritize-test cadence, because viability changes as the business model is validated over time [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]], pp.238-269].

Chapter 8 adds a crucial missing piece: value-based pricing depends on deeply understanding customer problems, existing alternatives, and switching triggers. If you can describe a customer's pain and desired outcome better than they can, you gain the right to make a stronger value claim-and a stronger price claim. Maurya's customer discovery process is therefore not only about validation; it also improves pricing by revealing what customers are already paying, what they are trying to avoid, and what would actually motivate a switch [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-345].

Price shapes perception, positions the offering, and helps determine which customers you attract. Because of that, pricing should be tested early alongside [[concepts/traction]], [[concepts/churn]], and other assumptions in the [[concepts/customer-factory]].

> [!note]- New from later chapters
> Chapter 8 makes the customer side of pricing more explicit: you cannot anchor price well unless you understand the customer's job-to-be-done, their switching trigger, and the cost of their current alternative. This strengthens the earlier viability argument by tying price not just to business math, but to customer discovery and behavior [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-345].

## Core Idea

The book argues that price should sit between two anchors:

1. **The floor** - what customers already spend in time, money, and effort on [[concepts/innovators-gift|existing alternatives]]
2. **The ceiling** - the monetary value customers expect from your [[concepts/innovators-gift|unique value proposition]]

The goal is to find a price that is fair to customers while still allowing the business model to work.

> Price is not just a number on a plan. It is a signal of value, positioning, and business viability.

## Why It Matters in Running Lean

In the viability stress test, Maurya uses a [[concepts/fermi-estimate]] to check whether a business model can realistically reach its [[concepts/minimum-success-criteria]]. Once a model appears too weak, pricing becomes one of the most powerful levers for correction.

The introduction broadens this point: investors today fund **business models that work**, not just solutions that work. That makes price a strategic variable from the start, because it changes the model's revenue math, the number of customers required, and the story you can credibly pitch [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp.11-49].

For Steve, the example entrepreneur in the book, the original assumption of $50/month was too low for the number of customers he would need to reach $10M ARR in three years. After running the numbers, he realizes he may need to charge $250/month or even $500/month to make the model viable.

That insight changes the entire business model:
- fewer customers are needed
- acquisition pressure drops
- churn becomes less punishing
- the idea becomes more plausible on paper

Later, Chapter 8 shows that this logic should be grounded in a deeper understanding of customer behavior. In other words, the strongest prices come from a mix of viability math and customer discovery: the more clearly you understand the problem, the easier it is to justify a price that reflects the value of switching [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-345].

## How the Book Frames Pricing

### 1. Price is part of the product
Customers do not evaluate price separately from value. A higher price can make a product seem more premium, while a lower price can signal something more basic or commoditized.

### 2. Price defines your customer segment
If you raise or lower price substantially, you may be serving a different kind of customer. Pricing and segmentation are tightly linked.

### 3. Price is early validation
Getting customers to pay is one of the strongest forms of validation. Revenue is evidence that people value the solution enough to exchange money for it.

### 4. Cost-based pricing is backward
The book warns against pricing based on internal costs plus margin. Customers do not care what it cost you to build the solution; they care about what it helps them achieve.

### 5. Existing alternatives matter
Customers compare your price not just against your features, but against the full cost of doing the job the old way. That makes [[concepts/innovators-gift|the Innovator's Gift]] central to pricing.

### 6. Pricing should be revisited in cycles
A first price is a hypothesis. In the Continuous Innovation process, you revisit that hypothesis during 90-day cycles, alongside the rest of the business model. That keeps pricing connected to real learning rather than frozen assumptions.

### 7. Customer discovery sharpens pricing
Chapter 8 extends the logic by showing that pricing improves when you understand switching triggers, existing alternatives, desired outcomes, and friction. If you know what customers are already paying to get the job done-and what they are trying to avoid-you can position price around value instead of guesswork [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-345].

### 8. Pricing is part of solution design
The new Chapter 9 material makes pricing even more concrete: when designing an MVP to cause a switch, pricing is one of the five P's that must be aligned with problem, promise, people, and packaging. A solution can be desirable and feasible, but if the price is wrong, the offer may still fail to convert [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-9-Design-Your-Solution-to-Cause-a-Switch]], pp.346-367].

### 9. Pricing becomes part of the offer
Chapter 10 adds an important final step: pricing is not just a model assumption or MVP variable; it is part of the **mafia offer**. The offer must be framed against the real competition, show a clear better outcome, and ask customers to make a concrete commitment. In this sense, price is part of the story that causes a switch [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-10-Deliver-a-Mafia-Offer-Your-Customers-Cannot-Refuse]], pp.368-401].

### 10. Pricing gets reviewed after each cycle
Chapter 11 makes pricing more operational again: at the 90-day cycle review, the team updates the Lean Canvas and traction roadmap, then decides whether to persevere, pivot, or pause. That means the pricing assumption is part of the reviewable system, not a one-time decision [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-11-Run-a-90-Day-Cycle-Review]], pp.402-423].

### 11. Pricing must fit the launch strategy
Chapter 12 brings pricing into launch readiness: once the MVP is being prepared for early access, pricing has to fit the product launch plan, the customer factory metrics, and the staged rollout. In other words, price is not separate from launch mechanics; it is part of how the team validates value delivery with early adopters [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-12-Get-Ready-to-Launch]], pp.424-439].

### 12. Pricing remains tied to making happy customers
Chapter 13 makes the post-launch implication explicit: products must make happy customers, meaning customers who achieve real outcomes, not merely feel good. Pricing still matters here because the product must continue delivering enough value to justify repeated use, retention, and renewal. Pricing therefore remains linked to the customer's ongoing perception of value, not just the initial sale [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-13-Make-Happy-Customers]], pp.440-466].

### 13. Pricing also has to fuel growth
Chapter 14 extends the logic to sustainable growth. A pricing model is only useful if it can help power a growth rocket-whether through revenue reinvestment, content/data, or referrals. In this view, pricing is not just about reaching viability; it also affects whether the business can build a scalable and renewable growth loop [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-14-Find-Your-Growth-Rocket]], pp.467-487].

> [!warning]- Potential tension across chapters
> Early chapters encourage quick modeling and rough estimates, but later chapters show that pricing must become much more evidence-based as discovery, offer delivery, launch readiness, and growth planning improve. The apparent conflict is resolved by treating early price as a hypothesis and later price as something to refine through customer discovery, offer testing, cycle reviews, and growth-loop learning [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-12-Get-Ready-to-Launch]], pp.424-439].

## Practical Questions to Ask

When using value-based pricing, the book implicitly asks founders to think through questions like:

- What problem are customers trying to solve today?
- What do they currently spend to solve it?
- How valuable is the outcome if our product works?
- Which customer segment feels that value most strongly?
- Can the business model still work if we charge more and serve fewer customers?
- What price makes sense when reviewed inside our next 90-day cycle?
- What switching trigger would make the customer pay more to change solutions?
- Does the price support the offer we need to cause a switch?
- Does the price also support a sustainable growth loop later?

## Example from the Book

Steve initially picks a $50/month subscription price for his no-code AR/VR platform. After doing the viability math, he sees that this price forces him to acquire too many customers to hit his goals.

He then reframes pricing using customer value:
- A typical AR/VR app may take 200 hours to build
- At $50-75/hour, that can mean $10K-15K in labor
- If his product saves most of that effort, a much higher monthly price may still be fair

This is the logic of value-based pricing: if customers receive a large economic benefit, the product can capture a meaningful share of that value.

In the later 90-day cycle material, Steve carries this logic into his first cycle kickoff. He compares pricing across customer segments and realizes that a $500/month assumption is the minimum price that keeps the roadmap viable for both software companies and architects, reinforcing that pricing is a live modeling variable rather than a fixed constant [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-7-Kick-Off-Your-First-90-Day-Cycle]], pp.270-299].

Chapter 8 deepens this further: for architects and homeowners, value only becomes legible when the interviewer understands the home-building or design job in context. That means the best pricing anchor may not be the AR/VR tool itself, but the value of saving time, improving sales, or helping a client visualize and commit to a house design [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-345].

The Chapter 9 update adds an important refinement: pricing is not only a viability calculation; it is also part of the MVP design itself. When Steve evaluates the 5 P's of MVP, he treats pricing as a constraint on how the solution is packaged and delivered, which shows that value-based pricing must work together with problem clarity, promise, and delivery format [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-9-Design-Your-Solution-to-Cause-a-Switch]], pp.346-367].

The epilogue's manifesto closes the loop: entrepreneurs should love the problem, build business models that work, and pay attention to traction-not rely on cost-based thinking or hope [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-15-Epilogue]], pp.488-494].

## Common Pitfalls

- Pricing too low to "get adoption"
- Anchoring price to development cost instead of customer value
- Treating price as a late-stage decision
- Underestimating how pricing reshapes the target market
- Forgetting to compare against existing alternatives
- Confusing a cheap entry price with a viable business model
- Setting a price once and never revisiting it during validation cycles
- Failing to ground pricing in customer discovery and switching triggers
- Designing an MVP without checking whether the price fits the offer
- Assuming pricing stops mattering once growth begins

> [!note]- Pricing conflict to watch for
> The book's early design chapters encourage founders to keep their first Lean Canvas rough and fast, but the viability chapter shows that pricing can invalidate an idea very quickly. So while the first estimate can be approximate, pricing itself should not be treated as an afterthought. The later 90-day cycle chapter adds that pricing should keep evolving as the team learns, and Chapter 8 shows that it gets stronger when you understand the customer's job-to-be-done and the trigger that caused them to switch [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-library.z-library.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-345].

> [!tip]- ELI5
> Charge based on the value a customer gets, not on what it cost you to build.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> Like pricing a shortcut by how much time and stress it saves, not by the cost of the road sign.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: value-based pricing means charging the maximum possible price. Reality: it means finding a fair price between the old solution's cost and the value of switching.
> Myth: pricing can wait until after launch. Reality: Running Lean treats price as an early viability assumption that must be tested.
> Myth: cost-plus pricing is safer. Reality: customers do not buy based on your costs; they buy based on their value and alternatives.
> Myth: low price always helps adoption. Reality: low price can weaken positioning and make the model impossible to scale.
> Myth: pricing is separate from MVP design. Reality: pricing is one of the 5 P's of MVP and must fit the offer.
