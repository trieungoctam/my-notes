# Build-Measure-Learn Loop

The **Build-Measure-Learn loop** is a rapid feedback cycle for testing assumptions, observing customer response, and deciding what to do next. In startup practice, it helps teams avoid building in the dark by turning ideas into experiments and experiments into evidence.

In [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]], Maurya treats this loop as useful but insufficient on its own. The loop works best when it is embedded in a broader system of [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], [[concepts/customer-factory]], and [[concepts/90-day-cycles]]. Otherwise, teams risk using it as a generic excuse to ship features instead of a disciplined way to learn.

## What the loop means

The loop has three parts:

1. **Build** - create the smallest thing that can test a hypothesis.
2. **Measure** - observe how customers actually behave.
3. **Learn** - interpret the results, update assumptions, and decide what to test next.

The point is not just to ship something. The point is to learn quickly enough to improve the business model before time and resources run out.

## How Running Lean reframes the loop

Maurya's treatment adds several important refinements:

### 1. Start with problems, not solutions
The loop should begin with a clear customer problem and a testable assumption, not with a solution looking for a use case. This aligns with the book's emphasis on [[concepts/innovators-gift]] and [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]].

### 2. Measure customer behavior, not vanity
In the book, useful measurement means tracking customer-centric outcomes such as acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral - the steps in the [[concepts/customer-factory]]. These are stronger indicators of traction than raw output metrics like features shipped.

### 3. Use experiments to test riskiest assumptions first
Maurya argues that teams should not test everything equally. The loop should be aimed at the current bottleneck or most uncertain assumption, consistent with [[concepts/theory-of-constraints]].

### 4. Put the loop inside a cadence
The loop is most effective when repeated inside a regular operating rhythm, especially the 90-day cycle. That cadence keeps learning externally accountable and prevents teams from endlessly "iterating" without making meaningful decisions.

## Why the loop matters

The loop is valuable because early-stage teams usually cannot predict what will work. They need a process that converts uncertainty into evidence. Build-Measure-Learn offers that process, but Running Lean shows that the loop must be disciplined:

- **Build** only enough to test the riskiest assumption.
- **Measure** the right customer behavior.
- **Learn** in a way that changes the model, not just the roadmap.

Used well, the loop helps teams move from [[concepts/lean-canvas]] assumptions to real traction.

## Relationship to other concepts

The Build-Measure-Learn loop sits inside a larger system of startup learning:

- [[concepts/lean-canvas]] defines the initial assumptions.
- [[concepts/customer-factory]] defines the metrics to watch.
- [[concepts/traction-roadmap]] defines the target trajectory.
- [[concepts/90-day-cycles]] define the rhythm of review and experimentation.
- [[concepts/innovators-gift]] helps teams choose the right problem and trigger.
- [[concepts/theory-of-constraints]] helps identify what to test next.

In this sense, the loop is not the whole method; it is the engine that powers the method.

## Common failure modes

- Building too much before testing.
- Measuring outputs instead of outcomes.
- Learning the wrong lesson because the original hypothesis was vague.
- Running experiments without a clear decision rule.
- Treating iteration as busywork instead of as a path to better evidence.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> The Build-Measure-Learn loop is a way to test ideas without guessing too much. First you make a small version of your idea, then you watch what people do, and then you use that information to improve or change your next version. It helps you learn quickly instead of spending months building something nobody wants.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like cooking a new recipe for a friend. You make a small batch, taste it, see what they like or dislike, and then adjust the ingredients for the next try instead of preparing a huge meal blindly.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> **Myth:** Build-Measure-Learn means shipping features as fast as possible. **Reality:** It means building the smallest test that can answer the riskiest question.
> **Myth:** Any metric counts as learning. **Reality:** The useful metrics are the ones that show customer behavior and business traction, not just activity.
> **Myth:** The loop works even if you start with a vague idea. **Reality:** Vague assumptions produce vague lessons; the loop is much more effective when anchored in a clear problem and hypothesis.
