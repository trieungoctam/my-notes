# Framework Combination for Innovation

Framework Combination for Innovation is the idea that breakthrough product work under uncertainty should not rely on a single method. Instead, teams combine multiple frameworks-such as Lean Startup, Design Thinking, Business Model Design, jobs-to-be-done, Systems Thinking, and Behavior Design-into one adaptable approach for learning, testing, and building.

This concept is central to [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-Preface-to-the-Third-Edition]] and is presented there as the basis for the [[concepts/continuous-innovation|Continuous Innovation Framework]]. The introduction expands the idea by showing why no single framework is sufficient in today's market: building is cheaper, competition is stronger, customers have more choices, and traction matters more than ideas alone. The later chapters deepen the concept by showing how customer discovery, [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]], and the [[concepts/customer-forces-canvas]] help explain why a new solution gets chosen over an old one.

## Core idea

Maurya argues that each framework has a specific superpower:

- **Lean Startup** helps teams run experiments and learn quickly.
- **Design Thinking** helps teams understand people and frame problems.
- **Business Model Design** helps teams shape a viable system for value creation and capture.
- **jobs-to-be-done** helps uncover what customers are really trying to accomplish.
- **Systems Thinking** helps reveal feedback loops, dependencies, and unintended consequences.
- **Behavior Design** helps turn interest into actual adoption and usage.

The point is not to choose one and ignore the others. The point is to use the right tool for the right uncertainty.

The introduction adds an important emphasis: the business model itself must be treated as the product. In other words, framework combination is not just about better ideation; it is about building a system for identifying the riskiest assumptions, validating them in stages, and proving traction before overcommitting to a solution.

## Why combination matters

Early product development is messy. Teams usually do not fail because they lack effort; they fail because they use the wrong lens for the problem they are facing. A single framework can be powerful, but it also creates blind spots.

A combined approach helps teams:

- identify real customer problems before building
- test business assumptions more rigorously
- design solutions that fit both user needs and business constraints
- avoid overcommitting to one worldview too early
- adapt as new evidence changes the plan

This is especially important when the team is trying to move from [[concepts/validation]] and [[concepts/customer-discovery]] toward a workable business model.

The later chapter on customer understanding sharpens this point further. Maurya argues that discovery interviews are not about asking customers to state problems directly; they are about uncovering the switching trigger, existing alternatives, friction, and desired outcomes. That means framework combination is also about using the right method for the right discovery task-not just "using more tools."

> [!note]
> The book's later customer-discovery material makes the framework-combination idea more specific: the right mix is not random. It begins with problem discovery, then uses business-model modeling, then validation, then stage-based rollout.

## Relationship to Continuous Innovation

In the preface, Maurya describes this synthesis as a **framework of frameworks**: the [[concepts/continuous-innovation|Continuous Innovation Framework]]. That framing suggests innovation is not a one-time process but a repeating cycle of discovery, stress testing, and learning.

The introduction ties this directly to a practical operating system for entrepreneurs:

- **Model** the business.
- **Prioritize** the riskiest assumptions.
- **Test** them with small experiments.

The customer-discovery chapter extends that system into a concrete research practice. Instead of treating interviews, canvases, and roadmaps as separate activities, Maurya shows they work together:

- the Lean Canvas captures assumptions,
- the customer interview uncovers real behavior,
- the Customer Forces Canvas organizes the story,
- and the resulting insights feed back into the business model.

This connects directly to [[concepts/iteration]] and [[concepts/uncertainty]]: the team begins with Plan A, then keeps refining the problem, customer, solution, and business model until the plan actually works.

## How the framework stack works in practice

The book suggests a layered use of frameworks across the startup journey.

### 1. Frame the problem
Use [[concepts/design-thinking]] and [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] to understand what people are trying to accomplish and where the existing solution fails.

### 2. Model the business
Use [[concepts/lean-canvas]] and [[concepts/business-model-design]] to make the assumptions explicit.

### 3. Stress test the model
Use [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] tools like Fermi estimates, traction roadmaps, and staged validation to test desirability, viability, and feasibility.

### 4. Learn from real behavior
Use the [[concepts/customer-forces-canvas]] and [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] lens to uncover switching triggers, inertia, friction, and pull.

### 5. Design adoption, not just features
Use [[concepts/behavior-design]] to help customers actually adopt the solution, not merely notice it.

## How the introduction extends the concept

The introduction gives the idea of framework combination a sharper edge by showing the modern startup context:

- Building is easier than before.
- Competition is much higher.
- Customers have more choices.
- Investors value traction, not just ideas.

In that environment, the key question is not whether the team can build something. It is whether the team can build the **right** thing. Maurya's answer is to combine frameworks around the riskiest uncertainties in sequence:

1. Start with the problem.
2. Model the business.
3. Validate with customers.
4. Define an MVP only after problem/solution fit.
5. Launch in stages.
6. Learn continuously.

That sequence shows why framework combination is fundamentally about **timing** as much as method. Use the right framework at the right stage.

## How customer discovery deepens the concept

The customer-discovery chapter adds one of the clearest practical uses of framework combination.

Maurya explains that customer discovery interviews should not begin by asking people about problems. Instead, they should start with the customer's current job, current solution, and the events that caused them to switch. That means the interview process blends several frameworks at once:

- [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] to define the job
- [[concepts/customer-forces-canvas]] to capture push, pull, inertia, and friction
- [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] to turn insights into validated campaigns
- [[concepts/business-model-design]] to turn those insights into a more viable offer

This is where the book becomes highly operational: frameworks are not competing theories, but complementary lenses for the same customer story.

## Practical implications

Using multiple frameworks well means:

1. **Start with problem understanding** rather than solution enthusiasm.
2. **Choose the method that fits the question** you are trying to answer.
3. **Switch frameworks as the work changes** from discovery to validation to execution.
4. **Treat insights as complementary**, not competing.
5. **Stress-test assumptions across dimensions**: desirability, feasibility, viability, and behavior change.
6. **Use interviews, canvases, and roadmaps together** to reduce uncertainty.
7. **Use evidence from actual customer behavior** instead of relying on intuition alone.

The book also implies that teams should not treat frameworks as ideology. They are tools for reducing uncertainty.

## Common failure modes

- Using only one framework for every problem
- Mixing frameworks without understanding what each contributes
- Treating frameworks as checklists instead of learning systems
- Confusing customer empathy with market validation
- Optimizing the solution before confirming the problem
- Building an MVP before proving problem/solution fit
- Mistaking activity for traction
- Running interviews that ask for opinions instead of reconstructing behavior
- Skipping the customer-force analysis and jumping straight to solutions
