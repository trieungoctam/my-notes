# Build Trap

The **build trap** is the pattern of pouring more and more effort into building, polishing, and extending a product while failing to prove that the market actually wants it or that the economics can work. In [[Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-Introduction|Running Lean]], Ash Maurya treats this as one of the most common failure modes for early-stage products: teams keep assuming that one more feature, one more release, or one more round of development will finally create traction, but the real issue is that they have not validated the underlying problem, offer, or business model.

In the book's introduction, this trap is sharpened by the contrast between **Steve** and **Larry**. Steve follows a build-first path, spending months alone in his metaphorical garage polishing an AR/VR product, only to discover weak traction and investor rejection. Larry instead uses a [[traction-first]] approach, starting with problems before solutions, sketching a [[lean canvas]], and testing the riskiest assumptions in stages. The lesson is not merely "don't build too much," but "don't confuse product progress with market progress."

Maurya's later chapters make the trap even broader. In [[Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-I-Design|Part I: Design]], the early job is not to perfect the solution, but to **deconstruct the idea into assumptions** using a [[lean canvas]], then stress test the idea for **desirability**, **viability**, and **feasibility** before committing heavily to building. The build trap happens when teams skip those checks and treat construction as if it were proof.

Chapter 2 adds a crucial nuance: even a "smaller" MVP can still be trapped if it is based on a bad desirability story. If you begin with a solution and then force-fit a problem around it, you can build something that is technically real but still fundamentally unwanted. Maurya's warning is that a smaller product is not automatically a better product if it still fails to create a switch from the customer's current alternative.

Chapter 3 broadens the trap beyond desirability. A team can have some traction and still get stuck if the business model cannot reach its [[minimum success criteria]]. Maurya shows that viability has to be tested with a bottom-up view of the [[customer factory blueprint|customer factory]], using metrics like pricing, churn, acquisition, and referral. In other words, building more does not rescue a model whose economics are broken.

Chapter 4 extends the same warning into feasibility. A team can know what customers want and even have a plausible revenue model, yet still fall into the build trap by over-planning or overbuilding too early. Maurya replaces product-roadmap thinking with a [[traction roadmap]] and a stage-based rollout plan so teams can prioritize the right risks at the right time.

## What the build trap looks like

Common signs include:

- repeatedly adding features without clearer demand,
- interpreting low engagement as a reason to build more rather than learn more,
- relying on analytics alone instead of talking to customers,
- searching for a mythical "killer feature,"
- delaying validation until after the product is already built,
- confusing product activity with business [[traction]],
- treating the MVP as the start of learning instead of the result of prior validation,
- assuming that a functioning product is enough if the economics cannot scale,
- building a solution before confirming the [[problem/solution fit]],
- creating a roadmap for output instead of a roadmap for validated outcomes.

In the introduction, Steve falls into this pattern. He launches too early, sees weak retention, and then assumes the MVP is missing core functionality. Rather than revisiting the problem and talking to customers, he keeps iterating on the product. Maurya uses this to show how teams can get stuck in a loop of build-measure without enough learning.

Chapter 2 adds a further nuance: even teams that think they are doing customer discovery can fall into the build trap if they frame the problem through their own solution. Maurya calls this **Innovator's Bias**-the tendency to unconsciously invent or distort problems so they justify the product already in mind. In that case, building more only makes the wrong idea more expensive.

Chapter 3 broadens the trap beyond desirability. A team can validate that people want something and still fail if the business model cannot convert that demand into a large enough, sustainable outcome. If pricing, retention, acquisition, or referral assumptions do not support the target ARR or minimum success criteria, the team may be building toward an unviable future.

Chapter 4 adds a feasibility version of the trap: teams can be tempted to optimize for the end-state too early, as though they already know the full 18-24 month product path. Maurya argues that this is just another form of build trap, because it substitutes speculative planning for staged learning.

## Why it happens

The build trap is often driven by [[innovator's bias]]: the tendency to selectively notice evidence that supports the solution you already want to build. It is also reinforced by traditional product thinking, where progress is measured by shipping features instead of validating assumptions.

Maurya argues that in today's market this is especially dangerous because customers have many alternatives. If a product fails to resonate, users usually leave rather than patiently help the team improve it. That means building more is not the same as learning more.

Chapter 2 explains the deeper behavioral mechanism behind this: customers already have an [[existing alternatives|existing alternative]], and change only happens when a [[switching-triggers|switching trigger]] creates an expectation violation. A stronger product is not necessarily enough; a founder must understand the old way, the trigger, and the desired outcome. Without that, teams may build an impressive solution in search of a market.

The new world described in the book makes this even harsher: products are cheaper to build, competition is higher, and customer attention is scarce. In that environment, the build trap is not just inefficient-it can kill the business model.

Chapter 3 shows a related reason it happens: teams often chase top-down forecasts and big assumptions instead of stress-testing the actual mechanics of the business. If the model needs too many customers, too much acquisition, or unrealistic conversion rates, more building does not fix the math.

Chapter 4 reveals a final cause: founders often confuse roadmap certainty with roadmap readiness. A product roadmap assumes too much too early; a traction roadmap starts from the riskiest assumptions and works backward from validated milestones. The build trap is what happens when that distinction is ignored.

## How to avoid it

The book's alternative is to start with problems before solutions and to treat the business model as the product. That means:

1. identifying the riskiest assumptions,
2. talking to customers early,
3. validating the problem and offer before investing heavily in development,
4. using [[Demo-Sell-Build]] rather than [[Build-Demo-Sell]],
5. making many small bets instead of one large product bet,
6. using [[continuous innovation]] cycles to learn fast and course-correct,
7. framing desirability around the customer's existing alternatives and switching triggers,
8. separating true customer problems from solution-shaped guesses,
9. testing whether the full economics can reach a believable [[minimum success criteria]] before building to scale,
10. using a [[traction roadmap]] and now-next-later planning to time action to risk.

In the design phase, Maurya recommends a structured sequence: outline the business model, stress test desirability, stress test viability, stress test feasibility, and then communicate the idea clearly and concisely. This sequence is meant to reduce the odds of falling into the build trap by ensuring that building happens after the biggest unknowns have been addressed.

The Lean Canvas example in the book reinforces this point. Steve initially fills in his canvas with a broad market, an incomplete solution, and a weak grasp of customer value. Only by narrowing to early adopters, identifying existing alternatives, defining the problem clearly, and shaping a plausible offer does he begin to move from guesswork toward a testable business model. The canvas is a tool for learning what to build before building it.

Chapter 2 adds another safeguard: before building, ask not only "what problem could my solution solve?" but "what old solution is being broken, for whom, and why would they switch?" That question helps teams avoid building something that looks compelling inside the solution context but fails in the bigger customer context.

Chapter 3 adds the viability check: even if the idea is desirable, ask whether the customer factory can produce enough customers, revenue, and retention to hit the target outcome. If not, the team must change pricing, customer segment, problem size, or goal rather than just adding more product.

Chapter 4 adds the feasibility check: instead of a static product roadmap, use a stage-based traction roadmap and a now-next-later plan to sequence the riskiest assumptions. This keeps teams from overcommitting to output before they know which learning milestones matter most.

## The deeper lesson from Running Lean

The build trap is not only a warning against overbuilding. It is also a warning against treating the solution as if it were the startup's true product. Maurya's thesis is that the **business model** is what must work first. A product can be technically impressive and still fail if the model is wrong.

The book's introduction expands this lesson through the Steve/Larry contrast:

- Steve keeps believing that better product execution will solve his traction problem.
- Larry recognizes that investors fund working business models, not just solutions.
- Steve asks, "What should we build next?"
- Larry asks, "What should we learn next?"

Chapter 2 sharpens the point further by showing that even "disruptive" or "new category" ideas still have competition in the form of existing alternatives. That means the build trap often begins when founders ignore the old way of doing the job and start from a product idea instead of a customer switch story.

Chapter 3 completes the warning by showing that a product can escape the build trap at the feature level and still fall into it at the business-model level. If the numbers don't work, the team is still trapped-just with a more expensive product.

Chapter 4 completes the picture by showing that feasibility can be trapped too: teams may overbuild infrastructure, features, or internal plans before they know which immediate risks matter most. A traction roadmap helps keep the team aligned on staged learning rather than premature scale.

That difference matters because the startup environment is more competitive than ever. Customers have more choices, investors have more options, and the window for experimentation is shorter. In such conditions, a team can no longer rely on build-first faith.

That is why the book emphasizes:

- [[problem/solution fit]] before MVP construction,
- [[traction-first]] thinking over build-first pride,
- and evidence-based decisions over hope-based development.

## Sources & Perspectives

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-Introduction]] - Introduction, pp. 11-49
Maurya presents the build trap as the danger of continuing to build and optimize a product without proving customer demand. His view is that teams must validate the problem and business model first, or else they risk creating something nobody wants even if the product is technically impressive.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-I-Design]] - Part I. Design, pp. 50-58
Maurya reframes the trap as a design-stage failure: teams begin with an untested Plan A, treat the solution as the product, and skip structured stress testing of desirability, viability, and feasibility. The remedy is to model the business first and only build after the riskiest assumptions have been surfaced and tested.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-1-Deconstruct-Your-Idea-on-a-Lean-Canvas]] - Chapter 1, pp. 59-98
The Lean Canvas chapter adds the practical mechanism for escaping the build trap: deconstruct the idea into assumptions, focus on early adopters, define the problem and alternatives, and only then shape the solution. The canvas turns the anti-build-trap principle into a repeatable modeling workflow.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]] - Chapter 2, pp. 108-132
Maurya connects the build trap to desirability failure: teams often invent problems to justify a solution, ignore existing alternatives, and mistake product features for customer value. He argues that escaping the build trap requires identifying switching triggers, framing the problem around the old way, and causing a real customer switch.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]] - Chapter 3, pp. 133-172
Maurya extends the build trap beyond product desirability into business-model viability: teams can overbuild even when customers want the product if the economics do not support the target outcome. He introduces the customer factory, Fermi estimates, and minimum success criteria as ways to test whether the model can actually scale.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]] - Chapter 4, pp. 179-209
Maurya extends the build trap into feasibility and rollout planning: product roadmaps can seduce teams into premature optimization, while traction roadmaps and now-next-later plans keep the team focused on the right risks at the right time. The remedy is staged learning, not early overcommitment to output.

> [!note]
> No conflict appears between the sources; Chapter 4 expands the concept by showing that the build trap can also appear as premature optimization and roadmap overreach, not just feature bloat or weak validation.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> The build trap is when you keep making the product better instead of checking whether people want it, will pay for it, and can keep using it.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like remodeling a store over and over while never checking whether customers actually come in, buy anything, or help the business grow.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: The build trap only means too many features.
> Reality: It also means building the wrong thing or a business model that cannot scale.
>
> Myth: A small MVP automatically avoids the trap.
> Reality: A small product can still be wrong if it is based on bad assumptions.
>
> Myth: Analytics alone are enough.
> Reality: Metrics show what is happening, but conversations and model tests explain why.
>
> Myth: If the idea is technically feasible, it is worth building.
> Reality: It also has to be desirable, viable, and timed correctly.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** What is the build trap?
> **A:** It is the tendency to keep building without proving demand, desirability, viability, or a staged path to feasibility.
>
> **Q:** Why is it risky?
> **A:** Because teams can waste time and money on a product that customers do not want or that cannot scale economically.
>
> **Q:** How does Chapter 2 expand the concept?
> **A:** It shows that solution-first thinking can create fake problems and lead teams to build the wrong thing.
>
> **Q:** What does Chapter 3 add?
> **A:** It shows that even a wanted product can still be trapped if the economics cannot reach the minimum success criteria.
>
> **Q:** What does Chapter 4 add?
> **A:** It shows that overplanning and premature optimization can also be build-trap behavior, especially when teams confuse a product roadmap with a traction roadmap.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> The build trap is one of the fastest ways to waste startup effort. Understanding it helps teams shift from feature-chasing to learning, from product activity to market traction, and from hopeful development to evidence-based decisions. It is especially important in crowded markets where customers can switch quickly and weak models fail fast.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> The build trap is when a team keeps making a product bigger, shinier, or more complete without first checking if people actually want it, will pay for it, and can keep using it. It's like spending a lot of time improving something before you know whether it solves a real problem or can become a real business.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like opening a restaurant and constantly changing the menu, decorating the dining room, and buying better equipment-without ever asking whether customers like the food, whether they come back, or whether the prices cover the costs.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: The build trap only means adding too many features. **Reality:** It also includes building the wrong solution, solving a fake problem, or creating a business model that cannot work.
> Myth: A smaller MVP automatically avoids the build trap. **Reality:** A small product can still be a trap if it is based on weak assumptions or does not create a real customer switch.
> Myth: If the product is technically feasible, it is worth building. **Reality:** It also has to be desirable to customers, viable as a business, and timed through staged learning.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** Why can a team still be trapped even after shipping a working MVP? **A:** Because a working product does not prove that customers want it, that they will switch from alternatives, or that the economics can scale.
> **Q:** How does the concept of existing alternatives change the way you think about desirability? **A:** It reminds you that customers are not choosing in a vacuum; they already have a current solution, so your product must create a strong enough reason to switch.
> **Q:** What does the viability check add beyond customer interest? **A:** It tests whether pricing, retention, acquisition, and referral can produce enough revenue and growth to meet minimum success criteria.
> **Q:** How is a traction roadmap different from a product roadmap in avoiding the build trap? **A:** A traction roadmap focuses on the riskiest assumptions and learning milestones first, while a product roadmap can encourage premature output and overbuilding.
> **Q:** Why is customer conversation important if analytics are available? **A:** Analytics show what is happening, but conversations help explain why it is happening and whether the underlying problem is real.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> The build trap wastes time, money, and attention on products that may never become real businesses. Understanding it helps teams focus on learning, validate demand earlier, and build only after the biggest risks have been reduced.
