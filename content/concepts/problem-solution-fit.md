# Problem/Solution Fit

Problem/solution fit is the first evidence-based checkpoint that a real customer problem is painful, important, and reachable enough to justify continued investment. It is not about having a finished product; it is about proving that a specific customer segment has a real problem worth solving, that the proposed offer can cause a meaningful switch from an existing alternative, and that the team has enough evidence to keep going. In [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], it is the first major validation milestone before committing heavily to an MVP.

In *Running Lean, 3rd Edition*, Ash Maurya makes this stage much more disciplined than a simple "build something and see." The sequence starts with [[concepts/lean-canvas]] modeling, then moves into customer interviews, offer testing, and only later building. The key shift is from solution-first thinking to problem-first learning.

The third edition deepens this by tying problem/solution fit to **switching**. Desirability is not just whether customers like an idea; it is whether the idea causes a meaningful switch from an existing alternative. That means the problem has to be framed in terms of the old way, the switching trigger, and the customer's desired outcome in the bigger context. Chapter 3 then extends the discipline into viability: once you know the problem is worth solving, you still have to prove that the business can monetize it. Chapter 4 then turns that validated direction into a staged rollout plan through a [[concepts/traction-roadmap]]. Chapter 5 adds one more layer: once the problem is clear, it becomes much easier to communicate the idea concisely to advisors, customers, and investors.

Chapters 6, 7, and 11 add an operating cadence: problem/solution fit is not just a one-off judgment, but a checkpoint inside a 90-day cycle. Teams identify the current constraint in the customer factory, define a cycle goal, run small experiments, and then review the evidence at the end of the cycle to decide whether to persevere, pivot, or pause. Chapter 14 shows why this checkpoint matters beyond validation: it marks the point where the business begins to look like a repeatable growth system, with retention and growth loops becoming the next focus.

> [!note]
> The third edition strengthens the sequence: validate the problem and the offer before building the MVP, then use viability modeling, rollout planning, and 90-day cycle reviews to see whether the business can scale enough to be worth pursuing. By the time the team reaches [[concepts/growth-loop]] thinking, problem/solution fit should already have produced evidence that customers will keep using and spreading the product.

## Definition

Problem/solution fit means:

- you understand the customer's problem clearly,
- you can describe a compelling offer to solve it,
- customers show enough interest or commitment that the idea deserves more investment,
- and the business model still has a credible path to traction.

It is not the same as having a finished product. In *Running Lean*, Ash Maurya argues that you should validate the problem and the offer before you build the solution in earnest. The goal is to reduce the risk of creating something nobody wants.

The third edition emphasizes a sequence:

1. deconstruct the idea into a business model using a [[concepts/lean-canvas]],
2. identify the riskiest assumptions,
3. test desirability through customer conversations and offers,
4. then build only after there is evidence the market wants what you are proposing,
5. and finally check viability and rollout feasibility so the model can work in the real world.

Chapter 3 adds a further check: once the problem is validated, the model must still pass a viability test. In other words, a painful problem is necessary, but not sufficient.

## Core Idea

The book reframes startup risk as primarily a customer and market problem, not just a technical one. The big question is usually not "Can we build it?" but "Should we build it?"

That is why the order matters:

1. [[concepts/lean-canvas]] to model the idea,
2. customer interviews to understand the problem,
3. an offer to test demand,
4. then an MVP only after there is evidence of fit,
5. and finally viability checks to see whether the model can reach a meaningful outcome.

This directly addresses the [[concepts/build-trap]]: if you start with a solution and only later ask whether the problem matters, you may waste months building the wrong thing.

The third edition sharpens the practical workflow by contrasting two approaches:

- **Build-Demo-Sell**: build first, then try to find demand;
- **Demo-Sell-Build**: validate demand first, then build what the market already wants.

Chapter 6 adds that this fit is best tested inside a 90-day cycle, where the team focuses on the current bottleneck in the customer factory. Chapter 7 then shows how the cycle kickoff turns this into an actionable team process: start with problem discovery, move to solution design, then test the offer. Chapter 8 makes the pre-MVP work explicit by showing how to interview customers about their timeline, triggers, alternatives, friction, and outcomes before any solution work. Chapter 9 then defines the MVP more concretely as the smallest solution that causes a switch, not merely the smallest feature set.

The bonus material reinforces why this stage deserves discipline: time is the scarcest resource, sweat equity is expensive, and bootstrapping should be about efficiency and speed of learning rather than doing everything yourself. That makes problem/solution fit a time-sensitive learning problem, not a cheap-build problem.

Chapter 14 closes the loop by showing that once problem/solution fit is solid, the business can start looking for a sustainable growth loop. In that sense, fit is not the end of validation; it is the point at which the company is ready to seek repeatability, retention, and scalable acquisition.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

In the source document, Mary explains that her team did not begin by building an MVP. Instead, they:

- sketched several variants of the idea on a Lean Canvas,
- ran around two dozen customer interviews,
- narrowed in on promising customer-problem-solution combinations,
- built a demo rather than a full product,
- crafted an offer,
- and only built the MVP after customers were willing to buy.

That sequence is the essence of problem/solution fit: the customer response comes before the product is fully built.

The Steve/Mary dialogue is especially important because it shows a common mistake. Steve launches a product, sees signups, and assumes the MVP is the issue. Mary pushes him back to the earlier stage and asks who the users are, where they came from, and whether he has actually talked to them. The lesson is that problem/solution fit depends on customer understanding, not just traffic or analytics.

Chapter 2 adds a second layer to this: the problem must be framed against the customer's **existing alternatives**, not against the founder's imagined solution. Customers do not wake up wanting your category; they want relief from an old way that is breaking down or no longer good enough. This is the heart of the [[concepts/innovator-s-gift]] and [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] view of desirability.

Chapter 3 then shows why this matters for viability too: once you have evidence of fit, the business model still needs enough pricing, retention, and customer acquisition leverage to reach a meaningful revenue target. Chapter 4 shows how those validated assumptions feed into a stage-based rollout plan.

Chapter 5 contributes the communication dimension: once you understand the problem well enough, you can frame an elevator pitch around the customer's triggering event, their job-to-be-done, the switching trigger, and the desired outcome. In other words, problem/solution fit is not only a validation milestone; it also becomes the basis for clear storytelling.

Chapter 6 adds the operating-system dimension. Instead of treating fit as a vague gut feeling, Maurya places it inside a 90-day cadence. The team identifies the current constraint, defines a cycle objective, and runs experiments sized to the cycle. Chapter 7 then shows how the cycle kickoff itself turns this into an actionable team process: define the problem, design the solution, test the offer, and only then move toward MVP building. Chapter 8 goes deeper still by showing that the real work is interviewing customers about how they use existing alternatives, not asking them to guess at abstract problems. Chapter 9 then shows how the team turns that learning into a minimal solution package that can actually cause a switch.

The bonus material adds a practical note: because time is more valuable than money at the earliest stage, teams should use the right learning accelerants, avoid waste, and value their sweat equity honestly. That makes problem/solution fit not just an intellectual checkpoint, but an efficiency checkpoint as well.

Chapter 14 adds a longer-horizon lens: once customers are repeatedly activating and retaining, problem/solution fit becomes the bridge to a sustainable [[concepts/growth-loop]]. The business begins to shift from proving demand to building a repeatable growth engine.

## Why It Matters

Problem/solution fit is the first proof that your startup is aimed at a real opportunity. It helps you avoid confusing enthusiasm for a solution with evidence of demand.

The book also emphasizes that customers do not want features in the abstract; they want their own problems solved. If you cannot show that the problem is worth solving, no amount of extra engineering, design polish, or investor optimism will save the business model.

This is why Maurya treats problem/solution fit as a business-model milestone, not merely a product-design milestone. The real question is not whether you can build, but whether you should build.

Chapter 2 further shows that desirability is about more than a vague "want." Customers switch when a new solution creates enough pull to overcome inertia, friction, and their current habit. Chapter 3 adds that once you have a desirable solution, it still has to be economically viable. Chapter 4 then turns that validated model into a phased traction roadmap. Chapter 5 shows that the same validated problem framing becomes the backbone of a clearer pitch to advisors, customers, and investors. Chapter 6 operationalizes the whole thing through 90-day cycles, forcing the team to focus on the current bottleneck rather than chasing too many goals at once. Chapter 8 finally shows how to get there in practice: interview customers about the trigger, the alternatives, the journey, and the gaps between desired and actual outcomes. Chapter 9 then packages the smallest viable solution so it actually causes the switch.

The bonus material sharpens the economic and time discipline behind all of this. It argues that startup time should be valued at a high effective hourly rate because sweat equity is costly and delay is expensive. That means problem/solution fit should be pursued with urgency, not as a side project.

Chapter 11 closes the loop by turning problem/solution fit into a reviewable milestone: at the end of a 90-day cycle, the team updates the Lean Canvas, overlays actual traction on the roadmap, and decides whether to persevere, pivot, or pause based on what the evidence says.

Chapter 14 extends the significance further: once fit is established, the startup can begin searching for a sustainable growth rocket. That makes problem/solution fit not just a validation gate, but the foundation for repeatable growth.

> [!info]
> Problem/solution fit is the point where customer pain, switching logic, early traction, economic plausibility, and team learning line up enough to justify building further.

## Relationship to MVP

A key contribution of the book is its distinction between **defining** an MVP and **building** an MVP. Maurya argues that many teams rush straight to building a minimum viable product, but the smarter move is to first validate whether the problem and offer resonate.

In other words, problem/solution fit is the stage that comes before true MVP development. The MVP should be the smallest solution that delivers monetizable value to an audience that has already shown evidence of interest.

This helps explain the difference between:

- **Build-Demo-Sell**: build first, then try to find customers;
- **Demo-Sell-Build**: validate the offer first, then build what the market already wants.

Chapter 2 adds a subtle but important refinement: you do not merely validate "an idea." You validate whether a particular customer segment, at a particular moment, is triggered to switch from an existing alternative to a better way of getting the job done. That is why problem/solution fit and MVP design are both downstream of [[concepts/customer-discovery]].

Chapter 3 adds the next layer: the MVP should also fit a plausible path to revenue, retention, and scale. A useful product that cannot support a viable business model is still a dead end.

Chapter 6 adds an execution-level nuance: fit is best tested as part of a 90-day cycle that prioritizes the current constraint in the customer factory. Chapter 7 then shows how the cycle kickoff turns this into an actionable team process: define the problem, design the solution, test the offer, and only then move toward MVP building. Chapter 8 makes the pre-MVP work explicit by showing how to use problem discovery interviews to uncover the deeper, jobs-based problem before any solution work. Chapter 9 then defines the MVP as the smallest solution that causes a switch, not just the smallest feature set.

The bonus material adds that efficient teams should spend money where it accelerates learning, not where it merely reduces discomfort. In practice, that means avoiding unnecessary build work and keeping the scope tight until the problem/solution fit evidence is strong.

Chapter 14 adds a growth-level nuance: MVP launch is not the finish line. If the product creates repeatable retention and referral, the business can begin to search for its primary growth rocket.

## Signals of Problem/Solution Fit

The book suggests that fit is visible when:

- customers recognize the problem immediately,
- they care enough to keep engaging,
- they respond positively to the offer,
- and some are willing to commit before the product is fully built.

Strong problem/solution fit often shows up as early traction: interest, willingness to commit, and repeat engagement.

The third edition adds an important nuance: the offer itself is a validation artifact. It is the moment where the idea meets reality. A strong offer can reveal demand even before the MVP exists.

Chapter 2 also implies a stronger signal: the customer should be able to clearly name the old way, describe why it is no longer good enough, and see the promise of the new way as materially better. When that happens, the switch becomes credible rather than hypothetical.

Chapter 3 adds a viability signal: if those same customers cannot support a believable path to revenue, retention, and acquisition, then the fit is incomplete.

Chapter 5 adds a communication signal: if advisors and stakeholders can understand the story quickly and ask useful questions instead of getting lost in the solution, that is another sign the problem has been framed well.

Chapter 6 adds an operational signal: the team should be able to formulate a 90-day objective, identify the current bottleneck, and design experiments that meaningfully move the customer factory forward.

Chapter 7 adds a tactical signal: when the first 90-day cycle is working, the team can move from problem discovery into solution design and offer delivery with enough clarity that a [[concepts/mafia-offer]] becomes possible.

Chapter 8 adds the deepest signal: you are learning the real customer story so well that you can predict their timeline, their alternatives, their friction, and their next step before they say it.

Chapter 9 adds the solution-design signal: the smallest packaged version of the product is already enough to cause a switch and can be delivered quickly through a concierge, Wizard-of-Oz, or foot-in-the-door approach.

Chapter 14 adds the growth signal: customers keep using the product, other users join through their activity, and the business starts showing the early shape of a sustainable growth loop.

## Common Failure Modes

A team may think it has problem/solution fit simply because users sign up or say the product sounds interesting. But if those users are not the right customers, or if they never convert, the team has probably not achieved fit.

Maurya stresses that metrics alone cannot explain why something is failing. Without ongoing customer conversations, teams may misread surface activity as validation and end up chasing a mythical killer feature instead of fixing the underlying problem fit.

Another failure mode is treating the MVP as the first test, when in fact the core question should already have been answered earlier. If you have not validated the problem and the offer, the MVP becomes just another guess.

Chapter 2 adds a related trap: founders can become trapped in their own solution context. They may believe their category is the problem, when the real problem lives in the bigger context of what customers are trying to accomplish. In that case, the team is not just missing fit; it is solving the wrong job.

Chapter 3 adds a different failure mode: even with strong desirability, the numbers may not work. You can have a pain point worth solving and still fail viability if pricing, retention, or acquisition economics are too weak.

Chapter 5 introduces a communication failure mode: if you cannot explain the problem, the switching trigger, and the offer clearly, stakeholders will not see the opportunity even if your logic is sound.

Chapter 6 adds a systems failure mode: teams often spread themselves across too many priorities. If you are not focused on the current constraint, you may be doing useful work that does not actually increase throughput.

Chapter 7 adds a process failure mode: trying to jump straight to a landing page, survey, or direct pitch before problem discovery can create false confidence and weak learning. The point of the first cycle is to understand the problem deeply enough that the offer is grounded in evidence.

Chapter 8 adds a research failure mode: asking customers directly what their problems are often produces shallow answers, biased answers, or solution suggestions. The better method is to interview them about their timeline, triggers, current workaround, and what changed.

Chapter 9 adds a packaging failure mode: building too much too soon makes the MVP slower and less testable, which delays learning and weakens the switch.

The bonus material adds a time-allocation failure mode: founders often undervalue their own time and spend it on low-ROI tasks. That slows problem/solution fit and makes the validation process far more expensive than it needs to be.

Chapter 14 adds a scaling failure mode: mistaking early retention for a growth loop. A product can retain users without yet having a sustainable growth rocket.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> - Friendly interest means fit. Reality: real demand needs evidence, not compliments.
> - Build the MVP first. Reality: validate the problem and offer before heavy building.
> - More features improve fit. Reality: fit comes from solving the right problem, not adding more stuff.
> - Metrics alone prove fit. Reality: customer conversations explain why customers behave a certain way.
> - Validation is one-and-done. Reality: fit sits inside a 90-day cycle of ongoing learning.
> - Surveys can replace interviews. Reality: interviews reveal the deeper why that surveys miss.
> - A strong problem automatically means a strong business. Reality: viability still has to work.
> - The MVP should be fully automated. Reality: concierge and Wizard-of-Oz patterns can be the right first step.
> - Retention alone equals growth. Reality: you still need a sustainable growth loop.
> - Bootstrapping means being cheap. Reality: bootstrapping is about efficiency and speed of learning.
