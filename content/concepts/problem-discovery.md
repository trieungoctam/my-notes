# Problem Discovery

Problem discovery is the practice of learning how real people currently get a job done before committing to a solution. In *Running Lean*, Ash Maurya treats it as the starting point for [[concepts/problem-solution-fit]] and a core activity in [[concepts/continuous-innovation]]. The goal is to uncover real customer problems, switching triggers, existing alternatives, desired outcomes, and the forces that shape change through direct conversations rather than guesswork.

It is not about pitching, surveying, or asking people to validate a favorite idea. It is about understanding the customer's current world well enough to identify problems worth solving and design an offer that causes a switch.

## Core Idea

Maurya's logic is straightforward:

- Customers do not buy solutions; they buy a promise of a better outcome.
- Real opportunities usually come from old solutions that no longer work well enough.
- Strong ideas begin with a deep understanding of customer behavior, not with a solution in search of a problem.

That is why problem discovery starts with the customer's current behavior and uses interviews to uncover:

- the customer segment
- the switching trigger
- the existing alternative
- the customer's job-to-be-done
- the desired outcome
- the points of friction, workarounds, and unmet needs
- the level of urgency or monetizable pain

Problem discovery helps replace founder assumptions with evidence. It also protects against [[concepts/innovator-bias]], where founders unconsciously twist customer feedback to justify the solution they already want to build.

> [!note]- Problem discovery expands beyond pre-launch pain
> In the later chapters and appendix, Maurya extends the same learning mindset beyond initial customer discovery. After launch, teams still need to uncover friction, habits, value creation, and growth behavior to make customers happy and to find sustainable growth loops. Problem discovery is therefore not a one-time activity; it is a recurring way of seeing how value is created and where the next constraint lives.

## What Problem Discovery Is For

Problem discovery interviews are meant to answer questions like:

- Who is trying to get a job done?
- What are they using today?
- What caused them to start looking for something else?
- What did they try before?
- What was frustrating, slow, expensive, or unreliable about the old way?
- What outcome were they really after?
- Is the pain strong enough to justify change?
- Would they be willing to switch or pay?

In Maurya's framework, problem discovery is the first step in building a [[concepts/mafia-offer]] and a prerequisite for credible desirability testing. It also helps fill the Customer Segments, Problem, Early Adopters, and Existing Alternatives boxes in the [[concepts/lean-canvas]].

## How It Fits the Framework

Maurya places problem discovery at the start of the startup journey:

1. Discover the problem
2. Define a solution
3. Test an offer
4. Build the MVP only after enough evidence exists

That sequence is intentional. You do not start by building a product and then ask people if they like it. You start by learning what people are already doing, what breaks in that current workflow, and what would make them switch.

Problem discovery is also part of the book's broader emphasis on [[concepts/traction]]-first validation and [[concepts/now-next-later]] execution. In the 90-day cycle, it is the earliest learning activity used to identify the limiting customer/problem constraint.

> [!note]- Problem discovery vs. solution testing
> Maurya's later chapters sharpen the distinction between discovering a problem and testing a solution. Discovery comes first because otherwise you risk validating a solution-shaped question instead of the real customer pain.

## Key Principles

### 1. Start with current behavior

The strongest customer insights come from studying what people do now, not what they say they want in the abstract. Existing alternatives are crucial because they reveal the real competition. Often the true competitor is not another startup but email, spreadsheets, a manual process, a habit, or the status quo.

### 2. Look for switching triggers

A good early adopter is not just anyone with a need. It is someone who has recently experienced a trigger that made the current solution feel insufficient. Examples include:

- a bad experience
- a change in circumstance
- an awareness event

This aligns with the book's [[concepts/customer-forces-model]]: push, pull, inertia, and friction shape whether a customer changes behavior.

### 3. Go beyond the first answer

The first complaint is rarely the whole story. The goal is to keep asking "why?" until you uncover the deeper context, the actual job-to-be-done, and the consequences of doing nothing.

### 4. Interview, don't interrogate

Maurya argues that surveys and focus groups are weak at discovery because they assume you already know the right questions and the right answer choices. Interviews are better because they let you explore unexpected details, body language, and the customer's timeline of events.

### 5. Find the bigger context

A major refinement in the book is that problem discovery should not stop at product-level problems. You must uncover the bigger context: the outcome the customer really wants.

For example:

- not "a better drill bit," but "a better homeowner"
- not "a better camera," but "a better photographer"
- not "a better business model canvas," but "a better entrepreneur"

This is where [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] becomes especially important. The product is only a tool; the real value lives in the outcome.

## The Customer Forces Model

The book frames customer change as a tension between four forces:

- **Push** - dissatisfaction with the current situation
- **Pull** - attraction of the new solution
- **Inertia** - comfort with the old way
- **Friction** - anxiety or difficulty in adopting the new way

Problem discovery interviews are designed to expose these forces. The resulting notes can be organized into a [[concepts/customer-forces-canvas]], which helps turn raw interviews into a structured story.

## Broad-Match and Narrow-Match Discovery

A notable addition in the later chapters is the idea that discovery should happen in two passes:

- **Broad-match discovery**: talk to people who recently used or bought an existing alternative
- **Narrow-match discovery**: after patterns emerge, interview your likely early adopters

This helps avoid the local maximum trap, where you define your market too narrowly too early and miss a better opportunity.

## How to Run Good Discovery Interviews

A strong customer discovery interview is:

- one-on-one, not a group session
- open-ended, not multiple-choice
- focused on past behavior, not hypotheticals
- centered on the customer's timeline of events
- exploratory, not persuasive
- built around learning, not pitching

Maurya recommends interviewing in a friendly, neutral setting, asking for enough time to go deep, and using pairs when possible so one person can ask questions while the other takes notes.

### A useful flow

1. Set the stage and make it clear this is not a pitch
2. Anchor on the chosen existing alternative or the last time the job was done
3. Reconstruct the timeline backward to the trigger
4. Explore the selection process
5. Explore early use and recurring use
6. Ask what happened next and whether the job was done well enough
7. Wrap up by asking for a follow-up and referrals

## What to Avoid

The book is especially critical of these failure modes:

- **Surveys too early** - they constrain discovery and hide unknowns
- **Focus groups** - they invite groupthink and vocal-minority bias
- **Pitching too soon** - it changes the conversation from learning to persuasion
- **Asking directly about problems** - people often answer with solutions, not real pain
- **Using your own experience as the whole market** - scratching your own itch is not enough
- **Treating partial confirmation as validation** - a positive response does not necessarily mean a strong problem exists

## Practical Outcome

The result of problem discovery is not just empathy. It is a concrete problem thesis: a clear statement of who has the problem, what they do today, why the current alternative is failing, what outcome they actually want, and what would make them switch.

That thesis becomes the basis for solution design, offer testing, and eventually building something customers actually want.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> Talk to real people about how they do a job today, what is annoying or broken, and what would make them switch before you build anything.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It is like detective work: follow the timeline, find the trigger, study the old way, and only then decide on the best fix.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: Problem discovery is just a survey. Reality: It is a conversation that uncovers unknowns.
> Myth: You should pitch your idea first. Reality: You should learn how people currently behave before proposing a solution.
> Myth: Any stated problem is big enough. Reality: The problem must be painful and urgent enough to motivate switching or paying.
> Myth: Discovery ends at launch. Reality: The same mindset keeps helping after launch by revealing friction, habits, and growth behaviors.
> Myth: Friends are enough. Reality: Friends help you practice, but you still need relevant real-world interviews with target customers.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** Why does problem discovery start with current behavior?
> **A:** Because current behavior reveals the real competition, the workaround, and the context where a solution has to fit.
>
> **Q:** What is a switching trigger?
> **A:** A triggering event that makes the current solution feel insufficient and pushes someone to consider a new way.
>
> **Q:** Why are interviews better than surveys for discovery?
> **A:** Interviews let you ask follow-up questions, notice body language, and uncover the deeper why behind decisions.
>
> **Q:** What does problem discovery produce?
> **A:** A problem thesis: who has the problem, what they do now, why the current alternative fails, what outcome they want, and what would make them switch.
>
> **Q:** How does the later part of the book extend this idea?
> **A:** It shows that after launch you still need to uncover friction, habits, and growth-loop behavior to keep customers making progress and to grow sustainably.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> Problem discovery keeps teams from building the wrong thing. It reveals real pain, real switching behavior, and the bigger job-to-be-done, which improves solution design, offers, pricing, and validation. After launch, it also helps teams reduce friction, build habits, and identify the behaviors that can power retention and growth loops.
