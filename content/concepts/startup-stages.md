# Startup Stages

Startup stages are the phases a venture moves through as it turns an initial idea into a validated business model, then into repeatable delivery, and finally into scalable growth. In *Running Lean*, Ash Maurya treats stages as a **risk-management sequence**: first design the business model, then validate the model, then design a solution that causes a switch, then launch in controlled batches, then make customers successful, and finally optimize for repeatable growth. The key insight is that the stage you are in determines what risk matters most, what to test, and what kind of plan, metrics, or pitch makes sense.

## Core idea

The startup journey is not one continuous "build more" process. It is a sequence of stages with different dominant risks:

- **Design stage**: clarify the idea and business model.
- **Validation stage**: test whether customers want it, whether the economics work, and whether the plan is practical.
- **Solution design stage**: narrow the validated model into the smallest solution that can cause a switch.
- **Launch stage**: get the MVP into the hands of early adopters in batches.
- **Post-launch / happy customer stage**: make the product the new status quo through activation, retention, and behavior design.
- **Scale stage**: pursue repeatable growth after product/market fit.

Maurya's key claim is that founders fail when they act as if they are already in the scaling stage while still in discovery or validation. The right actions at the wrong time waste time, money, and attention.

> [!note]- Stage language varies across the book
> The book uses several labels that map to the same overall progression: **design → validation → growth**, and within that, more detailed steps like **problem/solution fit → product/market fit → scale**. Later chapters also insert a distinct **launch** phase between solution design and scale, and then a **happy customer** optimization phase after launch. The underlying idea is consistent even when the labels shift.

> [!note]- 90-day cycles make stages operational
> The later chapters sharpen this idea by showing that stage transitions are managed through **90-day cycles**. Each cycle is used to model the current business, prioritize the riskiest assumptions, and test them with small experiments. In other words, the stage model is not just descriptive; it becomes a working cadence for execution. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]], pp.238-269]

## Stage 1: Design

In the design stage, the founder's job is to turn an initial idea into a visible business model. This is where the [[concepts/lean-canvas]] matters most.

### What happens here
- Deconstruct the idea into assumptions.
- Identify customers, problems, and alternatives.
- Draft a first UVP.
- Sketch revenue, cost, channels, and metrics.
- Split one vague idea into multiple variants if needed.

### Main risk
The main risk here is usually **solution bias**: assuming the product idea is the answer before proving the problem.

### Success output
A clear model of the opportunity, not a finished product.

Maurya emphasizes that this stage should be **fast and focused**. A Lean Canvas is meant to be sketched quickly, not perfected. It is a snapshot of the current hypothesis set, not the final truth.

## Stage 2: Validation

The validation stage asks whether the model is actually worth pursuing. Maurya divides this into three stress tests:

1. **Desirability** - do customers want this?
2. **Viability** - can the business make money?
3. **Feasibility** - can it be built and delivered quickly enough?

This is where the startup begins to replace guesses with evidence.

### Desirability
Desirability is about whether the product causes a real switch away from existing alternatives. It depends on [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]], switching triggers, and a compelling UVP.

The book's newer treatment makes this especially explicit: founders should look for **old solutions breaking under a switching trigger** rather than inventing problems around a solution they already love. That means startup stages begin with understanding the customer's current reality, not with building.

### Viability
Viability is where the startup must prove that the economics can work. Maurya uses a Fermi estimate, customer lifetime, churn, pricing, and required throughput to test whether the business can reach a meaningful goal.

This stage is about whether the model can become a real business, not whether it could someday become large in theory.

### Feasibility
Feasibility is about how to get there. Instead of a traditional product roadmap, Maurya recommends a [[concepts/traction-roadmap]] and a staged rollout plan.

This matters because a business can be desirable and viable on paper but still be impractical to execute in the near term.

> [!note]- Feasibility includes stage-appropriate rollout, not just buildability
> The latest material sharpens feasibility by showing that the question is not only "can we build it?" but "what is the smallest, most realistic way to pull it off right now?" That is a tighter interpretation of feasibility, but it is consistent with the broader stage model. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-339]

## Stage 3: Solution design

Once customer/problem fit is established, the startup moves into solution design. This stage narrows the model into an MVP that can actually cause a switch.

### What changes here
- The goal shifts from discovering the problem to designing the smallest solution that creates a compelling promise.
- The team starts translating validated problem insights into a launchable offer.
- The MVP is scoped against both desirability and feasibility, not just technical ambition.

### Main risk
The main risk is **scope creep**: building too much, too soon, or adding features that do not materially improve the switching case.

### Success output
A small, credible solution that can be delivered quickly and tested with early adopters.

The book stresses that this stage should not become a miniature product-planning exercise. The question is not "what could the product eventually do?" but "what is the smallest thing that can cause the switch now?"

## Stage 4: Offer delivery / launch

In the launch stage, the startup assembles a mafia offer, delivers it to qualified early adopters, and tests whether people are willing to commit before the full product is built.

### What happens here
- Build the offer, demo, and call-to-action.
- Use the customer story pitch to frame the switch.
- Roll out in batches to a small set of early adopters.
- Prepare the MVP for early access delivery.

### Main risk
The main risk is **misalignment between the promise and the customer's willingness to switch**. A good offer must be specific, credible, and anchored in the customer's current alternatives.

### Success output
Tangible commitments, repeatable early sales, and a launch path that matches the team's build capacity.

Maurya is clear that this is still not scale. It is a controlled transition from validated demand to actual delivery.

## Stage 5: Post-launch / happy customer optimization

After launch, the stage focus changes again. The goal is no longer just acquisition or even initial sales, but making the product the new status quo by helping customers achieve results repeatedly.

### What changes here
- Activation becomes the first critical success point.
- Retention and repeated value delivery matter more.
- Behavior design becomes central.
- The team works to reduce friction and prevent switching away.
- Features are validated through the same discovery-to-offer logic, not simply added because customers ask.

### Main risk
The main risk becomes **feature bloat and premature optimization**. Teams can easily drift away from the original promise and start building for imagined scale instead of real customer progress.

### Success output
Happy customers who repeatedly get value, return to the product, and spread referral value.

> [!note]- Launch is not the end of the stage model
> A subtle but important addition in the later chapters is that launch is followed by a distinct phase of **making happy customers**. This phase is not just "post-launch support"; it is where activation, retention, and behavior design turn early users into a repeatable customer base. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-13-Make-Happy-Customers]], pp.440-466]

## Stage 6: Growth / scale

Once the model has some repeatability, the focus shifts to growth. The aim is no longer just to prove that people want the product, but to make the business model more efficient, repeatable, and scalable.

### What changes at scale
- The team stops only searching for fit and starts optimizing a working engine.
- Metrics become more operational and growth-focused.
- The business transitions from learning mode to performance mode.
- The team begins to test growth channels and scaling constraints.

### Main risk
The main risk becomes scaling the wrong thing or scaling too early.

## Why stages matter

Maurya's startup stages are useful because they create a **right action, right time** discipline. For example:

- In early design, you should not obsess over full implementation.
- In validation, you should not hide behind a polished roadmap or financial fantasy.
- In solution design, you should not add features that do not change the switching decision.
- In launch, you should not launch broadly before you can make a few early adopters successful.
- In the happy customer stage, you should not keep adding features instead of improving activation and retention.
- In growth, you should not keep behaving like a tiny discovery team forever.

The stage model helps founders answer: *What is the next most important risk to remove?*

## Startup stages in *Running Lean*

The book's broader framework maps naturally to six operational phases:

- **Design** → build the business model and clarify the opportunity.
- **Validation** → test desirability, viability, and feasibility.
- **Solution design** → design the MVP to cause a switch.
- **Launch** → roll out the MVP in batches and make early customers successful.
- **Happy customer optimization** → reinforce activation, retention, and repeated value.
- **Growth** → scale after the model has proven repeatable.

This progression is reinforced by the book's use of:
- [[concepts/lean-canvas]] for modeling,
- [[concepts/customer-factory]] for traction,
- [[concepts/innovation-trinity]] for balance,
- [[concepts/now-next-later]] for stage-based execution,
- [[concepts/wizard-of-oz-mvp]] for early feasibility shortcuts,
- [[concepts/90-day-cycles]] for recurring review and accountability,
- [[concepts/customer-forces]] for understanding switching and habit formation,
- and staged launch practices for moving from learning to repeatability.

## Sources & Perspectives

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]]
- **Intro, Part I, Chapters 3-5 (pp. 11-228)**
- Presents startup stages as a disciplined sequence: design, validate, and scale. Maurya's view is that each stage has different risks, different metrics, and different actions, so founders should not use a one-size-fits-all execution style.
- Emphasizes that stage transitions should be driven by evidence: first prove desirability, then viability, then feasibility, and only then prioritize growth.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]]
- **Intro, Chapters 2-5; Validation section (pp. 11-237)**
- Reframes startup stages as a sequence of learning problems: start with the customer's switching trigger and existing alternatives, then test desirability, economics, and execution in stages.
- Strengthens the stage model with traction roadmaps, now-next-later planning, and the customer factory, showing how each stage has its own milestone and bottleneck.
- Clarifies that feasibility is not just "can we build it?" but "what is the smallest stage-appropriate way to pull it off?"

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]]
- **Chapter 6, Validation framework (pp. 238-269)**
- Makes startup stages actionable through 90-day cycles: model the current situation, prioritize the riskiest constraint, and test with small experiments.
- Treats stage progression as a repeated system of goal-setting, campaign design, and sprint-based learning, not a one-time planning exercise.
- Clarifies that stages are managed by the current bottleneck: once one risk is reduced, the next stage's constraint emerges.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]]
- **Chapter 8, Problem discovery and customer understanding (pp. 300-339)**
- Sharpens the early-stage model by showing that the startup's first stage is not only about modeling assumptions, but about deeply understanding customer jobs, triggers, existing alternatives, and switching behavior.
- Reinforces that customer discovery is the foundation for later stage decisions: if the problem is misread, desirability, viability, and feasibility tests will all be distorted.
- Connects stage thinking to customer-forces analysis, jobs-based segmentation, and the idea that early validation begins with learning before pitching.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-12-Get-Ready-to-Launch]]
- **Chapter 12, Launch preparation (pp. 424-439)**
- Adds a distinct launch phase after solution design: build the MVP in a limited number of sprints, then prepare for launch, then release to early access customers in batches.
- Emphasizes that a product launch is not a public marketing launch; it is a controlled soft launch focused on value delivery, learning, and onboarding the first customers.
- Reinforces stage discipline by warning against premature optimization, encouraging company-wide dashboards, and treating early-access rollout as part of the stage progression toward repeatable growth.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-13-Make-Happy-Customers]]
- **Chapter 13, Post-launch optimization and behavior design (pp. 440-466)**
- Extends the stage model beyond launch by focusing on activation, retention, and habit formation as the mechanisms that make customers truly happy.
- Argues that the product must become the customer's new status quo, which requires reducing friction, designing for progress, and reinforcing repeated use.
- Shows that feature requests should be filtered through the stage lens: until the first job is reliably delivered, new jobs should not distract the team.

### [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-15-Epilogue]]
- **Epilogue, manifesto and summary (pp. 488-494)**
- Reframes startup stages as part of the book's final manifesto: the real goal is not to build more, but to avoid building something nobody wants and to spend time on the right risks in the right order.
- Reinforces the stage model's practical payoff: use time wisely, measure traction, remove failure from the vocabulary, and keep progressing through iterative learning rather than big-bang execution.
- Summarizes the full journey from idea to traction as a repeatable discipline rather than a guarantee of success.

> [!note]- On feasibility and rollout timing
> The newer source emphasizes that feasibility is not just raw technical buildability. It is about **stage-appropriate rollout** and, later, **stage-appropriate customer success**: what is the smallest, most realistic way to pull it off right now, and how do we help customers adopt it well? That sharpens the earlier stage framing, but does not contradict it. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-13-Make-Happy-Customers]], pp.440-466]

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A startup goes through stages: first figure out the idea, then test if people want it, then build the smallest version, then launch it to a few people, then help those people get real value, and only then try to grow bigger.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like opening a restaurant: first choose the concept and menu, then see whether people want to pay for it, then run a tiny pilot kitchen, then open softly to a few regulars, then make sure they keep coming back before expanding to more locations.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: A startup should scale as soon as the idea sounds exciting.
> Reality: Early stages are for learning, not scaling.
> Myth: Feasibility only means technical buildability.
> Reality: It also means the smallest stage-appropriate way to deliver value.
> Myth: One roadmap works for every stage.
> Reality: Design, validation, solution design, launch, happy-customer optimization, and growth each need different plans and metrics.
> Myth: Launch means a big public campaign.
> Reality: The book recommends a soft launch to early adopters first.
> Myth: After launch, the job is done.
> Reality: Launch is followed by making customers happy and turning adoption into habit.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> Startup stages help founders avoid the biggest waste: scaling before the model is proven. The stage lens clarifies what to build, what to measure, what to test, and when to launch, which improves learning speed and makes better use of time and money. After launch, the same lens helps teams prevent churn by making customers successful, not just acquiring them.
