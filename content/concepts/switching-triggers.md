# Switching Triggers

A **switching trigger** is an event, change in circumstance, or expectation violation that makes a customer realize their current solution is no longer good enough for the job they need to get done. It creates the opening for a new product, service, or behavior to be considered.

Switching triggers are central to [[jobs-to-be-done]] thinking because they explain **why change happens now** instead of later. In Ash Maurya's framework, innovation is not mainly about inventing a new solution; it is about causing a **switch** from an old way to a better way. A switching trigger is the moment that breaks the customer's comfort with the status quo. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp.108-132]

Maurya expands this idea across desirability, viability, feasibility, and communication. In the viability chapter, switching triggers are not just a curiosity of customer discovery; they are a practical way to identify **who the customer is**, **what they use today**, and **whether they are ready to pay**. Early adopters are not defined by demographic detail alone, but by the fact that they have already experienced a trigger and are ready to act. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp.133-172]

> [!note]
> Maurya uses switching-trigger thinking across the book, but the emphasis shifts by chapter: first to reveal a real problem worth solving, then to identify a paying early adopter, then to guide staged rollout, and finally to structure the pitch itself.

In the feasibility chapter, the same logic helps founders distinguish **starting risks** from **scaling risks**. A now-next-later rollout plan should focus first on the moment that creates the switch, then on the smallest way to deliver value, and only later on expansion. In other words, the trigger tells you not just **who might buy**, but **what stage of the rollout matters now**. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]], pp.179-209]

In the communication chapter, Maurya shows that a good elevator pitch starts with the trigger itself: what changed, what's at stake, what broke, and why the old way no longer works. That makes the trigger the opening move in a clear business model story, not just a research detail. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp.210-228]

The book's introduction also frames the whole startup journey around a switch: an old solution fails, a new one is adopted, and the founder must learn where the customer adoption curve is heading. The trigger is therefore the point where the story begins to move from idea to market reality. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk]], pp.11-49]

The validation section adds a more operational angle: in real product work, switching triggers can show up as **activation problems** or sudden changes in user behavior. If users are dropping off, the question is not only what feature to build, but what changed in their context or expectations that made the existing experience feel inadequate. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]], pp.229-237]

Chapter 6 adds an important systems view: switching triggers should be treated as inputs to the **90-day cycle**. Once a team knows the current constraint, it can use the trigger to choose the most promising campaign, set an objective for the cycle, and make a small bet on the assumption most likely to move traction. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]], pp.238-269]

Chapter 12 extends the same logic into launch discipline. A soft launch works best when the team has already identified the trigger that causes early adopters to care, then rolls out the MVP in batches, keeping the customer factory running so the trigger-to-switch path can be observed and improved over time. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-12-Get-Ready-to-Launch]], pp.424-439]

## What makes a switching trigger different from an ordinary trigger?

Not every trigger leads to change. Many triggers simply start a routine job:
- It is lunchtime, so someone needs to eat.
- It is bedtime, so someone needs to sleep.

A **switching trigger** is more specific. It includes an **expectation violation** that reveals the current alternative is no longer sufficient.

Examples from the source include:
- **A bad experience**: food poisoning at a usual lunch spot
- **A change in circumstance**: a birthday, a new job, a move, a new team
- **An awareness event**: hearing about a new restaurant or product that seems better

These are the moments when the customer becomes open to a different solution.

## Why switching triggers matter

Switching triggers matter because they define the best entry point for innovation. If you understand the trigger, you understand:
- who is most ready to switch,
- what they are dissatisfied with,
- what old solution they are using now,
- and what kind of promise will feel compelling enough to try something new.

This is why the book advises founders to **chase switching triggers** instead of inventing problems. Real opportunities come from old solutions that no longer work well enough.

In the desirability chapter, the trigger is what exposes the old way as broken. In the viability chapter, it helps reveal whether the problem is important enough to support pricing and retention. In the feasibility chapter, it tells you which risks matter first. In the communication chapter, it gives your pitch a concrete "why now" story. In launch planning, it helps you stage rollout so that learning stays tight and visible.

## Problem discovery: switching triggers in the wild

The strongest update from the later chapters is operational: switching triggers are not something you infer from surveys or brainstorming. You uncover them in **problem discovery interviews** by reconstructing the customer's timeline.

Maurya advises founders to avoid asking directly, "What are your problems?" Instead, ask how people used existing alternatives, what changed, what happened before they switched, and what their desired outcome was. The switch becomes visible through:
- workarounds
- pet peeves
- disappointments
- failed expectations
- gaps between promised and actual outcomes
- the moment someone decided the old way was no longer good enough

In Chapter 8, this approach is used to identify jobs-based customer segments by grouping interview stories with the same trigger, chosen existing alternative, and desired outcome. That means a switching trigger is not just a lead-generation clue; it is a segmentation primitive.

Chapter 12 adds that once you have early adopters and an MVP, you should continue to keep the customer factory running. Launch is not the end of switching-trigger thinking; it is the start of measuring whether your trigger, promise, and rollout actually create repeatable value delivery.

## Practical use

When interviewing customers or defining early adopters, ask:
- What happened that made the current solution feel inadequate?
- What were they using before?
- Why did they look for something new?
- What changed in their situation, expectations, or awareness?
- What made the old way stop being good enough?
- What is the smallest first step that would help them switch now?

Those answers reveal whether your product fits a real switching moment and whether that moment belongs in your **now**, **next**, or **later** rollout.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A switching trigger is the moment someone realizes the old way doesn't work well enough anymore, so they become open to trying something new.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like your charger breaks while you're traveling: you weren't shopping before, but now you urgently need a replacement.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> **Myth:** Any trigger means people will switch. **Reality:** A trigger only matters if it makes the current solution feel inadequate.
>
> **Myth:** Switching triggers are product features. **Reality:** They are real-world events or changes in circumstance that create readiness to change.
>
> **Myth:** Interest alone means a switch is happening. **Reality:** A true switching trigger includes a concrete moment of dissatisfaction or change.
>
> **Myth:** Switching triggers only matter for desirability. **Reality:** They also shape early adopters, pricing, rollout timing, pitching, 90-day-cycle planning, and launch strategy.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** What is a switching trigger?  
> **A:** An event or change that makes the current solution feel no longer good enough.
>
> **Q:** Why are switching triggers important in customer discovery?  
> **A:** They reveal people who are already motivated to look for a better way.
>
> **Q:** How do switching triggers relate to the Customer Forces Model?  
> **A:** They create push away from the old way, which combines with pull from the new solution.
>
> **Q:** How do problem discovery interviews uncover switching triggers?  
> **A:** By reconstructing the customer timeline around the trigger, the old solution, and the moment of dissatisfaction.
>
> **Q:** Why do switching triggers matter for launch?  
> **A:** They help you choose the right early adopters, stage the rollout, and keep learning tied to real adoption.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> Switching triggers help founders find customers who are ready now, sharpen positioning, set more realistic pricing, and choose the right risks to tackle first. They also make customer discovery more factual, help define jobs-based segments, improve the timing and focus of rollout plans, and support repeatable launch learning.
