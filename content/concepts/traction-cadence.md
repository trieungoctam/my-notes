# 90-Day Traction Cadence

The **90-Day Traction Cadence** is a recurring operating rhythm for [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] teams that turns uncertainty into short, accountable learning cycles. Rather than executing a giant roadmap all at once, the team works in 90-day blocks to identify the current constraint, choose the riskiest assumptions, run small experiments, and review what changed.

The cadence exists to keep the team focused on **traction**, not activity. Each cycle is designed to improve one or more key metrics in the [[concepts/customer-factory]]: acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral. In *Running Lean*, the cadence also becomes the container for discovery work: teams use early cycles to uncover customer problems, validate demand, and only then move into solution design and build work. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.229-299]

## What the cadence is for

A 90-day cycle helps teams answer three practical questions:

1. **What is the current constraint?**
2. **What should we test next?**
3. **Did we move the needle enough to justify continuing?**

It also creates a bridge between [[concepts/lean-canvas]] modeling and real-world validation. The Lean Canvas captures the business model assumptions; the 90-day cadence turns those assumptions into an execution-and-learning system. In the updated framing, the cadence is not only about testing a business model, but also about systematically discovering whether the customer problem is real, important, and worth solving before solution-building begins. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.238-299]

## How the cadence works

The cycle is typically organized into three phases:

### 1. Modeling
The team updates and reviews the business model, traction roadmap, and assumptions. This is where the team aligns around goals, constraints, and the current version of Plan A.

### 2. Prioritizing
The team identifies the riskiest assumptions and chooses a small number of campaigns to test. This is where the team applies [[concepts/theory-of-constraints]] thinking: focus on the weakest link, not on everything.

### 3. Testing
The team runs a series of short sprints and experiments. In the customer-discovery-heavy version of the cadence, these sprints may begin with problem discovery, move into solution design, and only then test offers or build MVPs.

At the end of the cycle, the team holds a **cycle review** to decide whether to pivot, persevere, or pause.

## Why 90 days?

Maurya argues that 90 days is long enough to make meaningful progress, but short enough to preserve urgency. It is a practical time box for startup learning because it avoids two extremes:

- **too short**: not enough time to learn anything useful
- **too long**: too easy to drift, overbuild, or lose accountability

The cadence also nests inside a larger three-year traction goal. In the book, the three-year horizon is broken into twelve 90-day cycles, which makes the startup journey feel more manageable and measurable.

> [!note] Cadence beyond validation
> Later in the book, the same 90-day rhythm is extended beyond problem/solution fit into the growth stage. Once the team has repeatable value delivery, the cadence still matters: it becomes a way to keep improving retention while also searching for a scalable growth rocket. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-14-Find-Your-Growth-Rocket]], pp.467-487]

## Key design principles

### Work on the current constraint
The cadence assumes the business model always has one dominant bottleneck. Early on, that bottleneck is often customer or market risk; later it may become retention, pricing, or scaling.

### Use small bets
Instead of making one large bet, the team makes many smaller ones. This improves learning speed and reduces waste.

### Align before diverging
The team first agrees on goals, assumptions, and constraints, then separates to create campaign options, and finally reconverges to make decisions.

### Hold yourself externally accountable
The cadence is not just a planning habit. It is a social mechanism for making progress visible to teammates, advisors, and stakeholders.

## Relationship to rollout planning

The 90-day cadence is tightly linked to the [[concepts/now-next-later]] rollout plan:

- **Now** focuses on problem/solution fit
- **Next** focuses on product/market fit
- **Later** focuses on scale

Within each cycle, the team chooses work that matches the current stage of the business model. This prevents premature optimization and keeps the team from chasing scaling work before the foundation is ready.

In the customer-discovery chapters, this becomes even more concrete: early cycles are used to run problem discovery sprints, create jobs-based customer segments, and validate whether the team has uncovered a big enough problem worth solving. That means the cadence is not just a delivery rhythm; it is a discovery rhythm as well. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-345]

## Typical ceremonies

A healthy 90-day cadence includes a regular meeting structure:

- 90-Day Cycle Kickoff
- 90-Day Cycle Planning
- Sprint Planning
- Daily Standup
- Sprint Review
- 90-Day Cycle Review

These ceremonies create a predictable rhythm for learning and decision-making.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> Every 90 days, the team picks the biggest bottleneck, runs focused tests, learns, and then decides what to do next.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> Like training in 12-week blocks: focus on one weakness, test progress, then plan the next block.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> A 90-day cadence is just project tracking. Reality: it is a learning system for reducing risk.
> Every cycle must ship a finished product. Reality: sometimes the right output is a better insight or clearer customer problem.
> Working on many goals at once is faster. Reality: spreading effort thin usually slows learning and progress.
> The cadence ends after launch. Reality: the same rhythm continues into retention and growth once the product works.
