# Value Curve

A **value curve** is a way of mapping how an offering performs across the factors customers care about most, especially relative to existing alternatives. In the context of [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]], it is less about pretty graphs and more about making your [[concepts/lean-canvas]] concrete: what problems do you solve, what outcomes do you promise, and where do you create enough differentiation to cause a customer switch?

In Running Lean terms, the value curve is closely tied to the idea that customers do not buy features for their own sake. They compare your product against what they already use and ask whether your new way is meaningfully better. That comparison is what turns a vague idea into an actionable [[concepts/unique-value-proposition]] and helps you test [[concepts/desirability]] before investing heavily in building.

## Core idea

A value curve answers questions like:
- What matters most to customers in this category?
- How do existing alternatives perform on those dimensions?
- Where is our offering better, worse, or intentionally different?
- Is the difference strong enough to trigger a switch?

This makes the value curve a practical tool for [[concepts/innovators-gift]] thinking: start from the old way, identify the pain points in that old way, and shape a new offer that creates a compelling reason to change.

## How Running Lean uses the idea

Maurya repeatedly emphasizes that the problem is not simply to invent a new solution, but to find a **problem worth solving** by looking at the gap between current alternatives and desired outcomes.

Key uses of the value-curve mindset in the book include:
- **Customer Segments / Problem / UVP**: the early Lean(er) Canvas work is really about finding the right comparison set.
- **Existing alternatives**: the book insists on naming the true alternatives, not just direct competitors.
- **Pricing and viability**: the right price depends on the value customers perceive versus the cost of switching and the cost of staying with the old way.
- **Traction**: the curve only matters if it leads to real adoption, retention, and revenue.

In other words, a value curve is the bridge between a customer problem and a business model that can survive.

## What makes a strong value curve

A useful value curve is usually:

- **Customer-centered** - based on outcomes, not internal features
- **Relative** - measured against current alternatives
- **Specific** - tied to a clear customer segment or early adopter group
- **Outcome-oriented** - focused on what changes for the customer
- **Differentiated** - strong enough to justify attention, adoption, and often higher pricing

Maurya's examples show this clearly. For instance, the AR/VR platform idea is not interesting because it exists; it becomes interesting if it can make AR/VR creation dramatically easier, faster, or cheaper than the current stack. Likewise, the restaurant and Tesla examples show that the "better" offer is usually not the most technically advanced one, but the one that best matches a switching trigger and a desired outcome.

## Relationship to pricing

The value curve also helps explain [[concepts/value-based-pricing]]. In the book, price is not just a cost-plus calculation; it is a signal of positioning and customer segment. A product can only charge a premium if the perceived value curve sits above the old way by enough margin.

That is why Maurya anchors pricing between:
- the **cost of existing alternatives**, and
- the **monetary value of the UVP**

If the value curve is weak, high pricing will fail. If the value curve is strong, price becomes a leverage point for viability.

## Relationship to traction and product/market fit

A value curve is not the end goal. It is a hypothesis about why customers will switch. The real test is whether the curve produces:
- acquisition,
- activation,
- retention,
- revenue, and
- referral.

Once those customer-factory metrics improve, the value curve may be strong enough to support [[concepts/product-market-fit]]. If not, the idea likely needs a different customer segment, a different problem, or a different positioning story.

## Practical questions to ask

When sketching a value curve, ask:

1. What are the top 3-5 dimensions that matter in this market?
2. What does the current alternative do well?
3. Where are customers frustrated, delayed, overcharged, or underserved?
4. Which dimension can we own meaningfully better than the old way?
5. Does that improvement align with a real switching trigger?

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A value curve is a picture of how your product compares to the old way on the things customers care about. If your product is only a little better, people may not switch. If it is much better on the right things, they are more likely to try it, pay for it, and keep using it.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> Choosing a restaurant: you do not only compare food. You also compare price, speed, convenience, atmosphere, and reliability. A value curve is like scoring each restaurant on those same dimensions to see which one is actually better for you.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: A value curve is just a feature list. Reality: It compares outcomes and tradeoffs against existing alternatives.
> Myth: The best product is the one with the most features. Reality: The best product is the one that is better in the ways customers actually care about.
> Myth: If customers like the idea, they will automatically switch. Reality: Switching depends on whether the new offer is compelling enough relative to the old way.
