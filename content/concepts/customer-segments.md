# Customer Segments

Customer Segments are the specific people or organizations a business model is designed to serve. In [[concepts/lean-canvas]] thinking, this is one of the earliest and most important blocks to define because it anchors the rest of the model: the problem, unique value proposition, channels, revenue, metrics, and even feasibility assumptions all depend on who the product is for.

*Running Lean* treats Customer Segments as a design choice, not a demographic afterthought. The book's key message is that you should not start with a broad audience like "everyone." Instead, you should identify:
- the total addressable market at a high level,
- the specific customer segment you intend to serve,
- and, most importantly, your **early adopters** - the narrow subset most likely to care enough to try your idea first.

The book also makes a crucial distinction between **customers** and **users** when multiple actors are involved:
- A **customer** pays for the product.
- A **user** may use it but not pay for it.

That distinction matters because different actors can have different needs, different problems, and even different value propositions. In [[concepts/multisided-models]] or [[concepts/marketplace-models]]-style business models, you may need to model both sides separately.

## Why Customer Segments Matter

Customer Segments determine whether your idea is focused enough to be testable. If the segment is too broad, the model becomes vague and diluted. If it is too narrow, you risk picking a local maximum and missing a larger opportunity. The book recommends balancing breadth and specificity by starting with one clear customer story on a canvas, then exploring variants on separate canvases if needed.

This is tied to the broader theme of [[concepts/traction]]: traction comes from solving a real problem for a definable group of people, not from trying to appeal to everyone at once.

The book also connects Customer Segments to the broader shift from build-first thinking to traction-first thinking. In a world where products are easier to build and customers have more options, a founder needs a segment that is specific enough to support interviews, experiments, messaging, and early validation.

In the viability chapter, the segment also has to "work on paper." The number of active customers required for your target revenue, the churn rate you can tolerate, and the acquisition rate you can sustain all depend on how big and reachable the segment is. So Customer Segments are not just a desirability question; they are also a [[concepts/viability]] question.

## Key Principles from *Running Lean*

### 1. Start with customers, not solutions
The book repeatedly emphasizes that customers care about their problems, not your clever solution. That means the Customer Segments block should be defined before you get too attached to features or technology.

### 2. Identify early adopters
Your first goal is not mass adoption. It is to find the people most likely to adopt early because they have the strongest pain, urgency, or motivation. These are the people you can learn from fastest.

### 3. Separate customers from users
In products with multiple actors, the paying customer may be different from the end user. For example:
- In a blogging platform, the blogger is the customer and the reader is the user.
- In an ad-supported search engine, the advertiser is the customer and the searcher is the user.

This distinction helps avoid confusion in [[concepts/problem-solution-fit]] and later validation work.

### 4. Model multiple perspectives when needed
If a business model includes more than one actor, each perspective may need its own assumptions, problems, and channels. The book uses this idea later when discussing complex models like Facebook and Airbnb.

### 5. Keep it narrow enough to be actionable
The goal is not to define the entire market in one shot. The goal is to define a segment precise enough to support interviews, experiments, messaging, and early traction.

### 6. Split one idea into multiple canvases when necessary
When a single canvas tries to describe several distinct business model stories, it becomes muddled. The book recommends splitting the idea into separate canvases when there are different customer stories, different monetization logics, or multiple archetypes mixed together.

> [!note] Possible tension with broad-market thinking
> The book encourages you to think broadly across possible variants, but narrowly within each canvas. That means you should explore multiple potential customer stories, yet keep each individual Lean Canvas focused on one coherent business model.

## Desirability Lens: Customer Segments in Context

The desirability chapter adds an important refinement: Customer Segments should not be defined only by demographics or product category, but by the **job-to-be-done** and the **switching trigger** that moves someone away from an existing alternative.

In other words, the best early adopters are not just people who fit a profile; they are people who have experienced an expectation violation and are actively looking for a better way. This means Customer Segments should be defined alongside:
- [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]]
- [[concepts/switching-triggers]]
- [[concepts/innovators-gift]]
- [[concepts/customer-forces-model]]

This source also reframes the segment question as: **who is being pushed to change, and what are they trying to get done?** That is a stronger lens than static demographics alone.

> [!note] Segmenting by trigger matters
> The newer desirability framing suggests that two people with the same job title may be different segments if only one has a switching trigger. So a useful customer segment is often defined by context, urgency, and current alternative-not just by role.

## Viability Lens: Customer Segments Must Be Big Enough to Work

The viability chapter adds another filter: even if a segment is desirable, it must also support the business model economically. Maurya uses a Fermi-style estimate to ask whether the model can plausibly reach a minimum success target.

That means Customer Segments must be large and accessible enough to support:
- the number of active customers needed,
- the conversion rate you can realistically achieve,
- the retention or churn profile you can sustain,
- and the referral rate you can leverage.

A segment can look attractive in theory but still fail viability if:
- it is too small,
- the customers cannot pay enough,
- churn is too high,
- or acquisition costs are too heavy.

So Customer Segments are not just about "who might like this?" but also "who can sustain this business model?"

> [!warning]- A desirable segment is not always a viable segment
> A group may strongly want the product but still not produce enough revenue, retention, or scale to support the business. Running Lean insists on testing the segment through both desirability and viability lenses.

## Example from the Book: Steve's AR/VR Product

In the opening Lean Canvas example, Steve initially wants to target "everybody" for his imagined AR/VR platform. The book shows why that is too broad. After reflecting on the advice to avoid marketing to everyone, he narrows his Customer Segments to:
- software developers/agencies,
- marketers,
- retail,
- construction,
- travel,
- education,
- healthcare.

He then narrows his **early adopters** further to:
- software developers/agencies who build AR/VR apps for clients.

This move matters because it gives him a realistic starting point. He can now shape his [[concepts/unique-value-proposition]] around a specific audience and choose channels that can actually reach them.

Later, the book shows Steve realizing that these may actually represent multiple business models, not just one. He splits the canvas into more specific variants, such as:
- software developers/agencies,
- home construction,
- retail furniture.

This is an important lesson: Customer Segments are not just about who might like the idea; they are also about whether the idea actually belongs to one business model or several.

The desirability chapter sharpens that insight further: Steve's real segments should be defined by the **switching story** around what customers are trying to do. In the AR/VR example, the deeper segment is not "people who want AR/VR," but people who want better outcomes such as:
- more billable work,
- better sales conversions,
- better home visualization.

That shift moves the model from solution-centric to outcome-centric thinking.

The viability chapter then adds a hard reality check. Steve's chosen segment must support enough revenue to reach his minimum success criteria. When he estimates the number of customers needed for a $10M ARR goal, he realizes the segment and pricing assumptions are far tighter than they first appeared. In other words, the segment choice determines whether the model is not only desirable, but scalable enough to survive.

## Customer Segments in Different Business Model Types

The book notes that the right way to define segments depends on the business model archetype:

- **Direct model**: the user is also the customer.
- **Multisided model**: users and customers are different groups.
- **Marketplace model**: buyers and sellers are distinct segments.

In all cases, the rule is the same: define the segment from the perspective of the people whose behavior matters most to the model.

## Practical Implications

When you define Customer Segments well, you gain clarity on:
- who to interview,
- who to target first,
- what problem to investigate,
- what language to use in your UVP,
- which channels are realistic,
- what success metrics matter,
- and whether the segment can support your revenue goal.

Poorly defined segments lead to weak positioning, weak validation, and weak traction.

A good Customer Segments definition also helps you avoid the build trap. If you know exactly who you serve, you can validate whether the solution is desirable before you spend too much time building it. And if you can size that segment against a minimum success criterion, you can avoid pursuing ideas that look exciting but cannot become a viable business.
