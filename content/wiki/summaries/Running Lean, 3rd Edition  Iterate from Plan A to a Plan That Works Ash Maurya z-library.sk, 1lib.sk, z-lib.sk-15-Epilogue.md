# Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Summary

Ash Maurya presents a systematic framework for turning an early-stage idea into a working business model by iterating from **Plan A** to a plan that works. The core message is that startups fail less because of execution mistakes and more because they start with the wrong assumptions, focus on solutions too early, or chase scale before proving demand. The book reframes entrepreneurship as a disciplined process of **business model design**, **customer discovery**, **validation**, **offer delivery**, and **growth**.

## Main Thesis

The book argues that in today's low-cost, high-competition environment, the biggest risk is not technical feasibility first, but whether customers actually want the product and whether the business can monetize it. Instead of a traditional business plan, Maurya recommends a one-page [[Lean Canvas]] and a set of iterative experiments to test the riskiest assumptions first.

The central idea is that:
- **the business model is the product**
- **traction is the goal**
- **speed of learning is the unfair advantage**
- **the best way to build is to start with problems, not solutions**

## Key Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Continuous Innovation Framework
A framework of frameworks that combines ideas from [[Lean Startup]], design thinking, business model design, jobs-to-be-done, systems thinking, and behavior design. It organizes work into three repeating activities:
- **Model**
- **Prioritize**
- **Test**

This framework is used to move from a rough idea toward product/market fit through repeated 90-day cycles.

### 2. Lean Canvas
A one-page business model template used to replace long business plans. It helps founders capture the key assumptions behind an idea:
- Problem
- Solution
- Unique value proposition
- Customer segments
- Early adopters
- Existing alternatives
- Channels
- Revenue streams
- Cost structure
- Key metrics
- Unfair advantage

The book emphasizes that the canvas should be kept simple, filled out quickly, and treated as a snapshot of current thinking rather than a permanent plan.

### 3. Customer Factory
A system model for measuring traction. Customers move through:
- **Acquisition**
- **Activation**
- **Retention**
- **Revenue**
- **Referral**

Traction is defined as the rate at which the business captures monetizable value from customers. The customer factory helps reveal bottlenecks and supports evidence-based decisions.

### 4. Traction Roadmap
A staged growth model that translates a long-term success goal into short-term milestones. It helps founders distinguish between:
- **problem/solution fit**
- **product/market fit**
- **scale**

Maurya recommends starting with a 10x growth mindset over a three-year horizon, then working backward into 90-day goals.

### 5. Customer Forces Canvas
A behavioral model for understanding why customers switch from one solution to another. It includes four forces:
- **Push** - dissatisfaction with the current state
- **Pull** - attraction to the new solution
- **Inertia** - comfort with the old way
- **Friction** - anxiety and usability barriers in the new way

This model is used to uncover switching triggers, desired outcomes, and true competition.

## Major Arguments and Findings

### Start with a problem, not a solution
A recurring theme is that founders often invent problems to justify solutions they already love. Maurya calls this [[Innovator's Bias]]. The antidote is the [[Innovator's Gift]]: new problems come from old solutions. Good opportunities arise when existing solutions break under a new trigger or new context.

### Innovation is about causing a switch
The book frames innovation as helping customers switch from an old way of doing a job to a better new way. To do that, a product must be:
- desirable
- viable
- feasible

This is the [[Innovation Trinity]].

### Traction matters more than vision alone
Investors no longer fund ideas or patents; they fund evidence of a working business model. That evidence is traction. Maurya repeatedly contrasts "build-first" and "investor-first" behavior with a traction-first approach.

### Validation should be staged
The book strongly rejects the idea of building a complete product before testing demand. Instead, it proposes a staged process:
1. **Desirability** - Is there a real problem and a compelling promise?
2. **Viability** - Can the model make money?
3. **Feasibility** - Can it be built and delivered quickly enough?

### Experiments should be small and time-boxed
Maurya emphasizes 90-day cycles and two-week sprints. He recommends that experiments:
- declare expected outcomes up front
- be falsifiable
- use ranges rather than false precision
- measure actions, not opinions
- include control groups when possible

### Pitching is about storytelling and learning
The book provides a pitch structure aimed at different audiences:
- **Advisors**: learning frame
- **Customers**: problem/solution story
- **Investors**: market size, traction, margins, defensibility

The ideal pitch follows a Hero's Journey-like structure:
- bigger context
- stakes
- problem/old way
- better new way
- call to action

## Process Flow of the Book

### Part I: Design
The first part focuses on deconstructing an idea into a business model and stress testing it for:
- desirability
- viability
- feasibility

This begins with the Lean Canvas and a first pass at identifying customer segments, problems, UVP, and pricing.

### Part II: Validation
This section uses 90-day cycles and problem discovery to uncover customer jobs-to-be-done, then validates whether the opportunity is worth pursuing. It introduces:
- customer interviews
- problem discovery sprints
- solution design sprints
- offer delivery sprints
- cycle reviews

### Part III: Growth
Once problem/solution fit and product/market fit are in sight, the focus shifts to making happy customers and finding a scalable growth rocket. The book explains how to build sustainable growth through:
- revenue loops
- retention loops
- referral loops
- content/data loops

## Notable Examples Used in the Book

The book uses a long-running story of two entrepreneurs, Steve and Larry, to contrast two mindsets:
- **Steve** = build-first, solution-centric, slow to learn
- **Larry** = traction-first, problem-centric, iterative

Through this story and later examples, Maurya illustrates:
- why first movers are often at a disadvantage
- why product launches should start with an offer rather than a finished product
- why MVPs can be concierge, wizard-of-oz, or foot-in-the-door style
- why high-touch learning is often more valuable than scalable automation early on

Other examples include:
- music format transitions
- drill bits vs. "hanging a painting" jobs-to-be-done
- Tesla's staged rollout
- the iPhone and iPad as mafia offers
- restaurants and food trucks as staged validation examples
- home construction and architecture as real-world service/product hybrids

## Deep Takeaways

### 1. Time is the scarcest resource
The book repeatedly warns against wasting months on plans, features, or forecasts that can't be validated. Time-boxed cycles force focus and urgency.

### 2. A business model must work on paper before it can work in the world
The Fermi estimate exercise is used to test whether the economics can plausibly support the goal. If not, the model, pricing, segment, or goal must change.

### 3. Most problems are actually jobs-to-be-done
People don't want products; they want outcomes. Better product strategy comes from understanding the broader context in which a product is hired.

### 4. Growth is a system, not a slogan
Sustainable growth comes from feedback loops, not one-time campaigns. The book encourages founders to find a renewable propellant and an efficient engine.

### 5. Making happy customers is a behavioral problem
After acquisition, the real work is reducing friction, reinforcing progress, and helping users form new habits so the product becomes the new status quo.

## Practical Artifacts and Tools

The book repeatedly references artifacts and templates that support the process:
- [[Lean Canvas]]
- Fermi estimate
- traction roadmap
- customer forces canvas
- progress report deck
- 90-day cycle review deck
- problem discovery interview meta-script
- mafia offer pitch script
- company-wide metrics dashboard

## Bottom Line

Running Lean is a guide for building startups and new products in a disciplined, evidence-based way. Rather than betting everything on a single idea, the book teaches founders to continuously model, test, learn, and adapt until they find a scalable business model that customers want, can be built in time, and can grow sustainably.
