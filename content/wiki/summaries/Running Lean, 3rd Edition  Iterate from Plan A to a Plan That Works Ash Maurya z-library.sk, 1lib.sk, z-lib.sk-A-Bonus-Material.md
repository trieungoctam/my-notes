# Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Summary

Ash Maurya's *Running Lean* presents a step-by-step method for turning an early idea into a working business model by iterating from Plan A toward a plan that works before resources run out. The book argues that in today's environment, startups should prioritize traction, customer learning, and evidence-based decisions over heavy upfront planning.

## Core Thesis

The central idea is that the **business model is the product**. Rather than building first and hoping customers will come, founders should:

1. **Model** the business as assumptions
2. **Prioritize** the riskiest assumptions
3. **Test** them with fast, small experiments

This overall system is framed as the [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] framework.

## Key Themes

### 1. Build the business model before the product
A Lean Canvas replaces long business plans with a one-page model. It captures the essential assumptions behind the venture:

- customer segments
- problems
- solution
- unique value proposition
- existing alternatives
- channels
- revenue streams
- cost structure
- key metrics
- unfair advantage

The book repeatedly emphasizes that the solution box is the least important early on; the riskiest assumptions usually lie in the customer, problem, and monetization sides of the model.

### 2. Start with problems, not solutions
A major mindset shift is to **love the problem, not the solution**. Customers care about outcomes and jobs-to-be-done, not product features. Maurya stresses that strong innovation comes from finding a real problem with an existing alternative, then building a better way to do the job.

This idea is developed through:

- [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]]
- [[concepts/customer-forces]]
- [[concepts/innovation-switching]]

### 3. Use traction as the main measure of progress
The book defines traction as the rate at which a business model captures monetizable value from customers. Traction is treated as a leading indicator of a healthy business model, unlike vanity metrics or purely financial forecasts.

The customer factory model breaks traction into:

- acquisition
- activation
- retention
- revenue
- referral

This becomes a recurring measurement and optimization system.

### 4. Validate viability with a Fermi estimate
Instead of building a fragile spreadsheet forecast, the book recommends ballparking viability with a Fermi-style estimate. Founders should test whether their business model can realistically reach a minimum success criteria (MSC) within a chosen time horizon.

Key levers include:

- pricing
- customer lifetime / churn
- conversion rates
- acquisition volume

The chapter makes clear that pricing is often the most underused lever and should be anchored against both customer value and existing alternatives.

### 5. Validate feasibility with traction roadmaps and rollout planning
Maurya argues that feasibility is not about long-term product planning but about mapping a realistic trajectory from now to product/market fit and beyond. The traction roadmap breaks the journey into phases:

- now: problem/solution fit
- next: product/market fit
- later: scale

This leads to a **now-next-later** planning mindset and a stage-based rollout strategy.

### 6. Communicate the story to stakeholders
The book shows how to communicate the business model clearly to different audiences:

- **advisors** need a learning frame
- **customers** need a promise and demo
- **investors** need market size, margins, defensibility, and traction

The pitch is structured as a story: switching trigger, stakes, old way, new way, and call to action.

## Process Overview

The entire book is organized around a practical sequence:

### Part I: Design
- Deconstruct the idea on a Lean Canvas
- Stress test desirability
- Stress test viability
- Stress test feasibility
- Communicate the idea clearly

### Part II: Validation
- Run 90-day cycles
- Conduct problem discovery interviews
- Design offers and MVPs
- Validate problem/solution fit
- Manage cycles with metrics and experiments

### Part III: Growth
- Launch in batches
- Make happy customers
- Find a growth rocket
- Scale through sustainable loops

## Important Frameworks and Concepts

### Lean Canvas
A one-page model for capturing the essential assumptions of a business. It forces clarity and makes it easier to test assumptions quickly.

### Innovator's Bias
Founders tend to justify the solution they already want to build. The book warns against inventing problems to fit a solution.

### Innovator's Gift
A better framing: new problems come from old solutions. Innovation is about causing a switch from an existing alternative to a better new way.

### Customer Forces Model
A behavioral model describing:

- Push
- Pull
- Inertia
- Friction

This model helps explain why people switch, why they resist change, and how to design for adoption.

### Mafia Offer
An offer so compelling that customers cannot refuse. It combines:

- a strong UVP
- a demo
- a call-to-action

It is used to validate demand before building a full product.

### Concierge MVP, Wizard-of-Oz MVP, Foot-in-the-Door MVP
These are different ways to deliver value and test assumptions without building the full product immediately.

### 90-Day Cycles
The core operating rhythm of the framework. Each cycle includes:

- modeling
- prioritizing
- testing
- review

This keeps teams externally accountable and focused on the riskiest assumptions.

### Growth Rockets
Maurya's growth model distinguishes between:

- booster rockets: temporary, non-scalable channels
- primary growth rockets: sustainable, scalable channels powered by a loop

He identifies three types of sustainable loops:

- revenue growth loops
- retention growth loops
- referral growth loops

## Narrative Example: Steve vs. Larry
A recurring story contrasts two entrepreneurs:

- **Steve** follows a build-first path, spending too much time polishing a product no one wants.
- **Larry** follows a traction-first path, validating demand early, focusing on problems, and using small experiments to build a working business model.

This story illustrates the difference between traditional product thinking and [[concepts/continuous-innovation]].

## Practical Takeaways

- Start with a one-page business model, not a long plan.
- Validate the problem before validating the solution.
- Treat traction as the main scorecard.
- Use pricing as a strategic lever, not an afterthought.
- Run customer interviews before building.
- Launch in small batches and learn fast.
- Optimize for repeatability before scale.
- Use cohorts, dashboards, and 90-day reviews to stay honest.

## Overall Contribution

The book provides a full operating system for early-stage entrepreneurship: a way to model ideas, test assumptions, learn from customers, and move from an unproven concept to a repeatable business. Its strongest contribution is showing how to combine [[concepts/lean-canvas]], [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]], [[concepts/customer-forces]], and [[concepts/traction]] into one practical innovation workflow.
