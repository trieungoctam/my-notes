# Summary: Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works

Ash Maurya presents a systematic approach for turning an initial idea into a working business model under conditions of extreme uncertainty. The core argument is that founders should not start by building products or writing long plans, but by designing, stress-testing, and iterating a business model until it can reliably create traction, serve customers, and scale.

## Core Thesis

The book's central claim is that the startup's real product is the **business model**, not the software, service, or feature set. Instead of relying on intuition, optimism, or heavyweight planning, founders should use a structured process to move from **Plan A** toward **a plan that works**.

This process is organized into the [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] framework, which combines:
- [[concepts/lean-canvas]] for business model design
- customer discovery and problem discovery interviews
- viability modeling through a Fermi estimate
- feasibility planning through a traction roadmap
- offer creation and delivery before product build
- repeated 90-day cycles of model, prioritize, and test

## Main Ideas

### 1. The old startup playbook is backward
Maurya contrasts two founders:
- **Steve**, who builds first and hopes to sell later
- **Larry**, who starts with customer demand, traction, and model validation

The book argues that the build-first or investor-first approach is too risky in today's market because:
- products are cheaper and easier to make
- customers have many more alternatives
- traction matters more than intellectual property
- investors fund working business models, not just ideas

### 2. Traction is the real goal
A major theme is that **traction** is the best early signal of a working business model. Traction is not vanity growth; it is the rate at which the business captures monetizable value from customers.

The book breaks traction into the customer factory steps:
- acquisition
- activation
- retention
- revenue
- referral

This maps closely to [[concepts/customer-factory]] and the idea that a business should be treated as a system with feedback loops, not a linear funnel.

### 3. Start with problems, not solutions
A recurring warning is against [[concepts/innovator-bias]]: the tendency to invent or distort problems to justify an already-chosen solution.

Instead, Maurya pushes founders to use the [[concepts/innovators-gift]]:
- new problems come from old solutions
- innovation happens when customers switch from one way of doing a job to a better one
- the right opportunity is usually a broken or insufficient existing alternative

This leads to a [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] perspective where the focus is on the customer's desired outcome, not the product's features.

### 4. Business model validation happens in stages
The book repeatedly emphasizes that validation should happen in the right order:
1. **Desirability** - do customers want this?
2. **Viability** - can it monetize?
3. **Feasibility** - can we build and deliver it?

These stress tests are applied to the Lean Canvas and then refined over time using [[concepts/falsifiable-hypotheses]] and small experiments.

### 5. Use 90-day cycles and small bets
Rather than long planning horizons, Maurya recommends 90-day cycles structured around:
- modeling
- prioritizing
- testing

Teams should focus on the current bottleneck, make many small bets, and use evidence-based decisions. This is closely aligned with [[concepts/theory-of-constraints]] and [[concepts/continuous-learning]].

## Business Model Design

### Lean Canvas
The [[concepts/lean-canvas]] is the first major tool in the book. It replaces a traditional business plan with a one-page model that can be created quickly and updated often.

The canvas includes:
- problem
- solution
- unique value proposition
- unfair advantage
- customer segments
- existing alternatives
- key metrics
- channels
- early adopters
- cost structure
- revenue streams

The text strongly recommends:
- keeping the canvas to one page
- sketching it quickly
- avoiding groupthink
- leaving boxes blank when uncertain
- using simple customer labels
- splitting canvases when they mix multiple business model archetypes

### Different business model archetypes
Maurya explains that some models are:
- direct
- multisided
- marketplace

He uses examples like Starbucks, Facebook, and Airbnb to show that the canvas should reflect the structure of the business and the different roles involved.

### The importance of early adopters
The book stresses that founders should not target the mass market first. Instead, they should focus on a narrow group of early adopters who:
- have experienced a switching trigger
- are already using an existing alternative
- are motivated to change
- are close enough to the problem that they will engage

This makes [[concepts/early-adopters]] a key cross-document concept.

## Key Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Innovation Trinity
Success requires the overlap of:
- desirability
- viability
- feasibility

This is the intersection where a business idea is both wanted, buildable, and monetizable.

### 2. Customer Forces Model
The [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] explains behavior change through four forces:
- push
- pull
- inertia
- friction

This model is used throughout the book to explain switching, activation, retention, and product adoption.

### 3. Traction Roadmap
The [[concepts/traction-roadmap]] turns long-term ambition into staged milestones. It connects:
- short-term validation
- product/market fit
- scale

The book repeatedly uses 10x-style growth assumptions to estimate how many customers are needed at each stage.

### 4. Mafia Offer
The [[concepts/mafia-offer]] is a high-conversion validation offer assembled from:
- a clear UVP
- a demo
- a call-to-action

It is designed to uncover whether customers will actually commit, before the product is fully built.

### 5. Concierge MVP and Wizard-of-Oz MVP
Maurya presents multiple MVP recipes, including:
- Concierge MVP: deliver the service manually at first
- Wizard-of-Oz MVP: fake the product with existing tools or manual workflows
- Foot-in-the-door MVP: deliver the smallest meaningful step

These recipes help reduce feasibility risk and speed up learning.

## The Steve Story

A major narrative thread follows Steve, an entrepreneur building an AR/VR product. Over time, he learns to:
- move from a vague product idea to a Lean Canvas
- narrow down customer segments
- identify real problems and alternatives
- use pricing as a serious viability lever
- stop thinking in terms of features and start thinking in terms of customer jobs
- use customer interviews instead of surveys for discovery
- design a solution that can cause a switch
- use a Concierge MVP to launch quickly
- run 90-day cycles with his team
- optimize for happy customers rather than feature accumulation

Steve's eventual pivot toward home construction and architecture reveals a deeper job: not just creating 3D/AR/VR models, but helping architects and homeowners visualize, decide, and communicate around design. This is a strong example of moving from solution-centric thinking to job-centric thinking.

## Discovery and Interviewing

The book gives detailed guidance for customer discovery:
- interview people who recently used an existing alternative
- avoid leading with the word "problem"
- ask about actual behavior, not hypotheticals
- favor face-to-face interviews
- work backward from a recent event to uncover the timeline
- capture findings on a [[concepts/customer-forces-canvas]]

It warns against:
- surveys too early
- focus groups
- solution pitching during discovery
- relying only on metrics without context

## Offer Delivery and Pitching

Maurya distinguishes between:
- a learning frame for advisors and early conversations
- a customer frame for prospects
- an investor frame focused on traction, market size, margins, and defensibility

He recommends a four-act pitch structure:
1. setup
2. confrontation
3. resolution
4. call-to-action

The pitch should tell a story about:
- what changed
- what's at stake
- what the old way gets wrong
- why the new way is better
- what the customer should do next

## Launch and Post-Launch

After MVP launch, the book stresses that the work is not over. Teams must:
- keep the customer factory running
- measure customer behavior in cohorts
- focus on actionable metrics, not vanity metrics
- improve activation and retention
- reduce friction
- prevent churn and switching away
- use behavior design and habit formation principles

This section also introduces the idea that products should help customers progress in stages, creating repeated "aha moments" and reinforcing the product as the new status quo.

## Practical Takeaways

If condensed into a few actionable lessons, the book says:
- don't start by building
- start with a business model sketch
- identify the riskiest assumptions
- validate desirability before viability and feasibility
- focus on real customer behavior
- use offers to test demand before building
- keep your team aligned through 90-day cycles
- optimize for traction and happy customers
- always be willing to pivot based on evidence

## Overall Assessment

Running Lean is a comprehensive operating system for early-stage innovation. It combines strategic thinking, customer discovery, lightweight modeling, and staged experimentation into one coherent method. Its main contribution is making entrepreneurship feel less like guesswork and more like a disciplined process of learning, validation, and iteration.
