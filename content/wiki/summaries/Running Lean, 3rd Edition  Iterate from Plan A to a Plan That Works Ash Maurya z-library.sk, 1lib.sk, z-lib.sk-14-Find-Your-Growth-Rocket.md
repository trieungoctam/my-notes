# Summary: *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - Ash Maurya

This document lays out a systematic approach for turning an early idea into a working business model by iterating from **Plan A** to a **plan that works**. Its central message is that startups should not start with a product roadmap or a long business plan, but with a **business model design**, then stress test that model through staged validation cycles focused on the riskiest assumptions first.

The book frames entrepreneurship as a process of continuously moving through **design → validation → growth**, using lightweight tools and evidence-based decisions to avoid the build trap and premature optimization.

## Core Thesis

- A startup's real product is its **business model**, not just its solution.
- Success comes from **finding a plan that works before running out of resources**.
- The most important early question is not "Can we build it?" but **"Should we build it?"**
- The right approach is **traction-first**, not build-first or investor-first.

## The Continuous Innovation Framework

The book introduces a framework made of three repeating activities:

1. **Model** - deconstruct the idea into a business model
2. **Prioritize** - identify the riskiest assumptions and sequence them
3. **Test** - run small, fast experiments to validate or invalidate those assumptions

This is supported by 90-day cycles, weekly sprints, and recurring review meetings. The framework emphasizes that risks shift over time, so the team must keep re-evaluating what is riskiest now.

## Key Models and Tools

### Lean Canvas
The Lean Canvas is the main design tool. It replaces long business plans with a one-page model that captures:

- Customer segments
- Problems
- Unique value proposition
- Solution
- Channels
- Revenue streams
- Cost structure
- Key metrics
- Unfair advantage
- Existing alternatives

The canvas is used as a snapshot of assumptions, not a final truth.

### Lean(er) Canvas
A reduced version focusing only on:

- Customer segments
- Problem
- Unique value proposition

This is used for desirability testing when the main question is whether the customer problem is real and compelling.

### Customer Factory
The book uses a factory metaphor to describe traction as the throughput of the business model. The customer factory has five macro steps:

1. Acquisition
2. Activation
3. Retention
4. Revenue
5. Referral

This model is used to define and measure traction, identify bottlenecks, and understand growth.

### Traction Roadmap
A traction roadmap translates a high-level minimum success criteria into a staged growth curve. It helps the founder understand:

- how many customers are needed
- by when they are needed
- what growth rate is realistic
- which milestones matter at each stage

### Customer Forces Model
The Customer Forces Model explains switching behavior using four forces:

- **Push** - dissatisfaction with the current state
- **Pull** - attraction to the new solution
- **Inertia** - comfort with the status quo
- **Friction** - resistance to trying the new solution

This model is central to understanding why customers switch and how to design offers that cause a switch.

## The Three Stress Tests

### 1. Desirability
Questions:

- Do customers want this?
- What problem are we solving?
- What are the existing alternatives?
- What job is the customer trying to get done?

The book argues that the best way to uncover desirability is to start with the customer's current workflow and look for **friction, dissatisfaction, and switching triggers**.

### 2. Viability
Questions:

- Can we monetize this?
- Can the business model reach a minimum success threshold?
- Does the pricing and retention math work?

A **Fermi estimate** is used instead of a long financial forecast. This forces the founder to check whether the model works on paper using a handful of key assumptions.

### 3. Feasibility
Questions:

- Can we build this?
- Can we deliver it fast enough?
- Can we launch the smallest version that causes a switch?

Instead of a product roadmap, the book recommends a **traction roadmap** and stage-based rollout plan.

## MVP Philosophy

The book strongly reframes the MVP:

- MVP is not just the first tiny product
- MVP is the **smallest solution that creates, delivers, and captures customer value**
- MVP should be defined **after** problem discovery and solution design, not before
- A good MVP is a vehicle for learning and for causing a switch

It outlines several MVP recipes:

- **Concierge MVP** - manual service first, product later
- **Wizard-of-Oz MVP** - fake the automation until you can build it
- **Foot-in-the-Door MVP** - start with the smallest meaningful entry point

Related concepts: [[concepts/mvp]], [[concepts/concierge-mvp]], [[concepts/wizard-of-oz-mvp]], [[concepts/foot-in-the-door]], [[concepts/switching-behavior]]

## Customer Discovery and Interviewing

The book emphasizes that learning starts with **one-on-one interviews**, not surveys or focus groups. Surveys are criticized because they assume you already know the right questions and answers. Instead, the founder should:

- ask about the customer's timeline
- identify triggering events
- study how the customer uses existing alternatives
- uncover workarounds, pet peeves, and unmet needs
- reconstruct the story chronologically

A major insight is that interviews should focus on **the bigger context** of the job-to-be-done, not just the immediate product category.

### Customer Forces Canvas
After each interview, the founder captures findings in a structured canvas:

- Push
- Pull
- Inertia
- Friction
- Desired outcome
- Current and actual outcomes
- Next summit

This turns raw interview notes into repeatable insight.

## Problem Discovery → Solution Design → Offer Delivery

The book breaks validation into three sequential sprints:

### 1. Problem Discovery
Find out what is broken in the current way of doing the job.

### 2. Solution Design
Design the smallest solution that can cause a switch and satisfy the strongest customer jobs.

### 3. Offer Delivery
Assemble the story, demo, and call-to-action into a compelling mafia offer that customers cannot refuse.

This progression matters because the founder should learn before pitching, and pitch before building a full product.

Related concepts: [[concepts/mafia-offer]], [[concepts/customer-story-pitch]], [[concepts/problem-discovery]], [[concepts/solution-design]]

## Pitching and Storytelling

The book argues that pitching should be structured around the customer's worldview, not the founder's. It recommends a story format with four acts:

1. Setup - bigger context and switching trigger
2. Confrontation - break the old way
3. Resolution - demo the better new way
4. Call-to-action - ask for the switch

It also distinguishes how to pitch to:

- customers
- investors
- advisors

Each group cares about different parts of the model, but all are influenced by traction and a coherent narrative.

Related concepts: [[concepts/pitching]], [[concepts/customer-worldview]], [[concepts/investor-worldview]], [[concepts/advisor-worldview]], [[concepts/storytelling]]

## 90-Day Cycles and External Accountability

A major operational theme is running the startup in **90-day cycles**. Each cycle includes:

- kickoff and alignment
- modeling and prioritization
- sprint-based testing
- cycle review
- a 3P decision: **persevere, pivot, or pause**

This gives the team an external accountability structure and prevents drifting into endless execution without learning.

The book recommends:

- small complete teams
- cross-functional skill sets
- visible dashboards
- regular review ceremonies
- evidence-based decisions

Related concepts: [[concepts/90-day-cycle]], [[concepts/external-accountability]], [[concepts/persevere-pivot-pause]], [[concepts/experimentation]], [[concepts/constraints]]

## Behavior Design and Happy Customers

Once early customers are acquired, the next challenge is making them stay and succeed. The book connects retention to behavior design and habit formation:

- trigger
- action
- reward

It stresses that the first goal after acquisition is not expansion, but helping customers reach their **aha moment** and then repeatedly experience value.

It recommends designing a **customer progress roadmap** that breaks the big outcome into smaller summits, making it easier to activate, retain, and eventually convert customers into advocates.

Related concepts: [[concepts/habit-loop]], [[concepts/behavior-design]], [[concepts/customer-progress-roadmap]], [[concepts/happy-customers]]

## Growth Rockets

After product/market fit begins to emerge, the book shifts to finding a **growth rocket**: a scalable, sustainable acquisition loop. It identifies three main growth-loop types:

- **Revenue growth loop** - reinvest money into growth
- **Retention growth loop** - use usage and engagement to generate more growth
- **Referral growth loop** - use happy customers to bring in more customers

A strong growth rocket has:

- a renewable propellant
- an efficient engine
- sustainability over time

The goal is to find one primary growth rocket and optimize it, rather than stacking too many growth hacks.

Related concepts: [[concepts/growth-loop]], [[concepts/revenue-loop]], [[concepts/referral-loop]], [[concepts/retention-loop]], [[concepts/growth-rocket]]

## Notable Examples Used Throughout

The book uses many examples to make the framework concrete:

- Steve vs. Larry as contrasting mindsets
- Starbucks, Facebook, and Airbnb as canvas archetypes
- Tesla as a staged rollout example
- Apple/iPhone/iPad as offer and pitch examples
- Home construction and architects as a validation case study
- Drill bits and 3M Command Strips as a jobs-to-be-done example
- Pepsodent and habit formation as a behavior design example

These examples reinforce the book's central claim: most successful products do not win by building faster; they win by **learning faster, targeting the right problem, and sequencing risks correctly**.

## Overall Contribution

This document is a practical operating system for early-stage innovation. It synthesizes ideas from Lean Startup, Design Thinking, Jobs-to-be-Done, Behavior Design, and Systems Thinking into a single iterative method for:

- clarifying the business model
- discovering real customer problems
- testing desirability, viability, and feasibility
- designing offers that cause a switch
- launching the smallest viable product
- building traction and eventual growth loops

Its deepest message is that startup success is not about having the perfect idea at the start. It is about **systematically discovering what works** before resources run out.
