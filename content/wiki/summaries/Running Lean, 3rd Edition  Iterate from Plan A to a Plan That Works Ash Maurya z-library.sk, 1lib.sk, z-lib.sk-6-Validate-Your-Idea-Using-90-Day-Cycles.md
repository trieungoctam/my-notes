# Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Summary

## Core contribution
This book argues that successful startups do not begin by building a product and hoping for adoption; they begin by modeling a business, testing the riskiest assumptions first, and iterating from Plan A to a plan that works.

The central framework is the **Continuous Innovation Framework**, which combines:
- **Business model design** with [[concepts/lean-canvas]]
- **Validation** through customer discovery and experiments
- **Growth planning** via [[concepts/traction-roadmap]]
- **Execution** through 90-day cycles and small bets

## Main ideas

### 1) The world has changed
Maurya contrasts an "old world" where products were hard to build and competition was limited with a "new world" where products are cheap to create, easy to copy, and crowded by many alternatives. In this environment:
- investors care about **traction**, not just ideas
- customers have many choices and quickly switch away from weak products
- technical skill alone is not enough to win

This shift makes [[concepts/traction]] and fast learning more important than polished planning.

### 2) Start with the business model, not the product
A key thesis is that **your business model is the product**. The first task is to deconstruct a big idea into assumptions using a one-page [[concepts/lean-canvas]]. The canvas helps founders clarify:
- customer segments
- problems
- unique value proposition
- solution
- channels
- revenue streams
- cost structure
- key metrics
- unfair advantage

The book strongly prefers a one-page model over a traditional long business plan, because early-stage assumptions are mostly unknown and must be tested, not defended.

### 3) Use the Lean(er) Canvas to find desirability
The desirability test focuses on whether customers actually want the idea. Maurya emphasizes:
- **start with problems before solutions**
- define early adopters narrowly
- anchor the UVP in the customer's most painful existing alternative
- avoid innovator bias, where founders invent problems to justify their solution

This part introduces the idea of the [[concepts/innovators-gift]]: new problems worth solving usually come from old solutions that break under a new switching trigger.

### 4) Use Fermi estimates to test viability
Instead of building a detailed financial forecast, the book recommends a back-of-the-envelope [[concepts/fermi-estimate]].

The purpose is to test whether the business model can plausibly reach a **minimum success criteria (MSC)** over a reasonable time horizon. Important principles include:
- use revenue, churn, conversion, and customer lifetime as the core variables
- focus on **leading indicators** rather than vanity metrics
- think in terms of **customer factory throughput**
- recognize that pricing is one of the most powerful levers

A recurring lesson is that many ideas fail not because they are impossible to build, but because they cannot reach enough economic scale.

### 5) Use traction roadmaps to test feasibility
Feasibility is about whether the idea can actually be built and rolled out in stages.

Maurya recommends replacing product roadmaps with a [[concepts/traction-roadmap]]:
- now: problem/solution fit
- next: product/market fit
- later: scale

The roadmap is based on the [[concepts/hockey-stick-growth]] idea: progress is slow at first, then accelerates after product/market fit.

### 6) Work in 90-day cycles
The book's operational backbone is the **90-day Model-Prioritize-Test cycle**. Each cycle has three phases:
1. **Model** - update the business model and align on goals, assumptions, and constraints
2. **Prioritize** - identify the current bottleneck or riskiest assumptions
3. **Test** - run many small experiments

This creates a cadence of external accountability and avoids the common startup trap of working on too many things at once.

## Important frameworks and metaphors

### Customer factory
A major conceptual model is the **customer factory**, which treats the business as a system that converts strangers into customers through:
- acquisition
- activation
- retention
- revenue
- referral

This model frames [[concepts/traction]] as throughput. It also shifts focus from output-based measures to customer behavior and conversion.

### Theory of Constraints
The book repeatedly draws on [[concepts/theory-of-constraints]]: at any moment, one constraint limits system throughput. The right move is not to optimize everything, but to find and fix the current bottleneck.

### Innovation as switching
Innovation is framed as causing customers to switch from an old way of doing a job to a better new one. This depends on four forces:
- **push** from dissatisfaction with the old way
- **pull** toward the new way
- **inertia** from habit
- **friction** from anxiety or effort

This is central to the book's [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]]-style thinking.

## Notable tactical guidance

- Build a Lean Canvas quickly; don't aim for perfection.
- Avoid groupthink by having team members sketch independently first.
- Keep the customer segment simple; specify early adopters precisely.
- Don't confuse features with outcomes.
- Don't defer pricing; it is part of the product.
- Use evidence-based decisions, not opinions.
- Prefer small bets and fast learning over big upfront commitments.
- Use control groups and time-boxed experiments.

## Cases and illustrations

The book uses many story-based examples to show the framework in practice:
- **Steve vs. Larry** in the introduction: two founders with similar backgrounds, but different mindsets and outcomes
- **Steve's AR/VR platform**: a build-first founder who must learn to narrow his market, reframe his value proposition, and test viability
- **Starbucks, Facebook, Airbnb**: examples of different business model archetypes
- **Tesla**: an example of stage-based rollout and a Wizard-of-Oz MVP
- **A food truck restaurant**: an example of testing desirability before scaling

These examples show that startup success is usually not a linear execution story; it is a sequence of discovery, reframing, and disciplined validation.

## Deep takeaway
The book's deepest message is that entrepreneurship is a **search process**, not an execution process. The job is not to prove the initial idea right, but to discover the smallest workable version of a business model, validate it quickly, and then scale it with discipline.

In short:
- **model first**
- **validate second**
- **scale last**
- and keep learning the whole time
