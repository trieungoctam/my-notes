# Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Summary

## Overview
Ash Maurya's *Running Lean* presents a systematic way to turn an initial idea into a validated business model before running out of resources. The book argues that early-stage entrepreneurship is not mainly a product-building problem, but a **business model design and validation problem**.

The core message is simple: **don't start by building a solution; start by deconstructing and testing the business model**. The process is organized as a Continuous Innovation Framework that combines ideas from [[concepts/lean-startup]], Design Thinking, [[concepts/business-model-design]], jobs-to-be-done, and systems thinking.

## Main Story and Argument
The introduction uses a contrast between two founders, Steve and Larry:

- **Steve** follows a build-first, investor-first path.
- **Larry** follows a traction-first, problem-first path.

Steve spends months building in isolation, pitching investors, and repeatedly missing the market. Larry instead starts with customer problems, models the business, tests riskiest assumptions first, and uses small experiments to build traction.

The book uses this comparison to argue that in today's environment:

- products are cheaper and easier to build,
- competition is much higher,
- customers have many alternatives,
- investors care more about traction than ideas,
- and success depends on learning fast.

## Core Framework: Continuous Innovation
The book's central framework is a three-part cycle:

1. **Model**
2. **Prioritize**
3. **Test**

These activities are repeated in **90-day cycles**. The goal is to search for a repeatable and scalable business model through rapid learning.

### 1) Model
The first step is to describe the business model clearly, usually with a [[concepts/lean-canvas]]. This is treated as a snapshot of assumptions, not a final plan.

### 2) Prioritize
Next, the team identifies the **riskiest assumptions** and decides what to validate first. The emphasis is on testing what is most uncertain, not what is easiest.

### 3) Test
Finally, the team runs small, fast experiments to generate evidence. This enables evidence-based decisions instead of guesswork.

## The Lean Canvas
A major tool introduced in the book is the **Lean Canvas**, a one-page version of a business model. It replaces long business plans with a quick, living model that can be updated as learning occurs.

The Lean Canvas covers:

- Customer Segments
- Problem
- Solution
- Unique Value Proposition
- Unfair Advantage
- Channels
- Revenue Streams
- Cost Structure
- Key Metrics
- Existing Alternatives
- High-Level Concept
- Early Adopters

### Why the Lean Canvas matters
The book emphasizes that:

- a business model should be captured on one page,
- it should be sketched quickly,
- blank boxes are acceptable at first,
- teams should avoid groupthink,
- and the canvas should be used as a living hypothesis map.

## Important Mindset Shifts
A major contribution of the book is its focus on mindset. Maurya argues that tools only work when paired with the right worldview.

### Key mindsets include:

- **Your business model is the product**
- **Love the problem, not your solution**
- **Traction is the goal**
- **Right action, right time**
- **Tackle your riskiest assumptions in stages**
- **Constraints are a gift**
- **Hold yourself externally accountable**
- **Place many small bets**
- **Make evidence-based decisions**
- **Breakthrough requires unexpected outcomes**

These mindsets push founders away from intuition-only thinking and toward systematic discovery.

## Problem First, Solution Later
The book strongly argues against locking onto a solution too early. Instead:

- identify customer problems first,
- study existing alternatives,
- narrow in on early adopters,
- and only then define the solution.

This is framed as a way to avoid the [[concepts/build-trap]], where teams keep adding features without ever finding market demand.

## Validation Before Building
One of the book's strongest claims is that you do **not** need a complete product to validate a business.

Instead of Build-Demo-Sell, the book promotes **Demo-Sell-Build**:

- define the offer,
- validate demand through customer conversations and sales,
- then build the minimum viable product.

This approach helps teams avoid overbuilding and ensures that the MVP is based on validated customer needs.

## Problem/Solution Fit
A central milestone in the book is **problem/solution fit**.

Maurya argues that an MVP should not be treated as the first thing to build; rather, it should be the smallest solution that creates, delivers, and captures customer value.

The process is:

1. Understand the problem
2. Define possible solutions
3. Craft an offer
4. Build the MVP

The offer is the point where assumptions meet reality.

## Traction and Product/Market Fit
The book distinguishes between:

- **traction** as evidence of a working business model,
- and **product/market fit** as the inflection point where growth accelerates.

Traction is not just product usage; it is proof that customers care enough to engage, pay, and return.

The author stresses that investors today fund **business models that work**, not just solutions or technology.

## Risk and Validation
The book argues that startup risk is no longer primarily technical. In many modern products, the biggest risks are:

- customer risk,
- market risk,
- viability risk.

That is why the book urges founders to test:

- **Desirability** - Do customers want this?
- **Viability** - Can we monetize this?
- **Feasibility** - Can we build this?

This three-part overlap is framed as the [[concepts/innovation-trinity]].

## Special Topics Covered in the Introduction and Chapter 1
### Business model archetypes
The book explains three major business model types:

- **Direct** - users are customers
- **Multisided** - users and customers are different groups
- **Marketplace** - buyers and sellers interact through the platform

These models often require separate canvases or separate perspectives.

### Focus on early adopters
The book warns against targeting everyone. Instead, founders should identify a narrow starting group and design for them first.

### Unfair advantage
The book distinguishes between weak advantages like features or hard work, and real unfair advantages such as:

- network effects,
- platform effects,
- community,
- existing customers,
- SEO ranking,
- insider information,
- expert endorsements,
- and personal authority.

A founder can begin with an unfair advantage story even if the advantage itself has not yet fully materialized.

## Structure of the Book
The book is organized into three parts:

### Part 1: Design
How to deconstruct an idea into a business model and test its desirability, viability, and feasibility.

### Part 2: Validation
How to run 90-day cycles, use customer interviews, and test a business model before building the full product.

### Part 3: Growth
How to launch in stages, build an MVP, and iterate toward product/market fit while focusing on what is riskiest.

## Practical Takeaways
- Start with the problem, not the solution.
- Treat the business model as the product.
- Use the Lean Canvas to make assumptions visible.
- Validate demand before building.
- Test the riskiest assumptions first.
- Work in small, fast cycles.
- Use customer interviews and small experiments to learn.
- Aim for traction, not just feature completion.
- Start narrow and expand only after proving repeatability.

## Overall Contribution
*Running Lean* offers a practical operating system for early-stage innovation. Its main contribution is a repeatable method for moving from vague idea to validated business model by combining business model design, customer discovery, staged validation, and disciplined learning.

It is especially useful for founders, product managers, and intrapreneurs who need to build something customers actually want under conditions of extreme uncertainty.
