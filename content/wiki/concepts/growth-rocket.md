# Growth Rocket

A **growth rocket** is the primary scalable growth loop that can keep producing customers after early validation. In [[Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-14-Find-Your-Growth-Rocket]], Maurya uses the rocket metaphor to distinguish short-lived launch tactics from a durable engine of repeatable acquisition.

## Core Idea

Maurya separates growth into two broad kinds of mechanisms:

- **Booster rockets**: short-term, high-touch, nonscalable tactics that help the product lift off
- **Growth rocket**: the main scalable channel or loop that can carry the product forward over time

A growth rocket is not just a channel. It is a **self-reinforcing system** with:

1. a **propellant** that fuels acquisition
2. an **engine** that converts that fuel into repeatable traction

The rocket only works if both parts are strong:

- the **engine** must be efficient
- the **propellant** must be renewable

That is why Maurya treats growth as a systems problem rather than a marketing hack.

Related concepts: [[traction]], [[customer-factory]], [[growth-loop]], [[happy-customer-loop]], [[90-day-cycle]], [[customer-forces]], [[product-market-fit]]

## Why the Concept Matters

Early-stage teams often rely on founder time, direct outreach, events, or referrals to get initial customers. Those tactics are useful, but they usually do not scale far enough to support long-term growth.

The growth rocket concept answers a harder question:

**What mechanism will keep producing customers once the early validation phase is over?**

That mechanism might be:

- revenue reinvested into performance marketing
- user-generated content that attracts new users
- referrals from happy customers
- a sales motion that becomes repeatable

The key is that the loop should **regenerate fuel** instead of burning it once.

> [!note]
> The book's broader argument is that growth is not luck or hype. It is a repeatable discipline grounded in [[traction]], systems thinking, and sustainable loops.

## How the Book Frames It

Maurya places growth rockets in a staged journey:

- **Part 1: Design** - define the mission and business model
- **Part 2: Validation** - prove desirability, viability, and feasibility
- **Part 3: Growth** - identify and optimize the scalable channel that can carry the product forward

So the growth rocket is **not** the starting point. It comes after the team has:

- discovered a meaningful problem
- designed an MVP
- validated problem/solution fit
- established happy customers
- seen enough repeatability to know what deserves scaling

This makes growth rocket selection a **systems problem**, not a guess.

## Booster Rockets vs. Growth Rockets

Booster rockets are useful because they help the startup get off the ground. But they are limited.

Typical booster rockets include:

- warm referrals
- direct outreach
- events
- PR
- founder-led selling

These are often powered by [[traction|founder sweat equity]] and are ideal for early learning. But they eventually burn out.

A growth rocket, by contrast, is designed to be more sustainable. It should be able to keep generating customers with less dependence on the founder's direct effort.

> [!info]
> The central shift is from **"How do we get initial lift?"** to **"What loop can keep producing lift repeatedly?"**

## Types of Growth Rockets

Maurya highlights three broad types of sustainable growth loops that can serve as growth rockets.

### 1. Revenue Growth Loop

Existing customer revenue is reinvested into new acquisition.

Examples:

- paid ads
- outbound sales
- inbound sales teams
- company-generated content funded by revenue

This loop must satisfy unit-economics constraints such as:

- **LTV > 3 × CAC**
- **CAC payback under 12 months**

### 2. Retention Growth Loop

Existing customers keep returning, generating derivative assets such as content or data that help attract more customers.

Examples:

- user-generated content
- reviews
- product data
- usage-driven network effects

This loop turns customer activity into future acquisition fuel. In [[Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-13-Make-Happy-Customers]], Maurya shows how retention supports the [[happy-customer-loop]] and lays the behavioral groundwork for sustainable growth.

### 3. Referral Growth Loop

Happy users or customers bring in new users.

Examples:

- word of mouth
- referral programs
- invites to teammates or friends

A product is truly viral only when referrals exceed replacement and the viral coefficient is above 1.

## Selecting a Primary Growth Rocket

Maurya recommends not trying to optimize every possible channel at once. Instead, teams should:

1. shortlist growth rocket candidates
2. validate the best candidate over a 90-day cycle
3. optimize the chosen rocket once it shows promise

The right candidate is the one that best matches:

- the business model
- the economics
- the product's natural behavior
- the team's current stage
- the customer factory's current bottleneck

In practice, that means asking:

- What renewable propellant do we have?
- What engine can turn it into repeatable traction?
- What does the math say?
- Can this loop sustain itself without constant founder intervention?

## 90-Day Cycles and Growth Rocket Testing

A key update from the later parts of the book is that growth rocket work is itself staged. The team keeps using [[90-day-cycle|90-day cycles]] to test and optimize growth candidates.

In Chapter 14, Maurya recommends:

- using some time to **shortlist** possible growth rockets
- validating the best candidate in a cycle
- then optimizing the winner after it proves sustainable

That means growth is not a single launch event. It is a sequence of experiments, each one trying to improve one engine or loop.

## Relationship to Product/Market Fit

The growth rocket becomes especially important once the startup approaches [[product-market-fit]]. Before that point, the main job is to learn and validate. After that point, the main job is to find the scalable engine that can carry the product through the next staircase jump in growth.

So the growth rocket is both:

- a **result of validation**
- a **precondition for scaling**

It is how a startup moves from proving value to multiplying value.

## In Practice

A team using this concept should:

- keep the customer factory running
- measure acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral
- identify which loop is most likely to scale sustainably
- run experiments inside 90-day cycles
- invest in the loop that shows repeatability and favorable economics

The later chapters reinforce that the growth rocket should emerge from a real happy-customer system, not from wishful thinking about scale.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A growth rocket is the main growth system that keeps bringing in customers again and again without the founder having to do everything manually.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> Think of a spacecraft launch. Booster rockets help the ship leave the ground, but the main engine is what keeps it moving over the long trip. A growth rocket is that main engine for a startup.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> - **Myth:** A growth rocket is just any channel that gets users.  
>   **Reality:** It has to be repeatable and sustainable, not just effective once.
> - **Myth:** You should pick a growth rocket before validating the product.  
>   **Reality:** The book says to validate demand first, then choose the scalable loop.
> - **Myth:** More channels always mean more growth.  
>   **Reality:** Too many channels can add complexity; usually one primary loop should lead.
> - **Myth:** Growth rockets are only about marketing.  
>   **Reality:** They can be powered by revenue, content/data, or referrals.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** What is a growth rocket?  
> **A:** A scalable acquisition engine that can keep producing customers over time.
>
> **Q:** How is it different from a booster rocket?  
> **A:** A booster rocket is short-term and helps with lift-off; a growth rocket is the long-term scalable engine.
>
> **Q:** Why does it need renewable propellant?  
> **A:** Because sustainable growth must regenerate fuel instead of burning it once.
>
> **Q:** What are the main types of growth rockets?  
> **A:** Revenue, retention, and referral loops.
>
> **Q:** When should a team focus on it?  
> **A:** Start testing candidates early, then double down once a loop looks sustainable and economic.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> It helps teams move from early-stage hustle to a growth system that can actually scale. Instead of chasing random channels, they identify the one loop that can keep producing traction and support the move beyond validation into repeatable growth.
