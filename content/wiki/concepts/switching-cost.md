# Switching Cost and Customer Commitment

Switching cost and customer commitment describe the practical effort, risk, and evidence required for a customer to move from an existing alternative to a new solution. In *Running Lean*, Ash Maurya frames this as a **switching problem**: customers do not buy a product in the abstract; they change behavior when the new option creates enough pull to overcome the inertia of the status quo and the friction of adopting something new. 

At the same time, the founder must lower uncertainty by getting real commitment from customers early-through interviews, preorders, pilots, letters of intent, or paid trials. The result is that switching cost is not only about what the customer gives up; it is also about how much proof the founder must create before the customer will say yes.

## Core idea

A customer switches only when the new way is compelling enough to beat the old way on both emotional and practical grounds.

Maurya's customer behavior model says a switch is driven by:

- **Push**: dissatisfaction with the current solution
- **Pull**: attraction to the new solution
- **Inertia**: comfort with the current solution
- **Friction**: anxiety and usability barriers around the new solution

When push + pull outweigh inertia + friction, a switch becomes possible. In that sense, switching cost is the hidden burden of change: time, money, effort, learning, and risk that make customers hesitate.

This connects directly to [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] and [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]].

## Customer commitment

Customer commitment is the measurable signal that a customer is willing to cross the gap from interest to action.

In the book, Maurya argues that founders should not wait for a finished product before seeking commitment. Instead, they should use offers and staged validation to secure commitments such as:

- advance payments
- preorders
- demo calls
- trials
- pilots
- letters of intent
- referrals to decision-makers

This is why the book emphasizes the [[concepts/mafia-offer]]: it is designed to be compelling enough that qualified early adopters feel the offer is worth the risk.

## Why this matters in Running Lean

Switching cost is central to the book's validation logic because a startup must prove that its idea is worth the customer's effort before building too much. If customers will not commit, the issue may not be the product's features-it may be that the promise is too weak, the timing is wrong, or the alternative is still good enough.

Maurya uses several examples to show this:

- A product can be **functionally better** but still lose if the change is too hard.
- Price matters because it shapes what customers are willing to switch for.
- Early adopters are the people most likely to switch because a trigger has already disrupted the status quo.
- The best proof of demand is not applause, but commitment.

This is tightly linked to [[concepts/traction-roadmap]], [[concepts/lean-canvas]], and [[concepts/mvp]].

## How the book reduces switching cost

The book recommends lowering uncertainty for the founder while creating enough urgency for the customer. It does this through several tactics:

### 1. Start with the right trigger
Find customers who recently experienced a switching trigger:

- a bad experience
- a change in circumstance
- new awareness of a problem or better solution

These people already have motivation to change.

### 2. Focus on the customer's existing alternative
The real comparison is not your product versus nothing; it is your product versus what customers already use. The stronger the dissatisfaction with the old way, the lower the effective switching cost.

### 3. Make the promise concrete
A good UVP should clearly explain what better outcome the customer gets and why it matters. The promise must be strong enough to justify the effort of switching.

### 4. Raise signup friction for serious buyers
Maurya recommends not trying to make the ask too easy. Instead, use scarcity and seriousness to filter for people with real motivation. In this framing, commitment is a feature, not a bug.

### 5. Use staged rollout
Start with a small number of carefully chosen early adopters. If the first few customers can switch, it is more plausible that a broader group will later switch too.

## Switching cost vs. commitment risk

These are related but not identical:

- **Switching cost** is the burden on the customer to change.
- **Customer commitment** is the evidence that the customer is willing to bear that burden.

A high switching cost can kill a good idea if the founder asks for too much too soon. But low commitment is also a warning sign: it suggests the problem may not be painful enough, the solution may not be compelling enough, or the offer may not be specific enough.

This is why the book prefers tangible actions over verbal enthusiasm. A customer who says "interesting" is not committed. A customer who signs up, pays, preorders, or agrees to a pilot is.

## Practical indicators of strong commitment

Look for these signs:

- customers volunteer time for interviews
- customers keep showing up to demos
- customers ask implementation questions
- customers introduce you to the buyer
- customers are willing to pay now, even if delivery is later
- customers tolerate an early-stage product because the promise is strong enough

These are stronger indicators than compliments or vague interest.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> People do not switch to a new product just because it exists. They switch when the old way stops working well enough and the new way feels worth the effort. In startups, the founder also has to get real proof that people care enough to take action, not just say nice things.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It is like leaving your regular grocery store for a new one. You only make the change if your current store is annoying enough-bad produce, long lines, higher prices-and the new store feels worth the extra trip. If the new store is only slightly better, you stay put.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> {'Myth': 'If customers like the idea, they will switch later.', 'Reality': 'Interest is not commitment; people often need a strong trigger and a concrete offer before they act.'}
> {'Myth': 'Switching cost only means price.', 'Reality': 'It also includes learning effort, anxiety, workflow disruption, and the risk of trying something new.'}
> {'Myth': 'Lowering signup friction always helps.', 'Reality': 'Sometimes raising commitment filters for the right early adopters and improves learning.'}
