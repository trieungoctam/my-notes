# Customer Progress Roadmap

A **Customer Progress Roadmap** is a staged sequence of smaller outcomes that helps a customer move from a triggering event to the full desired outcome without getting overwhelmed. In [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-13-Make-Happy-Customers]], Ash Maurya presents it as a way to reduce friction, guide activation, and turn a product into the customer's new status quo.

The idea becomes even more powerful in the later parts of the book: once customers are making progress, the roadmap is no longer just onboarding. It becomes part of a broader system for **retention**, **repeatable value delivery**, and eventually **growth** through reusable customer behavior loops. In other words, the roadmap is a bridge between [[concepts/customer-forces-model]], [[concepts/happy-customers]], and [[concepts/growth-loop]].

> [!note]
> The appendix broadens the lens further: a roadmap is also a way to respect the true cost of customer progress in time and attention. If a step wastes time, creates friction, or makes people think too much, it harms flow and reduces ROI.

## What it is

The roadmap breaks a large job-to-be-done into **intermediate summits**. Each summit is a meaningful step that the customer can reach quickly and successfully before moving to the next one. This is especially important after acquisition, when motivation is high but fragile and customers can easily fall back to the old way.

Maurya treats this as a behavioral scaffold: customers do not simply "adopt" a product once. They move through a sequence of smaller wins that gradually make the product feel like the **new status quo**. That progression matters because customers are not just learning a tool; they are changing behavior.

This idea is closely tied to [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] and [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]]: customers do not simply adopt a product once; they progress through a journey shaped by push, pull, inertia, and friction. A good roadmap helps them keep making progress.

The roadmap is also a form of [[concepts/flow]] design. It should reduce queues, batch sizes, interruptions, and unnecessary learning. If customers need to stop, search, decode, or mentally translate too much, the roadmap is not doing its job.

## Core purpose

The roadmap exists to:

- reduce anxiety about a big, unfamiliar goal
- create quick early wins
- reinforce the product's promise through repeated value delivery
- move customers from activation to retention
- make the product feel like the new status quo
- support the transition from one-off success to a repeatable habit loop
- protect customer time by removing unnecessary effort and context switching

In Maurya's framing, the roadmap is one of the best tools for making happy customers because it translates a broad promise into a practical path customers can follow.

## Key characteristics

### 1. It starts with the first aha moment

The first summit should be reachable in under 30 minutes when possible. This first success should feel meaningful, not trivial. The goal is to create an immediate sense that the product is helping the customer do something they care about.

In Chapter 13, this first win is explicitly linked to **activation**: once customers experience value quickly, they are more likely to return and continue using the product.

### 2. It uses smaller, sequential summits

Each step should build on the previous one. The roadmap should not ask customers to master everything at once. Instead, it should guide them through a sequence of smaller outcomes that progressively level up toward the larger job.

Maurya emphasizes that the big job should be broken into a series of achievable summits, each one reinforcing the next.

### 3. It favors doing over learning

Maurya warns against forcing customers into instruction-heavy onboarding. The roadmap should get people doing real work quickly, not reading manuals. A cheat sheet or quick-start path is better than a long tutorial.

### 4. It reduces choice and cognitive load

Too many options create friction. A good roadmap gives defaults, recommendations, and constraints so customers can move forward with confidence.

### 5. It aligns with habit formation

The roadmap is not just about one-time success. It is designed to help customers repeat behavior until the product becomes habitual. This connects directly to [[concepts/happy-customers]] and behavior design principles.

### 6. It respects the economics of time

The appendix makes a complementary point: time is the scarcest resource, so the roadmap should maximize ROI in time spent. That means prioritizing actions customers can complete quickly, minimizing wasted motion, and avoiding overengineering early steps.

### 7. It can evolve into a growth asset

In the growth sections of the book, Maurya shows that once customers are progressing reliably, the same structured journey can feed broader loops: better retention, more referrals, more content/data, and stronger word of mouth. That means the roadmap is not only an onboarding device; it can also help create the conditions for a sustainable [[concepts/growth-loop]].

## How it works in practice

Maurya recommends designing the roadmap by starting from the customer's desired outcome and working backward into smaller milestones.

Typical design guidelines include:

- identify the big outcome the customer wants
- decompose it into smaller steps that are independently valuable
- make the first step quick and low-risk
- progressively increase the difficulty or complexity of later steps
- remove unnecessary features until the customer is ready for them
- share the roadmap with customers so they can see how they will progress

This approach helps customer onboarding, retention, and product education all at once.

In the later chapters, this same logic also supports **product launch** and **customer success**: the team should keep the customer factory running, observe how customers progress through the roadmap, and use that feedback to improve the product and the journey.

The appendix adds a practical operating principle: design the roadmap around activities that are worth the customer's time. If a step is better done by a shortcut, default, automation, or guided prompt, use that. The roadmap should preserve flow, not create labor.

## Relationship to the happy customer loop

The customer progress roadmap is central to the loop from acquisition to activation to retention.

- **Acquisition** gets the customer in the door
- **Activation** delivers the first real win
- **Retention** happens when each repeated use reinforces the value promise
- **Referral** becomes more likely when customers see clear progress and can explain that progress to others

In this sense, the roadmap is not just onboarding. It is a behavioral scaffold for turning a product into a habit.

Maurya's later chapters make this even more explicit: to make happy customers, the team must not only deliver the first aha moment, but also help the customer climb through additional summits so the product becomes the customer's new status quo.

## Relationship to growth

The later growth chapters add an important twist: a customer progress roadmap is not just about keeping a current customer satisfied. It can also help define the product's **growth rocket**.

Once customers are consistently making progress, that progress can generate:

- more usage and retention
- more referrals
- user-generated content or data
- a repeatable story that can scale into broader adoption

That means the roadmap is also a strategic input into sustainable growth. It helps the team understand not only how to make a customer successful, but also how that success can become an engine for future acquisition and expansion.

> [!note]
> Maurya's later chapters slightly broaden the role of the roadmap. Earlier, it is mainly an onboarding and retention scaffold; later, it becomes part of a larger system for repeatability and growth. This is not a contradiction so much as an expansion of scope: the same staged progress path can support both customer success and scalable growth.

## Example from the source

In Chapter 13, Maurya describes how the Altverse team uses customer progress thinking to help architects and homeowners move through their design journey. Instead of expecting users to master the full product immediately, the team focuses on smaller milestones such as visualization, material selection, and eventually other jobs like price estimation.

This is a classic customer progress roadmap: the product is not one giant leap but a sequence of guided wins.

Later, the same logic helps the team keep customers engaged long enough for the product to become embedded in real workflows. The roadmap supports the move from initial value delivery to repeated use, case studies, and eventual word of mouth.

## Why it matters

A customer progress roadmap helps founders avoid a common mistake: assuming that customers will instantly understand, trust, and habitually use a product just because it is powerful. By designing smaller steps, teams can make the product feel easier, more rewarding, and more likely to stick.

It also helps product teams focus on **value delivery**, not just feature delivery. The question is not "what can we build next?" but "what step helps the customer make progress next?"

That makes the roadmap useful across the whole customer lifecycle:

- before product/market fit, it helps customers reach activation
- after product/market fit, it helps reinforce retention
- as the business scales, it helps turn customer success into a growth asset

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: It's just onboarding. Reality: it also supports retention, habit formation, and growth.
> Myth: More features make a better roadmap. Reality: too many choices create friction.
> Myth: Customers should read a manual first. Reality: the roadmap should get them doing useful work quickly.
> Myth: The roadmap ends after sign-up. Reality: it continues until the product becomes the new status quo.
> Myth: Faster means doing more. Reality: faster usually means less waste, less context switching, and fewer unnecessary steps.
