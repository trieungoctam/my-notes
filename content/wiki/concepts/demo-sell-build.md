# Demo-Sell-Build

Demo-Sell-Build is a [[concepts/traction-first]] product development approach where you **demonstrate the offer**, **sell the offer to early customers**, and only then **build the full solution**.

It is one of the clearest tactical expressions of [[concepts/traction-first]] and a direct reversal of the usual [[concepts/build-demo-sell]] mindset.

## What it means

Demo-Sell-Build treats the **offer** as the first thing to validate. Instead of assuming the product should be built first, the team uses customer conversations, sketches, mockups, videos, landing pages, pilots, preorders, concierge services, and other lightweight signals to test whether people want the idea enough to commit.

The sequence is:

1. **Demo** - Show the idea in a convincing, low-cost form.
2. **Sell** - Test whether customers will pay, pre-order, sign up, or otherwise commit.
3. **Build** - Invest in the actual product only after demand is proven.

This shifts the earliest risk check from **"Can we build it?"** to **"Should we build it?"**

In the newer validation material, this sequence is made even more explicit:

**understand problem → define solution → craft offer → build MVP**

So the offer is the bridge between the problem and the product, and the MVP is what you build after the offer has already shown pull.

## Why it exists

Ash Maurya presents Demo-Sell-Build as a response to the classic startup trap of spending too long perfecting a product before learning whether anyone wants it. In the book's story, Steve keeps trying to build first, while Mary keeps redirecting him toward customers, traction, and a compelling offer.

The key insight is that in early-stage uncertainty, the hardest problem is usually not technical feasibility but **customer demand**, **pricing**, and **business model viability**. In other words, the riskiest thing is often not the code; it is the assumption that people will care enough to switch.

> [!note]
> In [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]], Demo-Sell-Build is reinforced by the 90-day validation system: teams model their business, prioritize the riskiest assumptions, and test them in small experiments. The offer is not the end goal; it is the first practical proof that the model has pull.

## How it works in practice

Demo-Sell-Build usually starts with a narrow version of the idea:

- sketch a [[concepts/lean-canvas]] or [[concepts/leaner-canvas]]
- identify the target [[concepts/customer-segments]] and [[concepts/early-adopters]]
- define the problem in terms of existing alternatives
- create a compelling offer or "mafia offer"
- use demos, interviews, landing pages, pilots, preorders, or concierge delivery to see whether people will buy in
- build only after enough evidence of demand exists

The offer is the bridge between the problem and the product. It tests whether the solution is compelling enough to cause a switch, even before the full product exists.

Maurya's validation flow makes this sequence even clearer: **understand problem → define solution → craft offer → build MVP**. The offer is the point where the idea meets reality; the MVP comes later, after the offer has shown evidence of pull.

In the 90-day cycle model, this fits naturally into the [[concepts/model-prioritize-test]] loop:

- **Model** the business and identify the riskiest assumptions
- **Prioritize** the assumptions worth testing first
- **Test** them with small, fast experiments

That means Demo-Sell-Build is not just a launch tactic; it is a way to organize early validation around the current constraint.

## What makes it different from Build-Demo-Sell

Build-Demo-Sell assumes the product should exist first, after which the team can show it to customers and try to sell it.

Demo-Sell-Build reverses that logic:

- **Build-Demo-Sell** starts with implementation.
- **Demo-Sell-Build** starts with customer learning.

That difference matters because building is expensive, while selling a promise is cheap. If the promise fails, the team learns early and cheaply instead of burning time and money on a product nobody wants.

## Relationship to other concepts

Demo-Sell-Build connects directly to several other ideas in the book:

- [[concepts/innovators-gift]] - sell against an old solution's weaknesses
- [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] - use push, pull, inertia, and friction to understand why someone would switch
- [[concepts/problem-solution-fit]] - validate that the solution is compelling enough to create demand
- [[concepts/customer-factory]] - think about traction as a system of acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral
- [[concepts/fermi-estimate]] - test whether the economics can work before scaling
- [[concepts/mvp]] - build the smallest solution only after the offer has been validated
- [[concepts/90-day-cycles]] - use a regular cadence of modeling, prioritizing, and testing to avoid overcommitting too soon
- [[concepts/problem-discovery]] - uncover the real customer story before attempting to pitch or build
- [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] - anchor the offer in the job customers are trying to get done

## Practical benefits

Demo-Sell-Build helps teams:

- avoid overbuilding
- learn what customers value before committing resources
- narrow down to the right early adopters
- improve pricing and positioning sooner
- reduce the risk of launching something nobody wants
- de-risk the transition from idea to MVP
- uncover problems worth solving before solution design

It is especially useful when the market is uncertain, the idea is new, or the team is tempted to confuse enthusiasm for validation.

## Limits and cautions

Demo-Sell-Build is not a trick for faking progress. The demo must be tied to a real customer problem and a believable offer.

A weak demo that excites nobody is not evidence of a good idea. The point is not to manipulate customers, but to learn whether the business model has enough pull to justify building.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: Demo-Sell-Build means you can skip building altogether. Reality: You still build, but only after demand is validated and you know what to build.
> 
> Myth: A demo is the same as a prototype. Reality: A demo can be a sketch, script, mockup, video, concierge service, or live walkthrough; its job is to validate interest, not prove full functionality.
> 
> Myth: Selling first is dishonest. Reality: The goal is to test a real offer honestly, not to trick customers; the offer should reflect a genuine path to value.
> 
> Myth: The MVP comes first. Reality: In this framework, the MVP is the later built expression of a validated offer.
> 
> Myth: Validation is a one-time pitch. Reality: In a [[concepts/90-day-cycles]] model, validation is continuous and repeated as assumptions shift.
