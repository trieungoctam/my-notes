# Customer Forces Model

The **Customer Forces Model** explains why customers switch from one solution to another, why they sometimes **stay** with the old way, and why they may **resist** adopting a new solution even when it seems better. In [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] terms, it describes the behavioral forces that shape how people **hire** a solution to get a job done.

The model is especially useful in [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] because it does not ask only, "Do customers have a problem?" It asks the more practical question: **Are customers motivated enough to leave the old way and adopt a new one?**

> [!note]
> The newer material in *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* makes the model more operational. The same switching logic now informs not only desirability testing, but also problem discovery interviews, customer story pitches, mafia offers, 90-day reviews, launch planning, post-launch behavior design, and growth-rocket strategy.

At a high level, switching happens when the forces toward change outweigh the forces holding people in place:

> **PUSH + PULL > INERTIA + FRICTION**

This makes the model valuable for product strategy, because it helps founders understand not only whether customers have a problem, but whether they are actually motivated to leave the current way of doing things.

## The Four Forces

### 1. Push
**Push** is dissatisfaction with the current situation. It comes from a **switching trigger** or an **expectation violation** that makes the old way feel no longer good enough.

Common pushes include:
- a bad experience
- a change in circumstances
- a new awareness of a better way or a worse downside to the current way

Push is the pain that starts the search for change.

### 2. Pull
**Pull** is the promise of the new solution. It is the attractive force that draws customers toward change because the new option seems better, easier, faster, safer, or more rewarding.

Pull is strongest when the new solution promises a better **desired outcome**, not just a better feature.

### 3. Inertia
**Inertia** is the comfort of the familiar. Even when the old way is imperfect, people often stay with it because it is habitual, known, and already integrated into their routines.

The old way can keep winning simply because it is already the status quo.

### 4. Friction
**Friction** is the resistance to adopting something new. It includes anxiety, learning cost, setup effort, switching cost, uncertainty, and fear of failure.

Friction is why a product can be logically better but still fail to cause a switch.

## Why the Model Matters

Maurya uses the Customer Forces Model to reframe innovation away from "building a better solution" and toward **causing a switch**. Customers do not compare your product to a blank slate; they compare it against the current workaround, habit, incumbent, or mental default.

This is why a strong [[concepts/innovators-gift]] depends on understanding the old way first. New products gain traction when they are positioned against the problems caused by existing alternatives.

The model also underpins the book's customer story pitch: the pitch should name the **switching trigger**, raise the **stakes**, break the **old way**, and show the **better new way**. In other words, the model is not only for research-it is also the backbone of a compelling offer.

## Connection to Desirability Testing

In the book, the Customer Forces Model is used to stress test the **desirability** of an idea. The key questions become:
- What is the customer currently doing?
- What event or trigger would make them reconsider?
- What problems do they experience with the old way?
- What promise makes the new way compelling enough to overcome resistance?

This means that desirability is not just about whether customers like your solution. It is about whether their current situation has become painful enough, and your new option compelling enough, to motivate a real switch.

## Connection to Customer Discovery Interviews

The later chapters turn the model into a practical interview discipline.

Instead of asking people to list "their problems" directly, Maurya recommends:
- starting with an **existing alternative**
- anchoring the conversation around the **last time the job was done**
- reconstructing the timeline from **first thought** to **switching trigger** to **selection** to **first use** to **repeat use**
- capturing the story in a [[concepts/customer-forces-canvas]]

The canvas organizes the story into:
- **Push**: triggering events, desired outcomes, and what's at stake
- **Pull**: consideration set, chosen solution, UVP, channel, and price
- **Inertia**: existing solution and habits
- **Friction**: anxieties and usability issues
- **What's next**: actual outcome and next summit

This extension matters because it makes the Customer Forces Model operational. It is no longer just a theory of switching; it becomes a way to turn interviews into structured evidence.

## Connection to Customer Story Pitches and Mafia Offers

The model also underpins the book's customer story pitch and [[concepts/mafia-offer]] approach.

A strong offer pitch follows the same logic as the Customer Forces Model:
- name the bigger context and the switching trigger
- raise the stakes of staying with the old way
- expose the weaknesses of the old solution
- present the new solution as a better way to achieve the desired outcome
- ask for a concrete next step

This is why the model is not only useful for research. It also helps craft an offer customers cannot refuse because it is anchored in a real switching story rather than in feature claims.

## Connection to 90-Day Cycles

The 90-day cycle review chapter extends the model into execution. Teams do not just collect stories; they use the resulting evidence to decide what to do next.

In practice, the model feeds into cycle review by helping the team answer:
- Did we uncover enough evidence of push and friction?
- Did our offer create enough pull?
- Did the current constraints shift?
- Do we persevere, pivot, or pause?

That makes the Customer Forces Model part of the operating system of [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], not just a research tool.

## Connection to Launch, Happy Customers, and Growth Rockets

The later chapters extend the model beyond acquisition into activation, retention, and growth.

Once the MVP is ready, the same switching logic helps teams manage rollout in batches and keep the customer factory running. Instead of launching broadly, Maurya recommends soft-launching to carefully selected early adopters, then iterating until customers repeatedly get value from the product.

Here, the Customer Forces Model helps identify:
- who is most motivated to switch
- which old way they are leaving behind
- what promise should be emphasized in the early-access pitch
- which objections or anxieties need to be reduced before full launch

In the happy-customer stage, the model shifts toward behavior design:
- activation creates the first successful experience
- retention reinforces the new behavior
- repeated progress reduces the temptation to return to the old way
- the product becomes the habit-forming new default

And in the growth stage, the same logic explains why sustainable growth loops work. Retention and referral create new customer force dynamics: happy customers generate content, data, money, or referrals that feed the next round of acquisition and reinforce the new status quo.

## Practical Implications for Founders

The model implies several practical rules:
- Start with the customer's existing alternative, not your solution.
- Look for switching triggers rather than abstract market interest.
- Make the new solution feel meaningfully better, not merely slightly better.
- Reduce friction as much as possible.
- Do not confuse willingness to talk with willingness to switch.
- Use interviews to uncover real switching stories, then group them into [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]]-based segments.
- Use the resulting story not only to validate the problem, but also to shape the pitch and offer.
- Feed those findings into 90-day cycle reviews and evidence-based decisions.
- Keep the customer factory running during launch so the business can learn and improve continuously.
- Design onboarding and early use so the customer gets to an aha moment quickly and the new behavior starts to stick.
- After product/market fit, keep optimizing the happy customer loop and look for sustainable [[concepts/growth-loop]]s.
- Treat time as your scarcest resource and spend it on the highest-ROI actions first, as emphasized in the appendix bonus material.

For early-stage products, this often means validating the problem in the language of the old way, then designing the solution around a concrete reason to change.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> People switch when the old way hurts enough and the new way feels clearly better.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It is like finally replacing a leaky umbrella: being soaked pushes you away from the old one, a better umbrella pulls you toward it, but the new umbrella still has to feel easy enough to use.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> - **Myth:** If customers like your idea, they will switch.  
>   **Reality:** Liking an idea is not enough; inertia and friction can still keep them with the old solution.
> - **Myth:** A better product always wins.  
>   **Reality:** A product can be better and still lose if the switching trigger is weak or adoption feels risky.
> - **Myth:** Competition only means direct competitors.  
>   **Reality:** People also compare you against habits, workarounds, and other existing alternatives.
> - **Myth:** Surveys are enough for discovery.  
>   **Reality:** Interviews and timeline reconstruction are better for uncovering the real switching story.
> - **Myth:** The model is only for research.  
>   **Reality:** It also informs pitches, offers, segmentation, validation cycles, launch decisions, retention, and growth loops.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** What does PUSH mean?  
> **A:** Dissatisfaction or pain from the current situation, often caused by a switching trigger or expectation violation.
>
> **Q:** What does PULL mean?  
> **A:** The attraction of the new solution and the promise of a better outcome.
>
> **Q:** What are INERTIA and FRICTION?  
> **A:** Inertia is the comfort of the familiar; friction is the anxiety, effort, and uncertainty of adopting something new.
>
> **Q:** Why is the model useful in startups?  
> **A:** It helps founders tell whether customers are truly motivated to switch, not just whether they have a problem.
>
> **Q:** How does the model connect to growth?  
> **A:** After activation, the same behavioral logic helps turn retention and referrals into sustainable growth loops.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> The model helps teams identify the real reasons people change behavior, which improves desirability testing, customer discovery, offer design, segmentation, validation cycles, launch planning, post-launch retention, and growth strategy. It turns vague interest into actionable evidence about why a customer will actually switch and how to keep that momentum through early access, repeated use, and sustainable growth loops. It also reminds founders to spend time on the highest-ROI learning actions instead of overbuilding or optimizing too early.
