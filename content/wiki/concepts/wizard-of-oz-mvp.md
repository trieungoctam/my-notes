# Wizard-of-Oz MVP

A **Wizard-of-Oz MVP** is a validation pattern where the product appears to work like a finished system, but much of the work behind the scenes is done manually or through existing tools rather than full automation. The goal is to **test customer demand, value, and willingness to commit quickly** without building the entire solution upfront. It is a [[concepts/lean-canvas]]-friendly way to lower risk in the early stages of [[concepts/continuous-innovation]].

## Core idea

The concept appears in *Running Lean* as part of a broader shift away from build-first thinking and toward staged validation. Rather than fully implementing a product before testing the market, founders can **simulate the experience** with a combination of manual labor, existing software, licensed components, concierge service, preorders, pilots, or other improvised means.

The key is not deception for its own sake. The point is to **learn what customers truly value before investing in expensive automation**. In the book's logic, the most important question is not "Can we build it?" but "Should we build it?"

A Wizard-of-Oz MVP is especially useful when:
- the full product would take months or years to build,
- the biggest uncertainty is demand rather than technical feasibility,
- the team needs evidence before making a large commitment,
- and the product can be staged as part of a [[concepts/now-next-later]] rollout.

In other words, the pattern helps answer: **Do customers want this enough to take action now?**

## How it works

A Wizard-of-Oz MVP usually has three traits:

1. **Visible promise** - the customer sees a real, compelling experience.
2. **Hidden manual work** - people, scripts, or existing systems fulfill parts of the service behind the scenes.
3. **Fast learning loop** - the team measures whether the offer causes a switch, creates engagement, and leads to commitment.

In *Running Lean*, this fits the broader **Demo-Sell-Build** approach: first validate the offer, then build the product after demand is clearer. The Wizard-of-Oz pattern reduces scope so the team can focus on the riskiest assumptions first rather than on infrastructure that may not matter yet.

The pattern also fits the book's emphasis on [[concepts/90-day-cycles]]: use a lightweight stage now, a more complete product next, and scale later only after the model has evidence.

## Why it matters

The biggest value of a Wizard-of-Oz MVP is that it lets a founder **test the hardest assumptions first**. Instead of spending months building features nobody wants, the team can learn whether the market response justifies building at all.

It helps de-risk:
- [[concepts/desirability]] - do customers care?
- [[concepts/traction]] - can you get early signals of demand?
- [[concepts/customer-factory]] - can you get prospects to move through acquisition, activation, and revenue steps?
- [[concepts/feasibility]] - can the eventual build be staged intelligently?

The pattern also reinforces the book's warning against premature optimization: do not scale the wrong thing too early. Start with the riskiest unknowns, validate them with the smallest possible solution, and only then invest in full automation.

> [!note]
> The book's examples frame the pattern as "fake it until you're ready to make it," but the intent is disciplined validation, not misrepresentation. The customer experience should be realistic enough to test the hypothesis and gather evidence.

## In Running Lean

Maurya uses Tesla as a strong example of the pattern. Tesla did not start by fully building a mass-market electric car. Instead, it:
- used a **licensed existing car chassis**,
- focused on the riskiest unknowns, especially the battery,
- and staged the rollout so it could learn before scaling.

That is the Wizard-of-Oz logic in practice: **fake the easy parts, validate the hard parts, and only then build the full product**.

The validation section broadens this idea beyond a one-off prototype. It shows how teams can use manual or semi-manual substitutes, preorders, concierge delivery, pilots, and trials inside **90-day cycles** to learn quickly and to build confidence in the business model before committing to a full implementation.

The later customer discovery material adds an important refinement: Wizard-of-Oz validation works best **after** you understand real customer jobs, triggers, and existing alternatives. The book's discovery process shows that teams should first run problem discovery interviews, identify switching triggers, and learn the customer's bigger context before designing the offer. In other words, the pattern is most powerful when it is built on [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] insights and customer-forces evidence, not on guesses.

The cycle review chapter adds a final accountability loop: even a manual MVP should be judged against traction, learning, and the next 3P decision. If the staged approach is working, the team can persevere into the next cycle; if not, the evidence may call for a pivot or pause.

In the broader [[concepts/customer-factory]] view, a Wizard-of-Oz MVP is often a way to simulate just enough of the customer journey to measure acquisition, activation, and early revenue before the product is fully built.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A Wizard-of-Oz MVP is a fake-it-first test: it looks like a real product, but people do some work behind the scenes so you can learn what customers want before building everything.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like a restaurant that opens with a tiny menu and a chef cooking some dishes by hand to see what people order before buying all the kitchen machines.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: It's dishonest. Reality: It's a transparent learning method used to test demand before automation.
> Myth: You need a fully built product to validate. Reality: You can simulate the hardest parts manually first.
> Myth: It only works for software. Reality: It can be used for services, hardware, and hybrid offers.
> Myth: It replaces customer discovery. Reality: It works best after you understand the customer's jobs, triggers, and alternatives.
