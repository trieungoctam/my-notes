# Validation Recipes

Validation recipes are repeatable ways to test a startup idea before committing to full product build-out. In *Running Lean*, they are the practical bridge between [[concepts/lean-canvas|business model design]] and real-world proof: you use them to uncover whether a problem is worth solving, whether your solution can cause a switch, and whether customers will actually commit.

Validation recipes are not one single tactic. They are a family of patterns you apply depending on the riskiest assumption and the stage of the idea. The book treats them as ways to move from **Plan A** to evidence, using small, fast, customer-facing experiments instead of guessing.

## Core Idea

A validation recipe is a structured way to learn with customers while minimizing waste. The most important principle is:

> **Do the smallest thing that can teach you the most.**

This is why Ash Maurya emphasizes that the early stages of innovation should not begin with a full MVP. Instead, they should begin with a recipe matched to the current risk.

## The Main Validation Recipes

### 1. Problem Discovery Interviews
Used to uncover real customer problems, switching triggers, current behaviors, and the deeper job-to-be-done. This is the discovery foundation for everything else.

- Focus on how people use [[concepts/customer-forces|existing alternatives]]
- Look for friction, workarounds, pet peeves, and unmet outcomes
- Avoid pitching your solution too early

This recipe is especially important because many founders start with solution bias and ask the wrong questions. Maurya argues that discovery conversations reveal whether you have a problem worth solving at all.

### 2. Lean(er) Canvas / Business Model Stress Tests
A validation recipe can also be a thought experiment. Maurya uses the [[concepts/lean-canvas|Lean Canvas]] and its narrower variant, the Lean(er) Canvas, as a way to stress test desirability, viability, and feasibility before building.

This is where you test:
- who the customer is
- what problem matters most
- what the promise is
- whether the economics can work

### 3. Fermi Estimate
This is a quick viability check using rough math. It asks whether the model can plausibly hit the minimum success criteria.

The Fermi estimate is not meant to be precise; it is meant to expose whether the business model works on paper. If it fails, you revise pricing, segment, or the goal.

### 4. Mafia Offer
A mafia offer is a validation recipe for problem/solution fit. It is an offer customers cannot refuse because it is compelling, specific, and grounded in real customer pain.

The process usually involves:
- understanding the problem deeply
- defining the solution
- delivering the offer one-on-one

The offer combines a [[concepts/mafia-offer|unique value proposition]], a demo, and a clear call-to-action. It is designed to secure tangible commitment before building a full product.

### 5. Concierge MVP
A Concierge MVP is a validation recipe where you manually deliver the product/service yourself before automating it. The customer experiences value, but the back end is partly or fully human-powered.

This is useful when:
- the product is too expensive to build fully first
- the team wants to learn quickly
- the desired outcome can be delivered in a high-touch way

The book uses this recipe to show how a product can be "real" to the customer even if it is not yet scalable.

### 6. Wizard-of-Oz MVP
A Wizard-of-Oz MVP hides manual work behind the scenes while making the product look automated to the user.

This recipe is useful when:
- the technical system is expensive or slow to build
- you want to test demand and experience first
- you can cobble together existing tools to simulate the final product

Tesla is used as a major example: it validated demand before fully building all parts of the car from scratch.

### 7. Foot-in-the-Door MVP
This recipe delivers the smallest useful outcome needed to get inside the customer's workflow or world.

It works well when the full product would be too broad or too ambitious, and when the first step can create a meaningful foothold that later expands into a larger relationship.

### 8. Offer Delivery Campaigns
Maurya treats offer delivery itself as a validation recipe. The goal is not just to pitch, but to test whether the customer accepts the promise and will take the next step.

Common offer forms include:
- smoke tests
- landing pages
- webinars
- preorders
- crowdfunding
- direct sales
- mafia offers

These are not just marketing tactics; they are structured learning tools.

## Why Validation Recipes Matter

Validation recipes help teams avoid the two biggest startup traps:

1. **Building too much too soon**
2. **Validating the wrong thing**

Instead of saying "build an MVP," Maurya asks: *which recipe best reduces the current risk?* That means validation is not one tactic but a sequence of decisions based on the current stage:

- discover the real problem
- test whether the business model can work
- prove customers will commit
- only then build and scale

This makes validation recipes tightly linked to [[concepts/continuous-innovation|continuous innovation]] and [[concepts/traction|traction]], because each recipe is meant to produce evidence, not assumptions.

## Relationship to Other Concepts

Validation recipes connect directly to:
- [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done|jobs-to-be-done]] - because good recipes start with the customer's real job
- [[concepts/customer-forces|customer forces]] - because switching is driven by push, pull, inertia, and friction
- [[concepts/customer-factory|customer factory]] - because recipes ultimately need to produce measurable customer movement
- [[concepts/90-day-cycles|90-day cycles]] - because recipes are run inside short, accountable cycles
- [[concepts/mafia-offer|mafia offer]] - the strongest demand-validation recipe in the book
- [[concepts/lean-canvas|Lean Canvas]] - the model used to identify what should be tested

## Sources & Perspectives

### Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Part II Validation / Chapters 9-12
Maurya presents validation recipes as practical patterns for testing the riskiest assumptions in sequence, starting with customer discovery and then moving to offer design and MVP delivery. His perspective is strongly anti-buildup-first: validate demand and switching behavior before investing heavily in product automation. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-15-Epilogue]], pp. 229-237, 346-367, 368-401, 424-439]

> [!tip]- ELI5
Validation recipes are different ways to check if people really want your idea before you spend months building it. Sometimes you talk to customers, sometimes you show a fake-but-believable version, sometimes you do the work by hand, and sometimes you ask people to pay or commit first.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
Think of opening a restaurant. Before leasing a huge building and hiring a full staff, you might test demand with a pop-up dinner, a food truck, or a pre-order event. Each option helps you learn in a cheaper, faster way.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
- **Myth:** An MVP always means a small software product. **Reality:** In this book, the best MVP may be a service, a manual process, or a staged offer.
- **Myth:** Validation means asking people if they like the idea. **Reality:** Validation means observing commitment, behavior, and evidence.
- **Myth:** One validation tactic fits every startup. **Reality:** The right recipe depends on the current risk: desirability, viability, or feasibility.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
- **Q:** Why does Maurya prefer validation recipes over building a complete product first? **A:** Because recipes let you test the riskiest assumptions with less time, money, and effort.
- **Q:** What makes a mafia offer different from a normal pitch? **A:** It is designed to uncover real pain and secure commitment by combining a compelling promise, demo, and clear call-to-action.
- **Q:** When is a Concierge MVP especially useful? **A:** When you can deliver customer value manually first and automate later.
- **Q:** What is the main purpose of a Wizard-of-Oz MVP? **A:** To simulate the product experience and test demand before building everything from scratch.
- **Q:** What should determine which validation recipe you use? **A:** The riskiest current assumption and the stage of the business model.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
Validation recipes help founders learn before they build, which reduces waste and prevents costly false starts. They are especially important because the earliest stage of a startup is mostly about uncertainty, not execution.
