# Model-Prioritize-Test Cycle

The **Model-Prioritize-Test Cycle** is Ash Maurya's recurring operating loop for turning an early idea into a business model that can survive uncertainty. Rather than building first and explaining later, teams repeatedly **model** their business, **prioritize** the riskiest assumptions, and **test** those assumptions with fast, small experiments.

It is a core mechanism of [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] and works alongside [[concepts/lean-canvas]], [[concepts/traction-roadmap]], and [[concepts/customer-factory]] to move an idea from Plan A toward a plan that works. In the later 90-day-cycle chapters, the loop becomes a full operating rhythm with kickoff, sprint work, and cycle review, making the framework not just a theory of learning but a practical cadence for team execution.

## What the cycle does

The cycle answers three questions in order:

1. **Model** - What is the business model hypothesis?
2. **Prioritize** - What is riskiest right now?
3. **Test** - What evidence can we gather quickly?

The point is not to prove everything at once. The point is to reduce the biggest uncertainty first and keep learning in short, disciplined loops.

## How Maurya defines each step

### 1. Model

Modeling means deconstructing the idea into a business model using a [[concepts/lean-canvas]]. Maurya treats the business model itself as the product: a startup is not just building a solution, but a system for creating, delivering, and capturing value.

This stage is about clarity. The team writes down assumptions about:

- customer segments
- problems
- existing alternatives
- unique value proposition
- solution
- channels
- revenue streams
- cost structure
- key metrics
- unfair advantage

Maurya emphasizes that the first model should be a snapshot, not a perfect plan. In the 90-day-cycle material, modeling is also the moment when the team aligns around goals, assumptions, and constraints before any meaningful test begins. That same idea carries into the review chapter, where the canvas is updated to reflect what actually changed.

### 2. Prioritize

Prioritization means identifying which assumptions are most risky and most worth testing first. The cycle is designed to focus on what could break the business model, not what is easiest to build.

In the book, this is tied to the idea that modern startups face more **customer and market risk** than technical risk. So the team should ask:

- What assumption would kill this idea if it's false?
- What is the riskiest part of the model right now?
- Which uncertainty must be reduced before more effort is spent?

This step prevents teams from falling into the [[concepts/build-trap]] by polishing features before confirming demand. In the 90-day-cycle chapter, Maurya sharpens this idea further by using **Theory of Constraints** logic: there is usually one limiting constraint at a time, and the team should focus on that constraint rather than spreading effort across many unrelated tasks.

The review chapter adds an important discipline: the constraint can shift as soon as the first one is broken, so teams must re-evaluate regularly instead of assuming the same bottleneck will remain forever.

### 3. Test

Testing means running fast, small experiments to gather evidence. Maurya recommends many small bets instead of one big one. The goal is to validate the model incrementally and adjust based on results.

In the book, testing includes:

- customer interviews
- offers before product
- demo-sell-build sequencing
- small iterative experiments
- evidence-based decisions

Testing is not just about whether something works technically. It is about whether the business model is desirable, viable, and feasible.

The 90-day-cycle material adds an important refinement: testing should happen inside a disciplined cadence of **modeling, prioritizing, and testing**, repeated every 90 days, with weekly check-ins and sprint-level experiments inside the cycle. That makes testing both faster and more accountable. The review chapter then closes the loop by requiring the team to inspect the results, compare them to the original roadmap, and decide whether to persevere, pivot, or pause.

## Why the cycle matters

The Model-Prioritize-Test Cycle matters because early-stage uncertainty is too high for traditional planning methods. A long plan can create the illusion of certainty, but it does not reduce risk the way evidence does.

Maurya contrasts this cycle with linear execution. In his view, startups succeed by learning faster than competitors and by continuously adapting the model as conditions change.

The 90-day-cycle chapter adds another layer: the cycle is not just a philosophy, but a **rhythm** for the team. The point is to create external accountability, keep assumptions visible, and make sure the team is always working on the current bottleneck.

The review chapter shows the payoff of that rhythm: teams can compare their original assumptions with what they actually learned, then use the gap between them to make a deliberate 3P decision.

> [!note]- Related tension with older startup advice
> Maurya's 90-day-cycle framing is more explicit than looser "build-measure-learn" advice because it adds cadence, shared artifacts, and decision gates. The core learning loop stays the same, but the operational discipline is stronger.

## How it connects to the broader framework

The cycle is used repeatedly throughout the book as part of the [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] framework. It appears after the initial Lean Canvas stage and supports the progression through:

- [[concepts/problem-first-thinking]]
- [[concepts/innovator-s-bias]] reduction
- [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] and switching triggers
- [[concepts/fermi-estimate]] viability checks
- traction-building through customer acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral
- recurring 90-day planning, sprint, and review rituals

In other words, the cycle is not a one-time checklist. It is a recurring operating rhythm for startup learning.

## 90-day-cycle layer

The later chapters expand Model-Prioritize-Test into a practical cadence:

- **90-day cycle kickoff**: align the team around goals, assumptions, and constraints
- **90-day planning**: generate campaign proposals and choose the best bets
- **2-week sprints**: run experiments quickly and collect evidence
- **cycle review**: compare actual progress to the roadmap and decide whether to persevere, pivot, or pause

This turns the cycle into an operating system rather than a one-off planning tool.

It also reframes execution: the team is not simply building features, but running campaigns to reduce the current constraint in the business model.

The review chapter adds that the team must bring updated artifacts into the meeting:

- an updated elevator pitch
- a refreshed Lean Canvas
- a current traction roadmap
- evidence from campaigns and experiments

That makes the cycle legible to the team and to external stakeholders.

## Review and the 3P decision

A key addition from the review chapter is that the cycle only truly completes when the team stops and reflects. Maurya uses the 90-day review to force a decision:

- **Persevere** if traction is on track
- **Pivot** if learning suggests a better path
- **Pause** if the model is a dead end or resources are exhausted

This closes the feedback loop and prevents teams from drifting into endless experimentation without interpretation.

The review chapter also reinforces the idea that teams should:

- update the Lean Canvas with new evidence,
- snapshot the old version for comparison,
- overlay actual traction on the roadmap,
- and discuss the next constraint, not just the last one.

## Practical takeaway

If you are working on a new product, the best next action is usually not "build more." It is:

1. write down the current model,
2. identify the most dangerous assumption,
3. run the smallest useful test.

Repeat inside a 90-day cadence so the team stays focused on the current bottleneck and does not drift back into guesswork.

The final review step matters just as much as the test step: learning only becomes valuable when it changes the next decision.

## Sources & Perspectives

### *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - pages 11-49, Introduction
Maurya presents the Model-Prioritize-Test Cycle as the heartbeat of Continuous Innovation. His perspective is strongly operational: the cycle is how teams de-risk a business model, avoid the build trap, and learn what customers actually want before committing heavy resources.

### *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - pages 50-58, Part I. Design
In the design section, the cycle starts with modeling through the Lean Canvas. The emphasis is on turning a vague idea into a structured set of assumptions that can be stress-tested rather than merely imagined.

### *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - pages 133-172, Chapter 3: Stress Test Your Idea for Viability
Here the cycle becomes more quantitative and decision-oriented. Maurya shows how prioritizing and testing should focus on the riskiest viability assumptions first, using quick estimates and customer-factory metrics to decide whether the model can realistically reach a minimum success target.

### *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - pages 229-237, Part II. Validation
The validation section shows the cycle in practice: teams use recurring metrics and sprint results to diagnose constraints, learn from experiments, and keep the operating loop alive across the product lifecycle.

### *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - pages 238-269, Chapter 6: Validate Your Idea Using 90-Day Cycles
Maurya formalizes the cycle as a recurring 90-day operating cadence: model the business, prioritize the current constraint, and test through time-boxed campaigns and sprints. This perspective adds explicit external accountability and makes the cycle the basis for ongoing validation.

### *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - pages 402-423, Chapter 11: Run a 90-Day Cycle Review
The review chapter completes the loop. Maurya adds the 3P decision, updated artifacts, and traction overlay so the team can compare assumptions to evidence and decide whether to persevere, pivot, or pause.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> Write down your idea, find the riskiest part, test it fast, and repeat in 90-day cycles. At the end, pause and decide whether to keep going, change direction, or stop.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> Like tuning a race car in pit stops: you check the car, fix the biggest problem first, test again, and at the end of each stint decide whether to continue, change strategy, or pull out.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> **Myth:** It's a one-time planning process. **Reality:** It repeats as you learn.
>
> **Myth:** Prioritize what's easiest. **Reality:** Prioritize what could break the model.
>
> **Myth:** Testing means only building features. **Reality:** Testing can include interviews, offers, pricing, commitments, and experiments before building much at all.
>
> **Myth:** The 90-day cycle is just project management. **Reality:** It is a learning cadence with explicit review and decision points.
>
> **Myth:** Review is optional. **Reality:** Without review, learning doesn't become a decision.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** Why does the cycle start with modeling instead of building?
> **A:** Because early ideas are assumptions, and writing them down makes them visible and testable before spending time and money.
>
> **Q:** What should be prioritized first?
> **A:** The riskiest assumption that could most damage the business model if wrong.
>
> **Q:** What is the purpose of testing?
> **A:** To gather evidence quickly and reduce uncertainty with small experiments.
>
> **Q:** What does the 90-day review add that the cycle itself doesn't?
> **A:** It forces comparison, accountability, and a decision: persevere, pivot, or pause.
>
> **Q:** Why is the business model treated as the product?
> **A:** Because the startup succeeds or fails based on whether the whole system creates value, not just whether the solution is technically impressive.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> This concept keeps teams from wasting months on the wrong idea. In practice, it helps founders and product teams learn faster, spend less, and make evidence-based decisions under uncertainty. The 90-day-cycle version makes that learning system repeatable, visible, and team-driven instead of ad hoc. It also creates a clean handoff from learning to decision-making, so progress becomes measurable rather than hopeful.
