# Bigger Context

The **bigger context** is the wider real-life situation in which a customer decides whether to hire a product. It includes the customer's **job-to-be-done**, desired outcome, switching trigger, current workaround, and existing alternatives-not just the product's category or feature set.

Ash Maurya's key distinction is that **features and benefits live in the solution context**, while **desired outcomes live in the bigger context**. In other words, a product is only meaningful if it helps someone make progress in the broader situation they actually care about. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk]], pp.108-132] [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]], pp.229-237] [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp.300-339]

This idea is closely tied to [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]], [[concepts/switching-triggers]], [[concepts/desirability-testing]], [[concepts/innovators-bias]], and [[concepts/customer-forces-model]]. It is also a practical way to improve [[concepts/unique-value-proposition|unique value proposition]] writing.

## What the Bigger Context Means

A product is never the whole story. Customers use products to make progress in a larger situation:

- they want to get a job done
- they want a better result
- they want less effort, less risk, or more confidence
- they want to switch away from an existing alternative

That means the right question is not simply, "What does this product do?" but "What is this product for?"

Examples:

- A coffee shop is not just selling coffee; it may be helping a person stay alert, meet someone, or create a ritual.
- Fire is not just warmth; it may help with cooking, protection, and survival.
- An AR/VR app builder is not just about AR/VR; it may help a developer sell more billable work, a retailer sell more furniture, or an architect help clients visualize a home.

This broader frame is the bigger context.

## Why It Matters

Maurya emphasizes that customers often switch solutions not because a new product is technically superior, but because it solves an old problem in a more compelling way. The bigger context helps explain **why a switch happens**.

A product can be:

- technically impressive but irrelevant,
- functionally useful but not compelling enough to trigger a switch,
- or emotionally better because it creates a better outcome in the customer's world.

By focusing on the bigger context, founders can craft a more compelling [[concepts/unique-value-proposition]] and avoid inventing problems that only make sense inside their own solution.

> [!note]
> The validation material reinforces the same idea from a different angle: when activation, retention, or referrals are weak, the issue is often not just a product UX problem-it is a mismatch between the product and the customer's broader job, expectations, or trigger for change. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]], pp.229-237]

## Features vs. Outcomes

One of the most important lessons in the book is the difference between:

- **features and benefits** - what the product does
- **desired outcomes** - what the customer is trying to accomplish

The product lives in the feature/benefit layer. The bigger context lives in the outcome layer.

That distinction changes how founders think about positioning:

- "We help you create a business plan faster" is functional.
- "We help you create a business plan that gets read" is broader and more outcome-driven.

In practice, stronger opportunities usually come from the outcome side, because that is where customer motivation really lives.

## Relationship to the Customer Forces Model

The bigger context helps explain why some changes feel worth making and others do not. A new solution must create enough pull to overcome:

- the comfort of the old way
- inertia
- friction
- uncertainty

A customer usually does not switch just because a product exists. They switch because, in the bigger context, the current solution no longer feels good enough for the job they need done.

This is why the bigger context works together with [[concepts/customer-forces-model]]: it clarifies what the customer is trying to achieve and what kind of change would make switching feel worthwhile.

## Interviewing for the Bigger Context

The newer problem-discovery material deepens this concept by showing how to uncover the bigger context through interviews. Instead of asking customers to list problems directly, the book recommends tracing:

- the **triggering event** that started the search
- the **existing alternative** they chose
- the **friction** and workarounds in their story
- the **desired outcome** at the end of the journey

This is where the bigger context becomes practical. It is not just a theory about outcomes; it is a way to listen for the story behind the story.

For example, when the homebuilder interviewed recent buyers, the real story was not "I wanted a bigger house" in the abstract. The triggering event was often a holiday gathering, a growing family, or a job change. Those events revealed the job to be done and the specific outcome the customer was trying to reach.

Similarly, the drill-bit example shows that the bigger context is not the hole; it is what the hole enables, such as hanging art, decorating a home, or expressing identity. The farther you move into the bigger context, the more meaningful the opportunity can become-up to the point where you have gone too broad.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> - Bigger context does **not** mean "broader market only." It means the broader outcome and job behind a real customer decision.
> - Bigger context does **not** replace product features. It helps you choose the right features by grounding them in what customers actually care about.
> - Bigger context does **not** mean asking customers to imagine the future. It means reconstructing real past behavior, triggers, and workarounds.

## Scoping the Bigger Context

Maurya's guidance is to keep widening the frame until you reach a context that is both meaningful and actionable.

A useful rule of thumb is:

1. Start with the immediate functional result.
2. Ask what comes after that.
3. Keep going until the answers start to feel out of scope.

This is why a drill-bit maker should not stop at "a quarter-inch hole." For homeowners, the bigger context may be hanging a painting, decorating a room, or expressing personal taste. For construction workers, the bigger context may be speed, precision, and job completion. Different contexts imply different products, markets, and value propositions.

## Implications for Founders

Thinking in terms of the bigger context leads to several practical habits:

1. **Start with the customer's job, not your idea**
   - Ask what they are trying to accomplish in real life.

2. **Describe alternatives broadly**
   - Your competition may be email, spreadsheets, a manual workflow, a habit, or doing nothing.

3. **Frame problems around current behavior**
   - What is broken about the old way?

4. **Test for switching triggers**
   - What changed that makes the customer open to something new?

5. **Position outcomes, not just features**
   - Show why the new way matters in the customer's life or work.

In validation work, this mindset helps teams interpret weak activation or retention not as random noise, but as evidence that the product has not yet reached the customer's bigger context strongly enough.

## Sources & Perspectives

### Running Lean, 3rd Edition - pages 108-132, Chapter 2: Stress Test Your Idea for Desirability
Maurya presents the bigger context as the customer's broader job-to-be-done and desired outcome beyond the product itself. He argues that features only matter when they help customers switch from an existing alternative to a better way of getting the job done.

### Running Lean, 3rd Edition - pages 108-132, Chapter 2: Stress Test Your Idea for Desirability
The chapter contrasts solution context with bigger context and shows how founders should focus on outcomes, switching triggers, and old solutions. Its perspective is that durable desirability comes from understanding the customer's real-life progress, not from optimizing product features in isolation.

### Running Lean, 3rd Edition - pages 229-237, Part II: Validation
The validation section shows that customer behavior metrics like activation are meaningful only when the product fits the customer's actual job and context. Weak early engagement often signals that the solution has not yet been framed around the customer's true outcome or switching trigger.

### Running Lean, 3rd Edition - pages 300-339, Chapter 8: Understand Your Customers Better Than They Do
Problem discovery interviews reveal the bigger context by tracing the timeline of a customer's decision, from triggering event to chosen alternative to actual outcome. This chapter turns the concept into a practical interviewing method and emphasizes that the customer story must be reconstructed from real behavior, not guessed from the solution.
