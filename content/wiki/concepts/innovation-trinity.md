# Innovation Trinity

The **Innovation Trinity** is a practical decision filter for early-stage ideas: move forward only when the idea is simultaneously **desirable**, **viable**, and **feasible**.

- **Desirable** - customers want it and will switch for it
- **Viable** - the business can make money from it
- **Feasible** - the team can build and deliver it

In [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-1-Deconstruct-Your-Idea-on-a-Lean-Canvas]], [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], and [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]], Ash Maurya presents this overlap as the core risk-balancing lens for early innovation. The book argues that founders should not rush straight into building. Instead, they should first stress test their [[concepts/lean-canvas]] and their assumptions about customer demand, monetization, and technical execution.

Chapter 2 sharpens **desirability** by showing that it is not just "do people like the idea?" but "can this idea cause a switch away from an existing alternative?" That means desirability must be understood through customer segments, early adopters, current behavior, switching triggers, and the problems created by old solutions.

Chapter 3 sharpens **viability** by showing that it is not a vague "can this make money?" question. It can be checked with concrete traction math, a [[concepts/customer-factory-blueprint]], a minimum success target, and a quick [[concepts/fermi-estimate]] instead of a long financial forecast.

Chapter 4 extends the same logic into **feasibility**. Rather than assuming a product roadmap is the right planning tool, Maurya recommends a [[concepts/traction-roadmap]] and stage-based rollout planning. The key question becomes not just "Can we build this?" but "Can we build and deliver the right thing at the right time, in the right sequence?"

Chapter 5 adds a communication layer: the trinity is not only a validation filter, but also a pitch framework. A good business-model pitch should surface the same three tests in audience-specific form: why now, what's broken, what the fix is, how it makes money, how it will scale, and how it will be delivered. That makes the trinity both an internal decision tool and an external storytelling tool. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp. 210-228]

Chapter 6 adds an operational layer: the trinity is not a one-time evaluation, but something that must be revisited inside a regular [[concepts/90-day-cycles]] cadence. Using [[concepts/theory-of-constraints]] thinking, teams should identify the current bottleneck and run campaigns that test the riskiest assumptions tied to desirability, viability, or feasibility. In practice, the trinity becomes part of the cycle of modeling, prioritizing, and testing rather than a static go/no-go diagram. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-6-Validate-Your-Idea-Using-90-Day-Cycles]], pp. 238-269]

## Core idea

The Innovation Trinity is a decision rule for early-stage uncertainty. An idea can succeed in one dimension and still fail overall:

- A product may be **desirable** but not **viable** if customers like it but will not pay enough.
- It may be **feasible** but not **desirable** if it can be built but nobody wants it.
- It may be **viable** but not **feasible** if the business case works on paper but the team cannot deliver it.

Only the overlap of all three creates a strong foundation for [[concepts/product-market-fit]] and sustained growth.

Maurya's Chapter 3 sharpens the meaning of **viable**: it is not simply "can we make money someday?" but "can the customer factory actually produce enough customers, retention, and revenue to hit the minimum success criteria?" That makes the trinity a decision rule, not just a diagram.

Maurya's Chapter 4 sharpens the meaning of **feasible**: feasibility is not just about whether the product can be built in the abstract. It is about whether the team can de-risk delivery through a stage-based rollout, focus on the current bottleneck, and avoid premature optimization. In other words, feasibility is about right action, right time.

Maurya's Chapter 5 sharpens the meaning of **communicable** use of the trinity: founders must be able to explain all three dimensions clearly to different audiences, especially advisors, customers, and investors. The trinity becomes not only a filter for internal judgment, but also a structure for external buy-in.

Chapter 6 adds a further refinement: even after the idea is modeled and pitched, the trinity stays alive inside the operating cadence. Each 90-day cycle should revisit which dimension is weakest right now and use experiments to break that constraint.

> [!note]- New emphasis from Chapter 9
> Chapter 9 makes the trinity even more actionable by showing that the MVP is not just a small product, but the **smallest solution that causes a switch**. A good MVP must simultaneously satisfy desirability, viability, and feasibility; otherwise it may be small, but it will not move customers away from their existing alternative.

## Why it matters in Running Lean

Maurya uses the Innovation Trinity to explain why the early stages of a startup should be treated as a learning problem, not a building problem. Before a team spends months on product development, it should ask:

1. **Do customers want this?**
2. **Can we monetize this?**
3. **Can we build this?**

These questions map directly to the book's three-phase approach to continuous innovation:

- **Model** the idea clearly
- **Prioritize** the riskiest assumptions
- **Test** them with fast experiments

Chapter 2 shows that desirability is not just "do people like the idea?" but "can this idea cause a switch away from an existing alternative?" Chapter 3 shows that viability is not just "does the spreadsheet look good?" but "can the customer factory reach the throughput needed for the business to survive and grow?" Chapter 4 shows that feasibility is not just "can we engineer it?" but "what is the smallest, safest, most time-appropriate path to delivery?" Chapter 5 shows that the same three questions also structure the story you tell others. Chapter 6 shows that the trinity must be revisited within a cadence of cycles, because the current bottleneck changes as learning accumulates.

Chapter 8 adds an important refinement: the desirability check should start with real customer behavior, not with a list of guessed problems. The best way to validate desirability is to study how people use existing alternatives, what triggers them to switch, what job they are really trying to do, and what outcome they are actually chasing.

Chapter 9 adds a final refinement: once you have a big enough problem worth solving, your job is to design the smallest solution that actually causes a switch. That means the trinity is not only a filter for rejecting weak ideas; it is also a design constraint for shaping the MVP.

> [!warning]- Common confusion
> The trinity is not a sequence where you finish one box and move on forever. In practice, the three dimensions inform each other. Pricing affects desirability, customer acquisition affects viability, and technical constraints can reshape both the offer and the market. Chapter 5 adds another tension: how you frame the story for different audiences can affect what feedback you get. Chapter 6 adds a systems warning: the current bottleneck changes over time, so what was riskiest in one cycle may not be riskiest in the next. Chapter 8 adds that even "desirable" must be grounded in real switching behavior, not surface opinions. Chapter 9 adds that an MVP which does not actually cause a switch is too small in the wrong way.

## Relationship to the Lean Canvas

The [[concepts/lean-canvas]] is the tool used to surface the assumptions that feed the Innovation Trinity.

- The **Problem**, **Customer Segments**, and **Unique Value Proposition** boxes mainly inform **desirability**.
- The **Revenue Streams**, **Cost Structure**, **Key Metrics**, and **Customer Factory** assumptions help test **viability**.
- The **Solution**, **Channels**, and **Unfair Advantage** boxes relate strongly to **feasibility** and execution constraints.
- The **Business Model Pitch** translates the same underlying model into a form that different audiences can understand.

In that sense, the Innovation Trinity is the evaluation lens, while the Lean Canvas is the model being evaluated.

Chapter 2 adds an important refinement: the desirability check should begin with the **customer segment**, then the **early adopter**, then the **existing alternative**, and only then the **problem** and **UVP**. This keeps the team grounded in the real market instead of inventing problems to fit a favorite solution.

Chapter 3 adds a parallel refinement for viability: instead of starting with a top-down financial forecast, founders should estimate a target throughput goal, then work backward through customer acquisition, retention, activation, revenue, and referral.

Chapter 4 adds a parallel refinement for feasibility: instead of pretending the product roadmap can be perfectly specified up front, founders should build a now-next-later rollout that prioritizes the riskiest unknowns at each stage.

Chapter 5 adds a parallel refinement for communication: instead of forcing one pitch on everyone, founders should use worldview-based framing for investors, customers, and advisors.

Chapter 6 adds a parallel refinement for execution: instead of treating the initial model as final, founders should work in 90-day cycles, select campaigns based on the current constraint, and use experiments to continuously update the model.

Chapter 8 further sharpens the desirability side of the canvas by showing that the problem box should be populated from discovery interviews, not from solution bias. The key evidence comes from stories about switching triggers, workarounds, and friction in existing alternatives.

Chapter 9 adds the final product-design layer: once the problem is real and the model can work, the MVP itself must be shaped to cause a switch, not merely to exist.

## Desirability through switching behavior

The second source deepens the desirability side of the trinity using the [[concepts/innovators-gift]] and jobs-to-be-done thinking.

### What changes the question
Instead of asking:
- "What problem can my solution solve?"

Ask:
- "What job are customers trying to get done?"
- "What old solution are they currently using?"
- "What switching trigger makes them search for a new way?"

This matters because customers usually tolerate old solutions until a trigger creates an **expectation violation**. At that point, they become open to a better alternative. Maurya frames the change forces as:

- **Push** - dissatisfaction with the current way
- **Pull** - promise of the new way
- **Inertia** - comfort with the old way
- **Friction** - anxiety about adopting the new way

A switch happens when:

**PUSH + PULL > INERTIA + FRICTION**

This makes the Innovation Trinity more actionable: desirability is not just preference, but the ability to cause a switch from an existing alternative to a better one.

## Viability through traction and the customer factory

Chapter 3 expands the Innovation Trinity by treating viability as a measurable throughput problem. Maurya defines traction as the rate at which a business model captures monetizable value from customers. To reason about traction, he introduces the [[concepts/customer-factory-blueprint]]:

1. Acquisition
2. Activation
3. Retention
4. Revenue
5. Referral

These are leading indicators of whether the business can reach its minimum success criteria.

Instead of trusting large financial forecasts, the book recommends a Fermi-style estimate:

- Set a **minimum success criteria** (often framed as ARR over three years)
- Estimate the number of **active customers** required
- Estimate **churn** from customer lifetime
- Estimate the **minimum acquisition rate** required to replace churn
- Estimate the number of **leads** needed to produce that acquisition
- Estimate whether **referrals** reduce the burden enough to matter

This makes viability concrete. A product is not viable simply because the math looks impressive; it is viable only if the input assumptions can support the needed customer throughput.

> [!note]- Why Chapter 3 matters
> A business can be attractive in a story, attractive in a demo, and even attractive in a feature list, but still fail the viability test. The customer-factory math forces the founder to ask whether the model can actually scale to the desired outcome.

## Feasibility through staged delivery

Chapter 4 makes the feasibility side of the trinity more practical by shifting from product-roadmap thinking to traction-roadmap thinking. Maurya argues that product roadmaps assume too much certainty too early. Instead, founders should use a stage-based rollout that respects uncertainty and focuses on the most important risk first.

The chapter's core message is that the team should not start by trying to optimize for scale. At the earliest stage, the key risk is usually demand-side, not supply-side. That means the founder should first prove that the product can get attention and create value for early adopters before building out the full solution.

### Now-next-later rollout
Maurya's **now-next-later** plan maps the startup journey to three stages:

- **Now** - problem/solution fit
- **Next** - product/market fit
- **Later** - scale

This sequencing matters because each stage has different dominant risks:

- **Now:** customer understanding, value proposition, and evidence of demand
- **Next:** repeatable delivery, product iteration, and feedback loops
- **Later:** scaling channels, optimization, and growth engines

The book warns against [[concepts/premature-optimization]] such as hiring for scale before demand is validated or raising money before traction exists.

### Wizard-of-Oz and staged MVPs
Chapter 4 also introduces validation patterns such as [[concepts/wizard-of-oz-mvp]], where teams cobble together existing solutions to simulate the final product and validate demand before fully engineering the system. The point is not deception for its own sake; it is scope reduction. By doing less of the hard technical work early, teams can test the riskiest assumptions sooner.

This makes feasibility a matter of sequencing and scope, not just engineering capacity.

> [!note]- Feasibility is not just buildability
> The feasibility chapter broadens "can we build this?" into "can we build the smallest credible version, in the right sequence, while preserving learning speed?" That is why traction roadmaps and staged MVPs matter.

## Communication through worldview-based pitching

Chapter 5 shows that the Innovation Trinity is also a communication framework. The same underlying model must be translated differently depending on the audience.

### Investor worldview
Investors care most about:
- traction,
- market size,
- margins,
- and defensibility.

### Customer worldview
Customers care most about:
- the problem,
- the promise,
- and the price.

### Advisor worldview
Advisors care most about:
- clarity,
- learning,
- feedback,
- and the quality of your thinking.

This adds an important dimension to the trinity: if you cannot explain desirability, viability, and feasibility clearly, you may not get the feedback or buy-in needed to continue.

Maurya's 10-slide pitch structure mirrors the trinity:

- **Desirability**: why now, what's at stake, what's broken, the fix
- **Viability**: moat, revenue, key milestones
- **Feasibility**: rollout plan, team, ask

So the trinity becomes both a decision tool and a narrative spine.

## The 90-day-cycle version of the trinity

Chapter 6 turns the trinity into a recurring operating system. Rather than treating desirability, viability, and feasibility as one-time checks, Maurya places them inside a 90-day cadence driven by [[concepts/theory-of-constraints]].

The logic is:

- each system has a current bottleneck
- the bottleneck changes as the system improves
- the team should focus mostly on the current constraint
- the team should revisit assumptions, goals, and metrics every cycle

This means the trinity is not just a product evaluation diagram. It becomes part of a loop:

1. **Model** the business
2. **Prioritize** the riskiest constraint
3. **Test** it with small experiments
4. **Review** what changed
5. Repeat

In this framing, desirability is tested through discovery, viability through traction metrics, and feasibility through execution experiments. The trinity is thus embedded in an experiment-driven management system.

## Chapter 8: desirability from real customer stories

Chapter 8 makes the desirability side more rigorous by showing how to uncover customer problems without asking customers to invent them for you. The best interviews are not surveys or focus groups; they are structured conversations about how people actually use existing alternatives.

That matters because desirability is not merely a matter of opinion. It comes from a real switching story involving:

- a triggering event,
- an existing alternative,
- push and pull forces,
- friction and inertia,
- and a desired outcome.

This is where the trinity becomes more concrete. If the team cannot explain why someone would switch, then the idea is not yet desirable enough.

## Chapter 9: the MVP must cause a switch

Chapter 9 sharpens the trinity with a solution-design rule: the MVP should be the smallest thing that causes a switch. That means the product must be small enough to build quickly, but meaningful enough to displace an old solution.

Maurya's solution design guidance makes the trinity visible in product form:

- **Desirability**: solve the smallest subset of problems that still creates a compelling promise
- **Viability**: make sure the price and model work
- **Feasibility**: make sure the team can deliver within the time constraint

The chapter also highlights several MVP patterns that fit this logic:

- [[concepts/concierge-mvp]] - deliver the service manually first
- [[concepts/wizard-of-oz-mvp]] - fake the hard parts until they are worth automating
- [[concepts/foot-in-the-door-mvp]] - start with the smallest entry point into the customer's world

This is the most product-specific expression of the trinity: a successful MVP is not just minimal; it is minimal **and** switch-causing.

## Practical implications

Using the Innovation Trinity changes how teams work:

- They avoid spending too long on assumptions that are merely interesting.
- They test the riskiest unknowns first.
- They define an MVP only after understanding the problem and the business model.
- They use evidence, not optimism, to decide whether to continue, pivot, or stop.
- They frame desirability around customer jobs, triggers, and alternatives rather than feature lists.
- They frame viability around throughput, pricing, retention, churn, and acquisition rather than vague growth hopes.
- They frame feasibility around staged rollout, scope control, and the right action at the right time rather than an all-at-once build plan.
- They frame communication around audience worldview rather than one-size-fits-all pitching.
- They revisit all of the above inside a 90-day cycle so the current bottleneck never gets ignored.
- They design the MVP to cause an actual switch, not just to exist.

This is especially important in the early stage, where false confidence can lead to the [[concepts/build-trap]]: building more and learning less.

## How the book uses it in practice

Maurya's examples show that the trinity should be used as a sequence of questions:

- Start with **who** the customer is.
- Identify **what they are using today**.
- Understand **what changed** that makes them look for something new.
- Then define the problem in terms of the breakdown of the old solution.
- Craft a UVP that promises a better way to get the job done.
- Then test whether the resulting business can actually reach the needed throughput and revenue goals.
- Then determine how to deliver the solution in the right stage with the right level of investment.
- Then communicate the story in a way that fits the audience's worldview.
- Then repeat inside a 90-day cycle so the model keeps learning.
- Then design the MVP so it is not only buildable, but switch-causing.

This makes the trinity a bridge between strategy, validation, delivery, communication, and product design. It helps teams avoid category-first thinking and instead anchor their ideas in a real switching story, with a real path to commercial scale.

> [!note]- Possible tension across chapters
> Chapter 2 emphasizes that desirability should be defined through the customer's existing alternative and switching trigger, not just through solution features. Chapter 3 adds that even strong desirability is insufficient unless the business model can sustain the customer factory math. Chapter 4 adds that even a viable idea should be delivered in stages, not fully built upfront. Chapter 5 adds that the same model must be pitched differently to investors, customers, and advisors. Chapter 6 adds that the current bottleneck must be re-evaluated every 90 days. Chapter 8 adds that desirability must be discovered from customer stories rather than invented from the solution outward. Chapter 9 adds that an MVP must actually cause a switch, not merely be small. These are not contradictions; they are successive filters and translation layers.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> An idea is worth pursuing only if people want it, the business can make money from it, and the team can actually build it. The trick is that the product also has to be small enough and smart enough to make people switch from what they already use.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like opening a restaurant: people must want the food, the price must pay the bills, the kitchen has to work, and the first menu has to be simple enough that the restaurant can open quickly and attract customers away from other places.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: If people like the idea, it will succeed. Reality: it also has to be profitable and buildable.
> Myth: A strong spreadsheet proves viability. Reality: Maurya recommends simple traction math and customer-factory assumptions instead.
> Myth: Feasibility means building the whole product upfront. Reality: feasibility is about the smallest credible delivery path and the right sequence of actions.
> Myth: One pitch should work for everyone. Reality: investors, customers, and advisors care about different parts of the story.
> Myth: You only need to check the trinity once. Reality: Chapter 6 treats it as a recurring discipline inside 90-day cycles.
> Myth: Desirability can be validated by asking people what they want. Reality: Chapter 8 shows that you need to uncover switching behavior, existing alternatives, and desired outcomes through discovery.
> Myth: A small MVP is automatically good enough. Reality: Chapter 9 says the MVP must cause a switch, not just be minimal.
