# Behavior Design

Behavior design is the practice of shaping product experiences so customers are more likely to take the desired action, repeat it, and eventually form a habit. In *Running Lean*, it is the bridge between a product that merely gets adopted and a product that becomes the customer's new status quo.

The core idea is simple: customers do not just need a good product; they need a sequence of experiences that makes progress feel easy, rewarding, and worth repeating. Maurya connects this to [[concepts/customer-factory]], [[concepts/happy-customers]], and [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] by showing that retention depends on reducing friction and reinforcing progress after acquisition.

## What Behavior Design Does

Behavior design helps teams:

- turn first-time use into repeat use
- reduce anxiety and resistance during adoption
- create small, achievable progress steps
- reinforce the product's promise with immediate rewards
- build habits that keep customers from switching back to the old way

In the source text, this is framed as the work of making happy customers rather than simply making customers feel good. The aim is not free perks or superficial satisfaction, but real outcomes and sustained use.

## The Behavioral Models Behind It

Maurya links behavior design to two related models:

### 1. The habit loop
A habit is described as a repeating loop of:

- a cue or trigger
- a routine or action
- a reward

This loop matters because a product becomes sticky when customers repeatedly receive a reward that makes the action feel worth repeating.

### 2. The Fogg Behavior Model
The book also cites BJ Fogg's idea that behavior happens when three things converge:

- motivation
- ability
- prompt

This maps closely to product design: if a customer is motivated, can easily perform the action, and gets prompted at the right time, behavior is much more likely to occur.

## Behavior Design in Running Lean

In *Running Lean*, behavior design is not presented as a separate psychology theory detached from startups. It is used to improve the **happy customer loop**:

1. **Acquisition** brings the user in.
2. **Activation** gives the user the first meaningful value experience, the "aha moment."
3. **Retention** keeps the user coming back.
4. **Revenue** follows from repeated value delivery.
5. **Referral** can emerge once the customer trusts the product.

The key insight is that activation alone is not enough. Customers may sign up, but if they do not quickly experience value, inertia and friction pull them back to the old way. Behavior design helps structure the product so that the new behavior is easier, more rewarding, and more likely to stick.

## Progress Roadmaps and Small Wins

A major behavior design tactic in the book is breaking a large goal into smaller, staged summits. Instead of expecting users to jump from nothing to mastery, the product should guide them through incremental progress.

This means:

- delivering the first aha moment quickly
- giving customers simple defaults and clear next steps
- limiting choice until the customer is ready
- offering prompts and reminders at the right time
- reinforcing each small win with meaningful feedback

Maurya extends this idea into a **customer progress roadmap**: each summit should represent a smaller desired outcome that reinforces the main value proposition and helps the customer keep moving.

## Friction, Anxiety, and Habit Change

Maurya emphasizes that when customers switch to a new product, they are not just learning something new; they are leaving behind a familiar routine. That creates resistance.

Behavior design therefore focuses on reducing:

- anxiety about the new way
- friction during early use
- confusion from too many options
- cognitive load from unclear next steps

At the same time, it needs to account for the strength of old habits. Even if a product is objectively better, customers may keep using the old way unless the new experience is clearly easier, more rewarding, or more socially reinforced.

> [!note]
> The epilogue reinforces a broader point: the goal is not just to make products usable, but to help customers repeatedly progress toward a desired outcome until the product becomes their default way of getting the job done. That makes behavior design a product strategy concern, not only a UX concern.

## Relation to Product Strategy

Behavior design is not just a UX concern. In *Running Lean*, it is a product strategy concern because it affects:

- retention
- churn
- monetization
- referrals
- product/market fit

A product that does not support new behavior will struggle to become the customer's default choice. For that reason, behavior design is part of making the business model work, not just making the interface pleasant.

The epilogue sharpens this point by arguing that the real measure of progress is **traction**: the rate at which the business captures monetizable value. Behavior design supports traction by making repeated value delivery more likely.

## Source Context in the Book

The source chapters use behavior design to explain how the Altverse team improves the customer experience after launch. The team learns that they should not keep adding features indiscriminately. Instead, they should focus on helping customers achieve the first meaningful result quickly, then gradually guide them to the next step.

Later, the book frames this as part of a bigger system:

- make happy customers
- reduce friction
- turn activation into retention
- make the product the new status quo
- sustain growth through repeated value delivery

This turns launch into an ongoing process of reducing friction, reinforcing progress, and designing for repeated success. Over time, that behavior becomes habit, and habit becomes retention.
