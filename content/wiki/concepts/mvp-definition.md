# Minimum Viable Product

A **minimum viable product (MVP)** is the **smallest validated product** that can **create, deliver, and capture customer value** while causing a real customer switch. In [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], the MVP is **not** the starting point; it comes **after** customer discovery, offer validation, and narrowing to a specific early adopter segment.

Maurya's definition is stricter than the common "ship something small and see what happens" interpretation. In *Running Lean*, the MVP is the first built expression of a **validated business model**, not a shortcut around discovery. The team should know what problem it is solving, for whom, why those customers care, and whether the business can plausibly work before building.

> [!note]- Terminology conflict
> Some product communities use "MVP" to mean "the first thing you can ship." *Running Lean* uses a narrower definition: the smallest product that creates, delivers, and captures value **after** problem discovery and offer validation. This page follows Maurya's definition.

## Core idea

The MVP exists to reduce risk. It helps a team test the most important assumptions with the least amount of time and money. But the book stresses that an MVP is only useful if the team has already done enough work to know:

- what problem it is solving
- who the early adopters are
- what offer would cause a switch
- whether the business model can plausibly work

So the practical rule is:

- **Not**: build quickly and hope users give feedback.
- **Yes**: validate the problem, craft an offer, then build the smallest solution customers will buy.

This is why Maurya ties MVP thinking to [[concepts/traction]], [[concepts/build-trap]], [[concepts/business-model-design]], [[concepts/problem-solution-fit]], [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], and [[concepts/feasibility]].

> [!info]- New emphasis from the later chapters
> The later material connects MVP thinking to [[concepts/traction-roadmap]], [[concepts/90-day-cycles]], the [[concepts/mafia-offer]], launch batching, and growth loops. That means the MVP is not just a launch artifact; it is the first built expression of a business model that has already been modeled, stress tested, prioritized, validated, delivered to early adopters, and prepared for repeatable delivery.

## How *Running Lean* defines MVP

Maurya frames MVP as the smallest solution that can:

- **create value** for customers
- **deliver value** in a usable way
- **capture value** for the business

That means an MVP is not just a prototype, demo, or feature slice. It should be the first version of a product that supports a real business exchange.

The book also argues that many founders misuse MVP as a license to skip discovery. Steve, the cautionary example in the introduction, thinks he can simply rush to ship a first release. Mary corrects him: if the underlying idea is weak, launching something small only reveals that the idea is weak. What matters is not speed alone, but whether the team has already reduced the market and problem risk enough to make the MVP meaningful.

The later chapters sharpen this point further: the whole purpose of customer discovery is to understand the customer's world so well that the team can design the right offer and then the right product. In that sense, the MVP is not a substitute for understanding customers; it is the built expression of that understanding.

## MVP in the product discovery flow

In the book's sequence, the MVP comes after earlier discovery steps:

1. **Understand the problem**
2. **Define possible solutions**
3. **Craft an offer**
4. **Build the MVP**

That sequence matters because the offer is where the market tests the idea most directly. The MVP then becomes the smallest product implementation of a validated offer.

This is closely connected to [[concepts/demo-sell-build]] versus [[concepts/build-demo-sell]]: Maurya favors validating demand before full product development. The book's examples show that you do not start by building a product and hoping the market responds; you start by deconstructing the idea, testing the riskiest assumptions, and only then turning the validated offer into software or another deliverable.

The introduction and Chapter 1 also place this inside a broader workflow: deconstruct the idea with a Lean Canvas, stress test desirability, viability, and feasibility, then move toward product implementation. That means the MVP is downstream of model design, not the starting tactic.

## What an MVP is for

A good MVP helps a team:

- confirm that customers care enough to buy or engage
- learn which assumptions matter most
- avoid overbuilding
- create traction early enough to keep going

It is a learning instrument, but also a business instrument. It should produce evidence of a working business model, not just product usage.

In Maurya's framing, MVPs are especially useful because they help teams reach evidence-based decisions in conditions of extreme uncertainty. The goal is not to ship something minimal for its own sake; the goal is to validate the model fast enough to preserve resources.

## What an MVP is not

An MVP is not:

- a random small feature
- a rough prototype with no clear customer value
- a "throw it over the fence" release
- a substitute for customer discovery
- a license to ignore traction and economics

The book warns that if a team starts with the wrong idea, even fast iteration can become a loop of confusion. In that case, the team learns only that the idea does not resonate, and customers simply leave instead of helping refine it.

## Relationship to the build trap

The MVP is supposed to help teams escape the [[concepts/build-trap]]. Without it, founders may keep polishing features, assuming one more addition will unlock growth. Maurya argues that real progress comes from solving the right problem for the right people, not from endlessly expanding the solution.

A useful nuance from the later chapters is that **the MVP is downstream of model design**. The book's Continuous Innovation flow is: deconstruct the idea, stress test the model, identify the riskiest assumptions, and only then build the MVP. That makes MVP less a starting tactic and more the first product expression of a model that has already been shaped by evidence.

> [!tip]- Practical rule
> If you cannot explain the problem, the target customer, and the offer in a few clear sentences, you are probably not ready to build an MVP yet.

## How 90-day cycles change the meaning of MVP

The later chapters add an important operational layer: the MVP is not developed in isolation, but inside a [[concepts/90-day-cycles]] system. Each cycle starts by modeling the business, prioritizing the riskiest assumptions, and testing them with small campaigns. In that setting, the MVP becomes the **built result of validated campaigns**, not a standalone first release.

Chapter 6 especially makes this clear:

- the team uses a constraints-based approach to identify the bottleneck
- defines a 90-day OKR from the [[concepts/traction-roadmap]]
- and runs experiments that reduce uncertainty before committing to build

Chapter 7 tightens this further by showing that problem/solution fit must come before the MVP build. The team should first discover the customer's real problem, define a fitting solution, and test an offer. Only after that does the MVP become the right next move. In this framing, the MVP is not the discovery mechanism; it is the first built embodiment of validated learning.

Chapter 11 reinforces the same idea from the retrospective side: the 90-day review forces the team to look at what they learned, what traction they created, and whether the next move should be persevere, pivot, or pause. That means MVP decisions should stay tied to evidence and cycle reviews, not to sunk cost or pride.

## Customer understanding comes first

Chapter 8 deepens the book's view of MVP by showing how customer interviews uncover real jobs-to-be-done and the forces that shape switching behavior. That chapter argues that founders should understand their customers better than the customers understand themselves, because the quality of the MVP depends on the quality of the underlying insight.

This means the MVP is only as good as the team's ability to uncover:

- triggering events
- existing alternatives
- switching triggers
- desired outcomes
- push/pull/inertia/friction forces
- the bigger context of the job-to-be-done

So the MVP should not be treated as a shortcut around customer research. It is the product that comes **after** the team has learned enough to have a credible theory of what customers need.

## How the later chapters sharpen the definition

The newer material makes the MVP definition even more concrete by tying it to the solution-design sprint. The path is:

- problem discovery
- solution design
- offer delivery
- then MVP

That ordering matters. It means the MVP is not the discovery tool; it is the implementation of a solution and offer that has already been sharpened by interviews and pattern-finding.

Chapter 9 also adds a more practical emphasis: the MVP should be the **smallest solution that causes a switch**. In other words, the best MVP is not just the smallest buildable thing - it is the smallest thing that can displace the current way customers solve the problem.

That chapter also adds a timing constraint: after problem/solution fit, the MVP should be something the team can **build and launch within about two months**. If it takes longer, the market context may shift and the team may need another discovery pass. This makes speed of delivery a feasibility concern, not the only concern.

## MVP recipes in *Running Lean*

Maurya uses several MVP patterns to show that "MVP" is more about validation strategy than feature count:

- **Concierge MVP** - deliver the service manually at first
- **Wizard-of-Oz MVP** - fake the automated product by cobbling together existing tools
- **Foot-in-the-Door MVP** - deliver the smallest promise needed to enter the customer's world

The offer-delivery chapter also reinforces that early validation can happen through a **mafia offer**: a compelling offer customers cannot refuse. In that case, the "MVP" is still downstream of demand validation and is best thought of as the built form of a switch-worthy promise.

The growth chapter adds a final refinement: after product/customer fit, the MVP becomes the payload inside a larger growth system. That means a strong MVP is not just something that can be sold once; it is something that can be delivered repeatedly inside a happy-customer loop and eventually scaled through a growth rocket.

## What makes an MVP strong

The book's later synthesis can be summarized as five practical checks:

- **Problem**: Are you solving the smallest useful subset of a real problem?
- **Promise**: Is the value proposition compelling and specific enough to cause a switch?
- **Price**: Does the pricing support both customer value and business viability?
- **People**: Are you targeting the right early adopters at the right moment?
- **Packaging**: Can you deliver the solution quickly in the right form factor?

This makes the MVP a design challenge, not just a development task.

## Source perspective

In *Running Lean*, the MVP is part of a broader strategy for reducing startup risk under uncertainty. It is most effective when built from validated learning, not assumption-driven enthusiasm. The book's perspective is that the MVP should emerge from problem discovery, not replace it.

The later chapters reinforce this with the Continuous Innovation view: teams should prioritize traction, use the Lean Canvas to deconstruct the idea, and test the riskiest assumptions before building. In that framing, the MVP is not the hero of the story; it is one milestone inside a larger evidence-based process.

> [!info] Key takeaway
> The MVP is the smallest **validated** product, not merely the smallest possible product.

## Deep study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> An MVP is the smallest real product you build after proving people want it.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like offering a sample dish before opening a restaurant: prove demand first, then scale up the kitchen.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> - MVP means the first rough thing you can ship.
> - Smaller is always better.
> - Metrics alone can validate an MVP.
> - The MVP is the goal instead of a tool.
> - You must build the full product before selling.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** Why doesn't Maurya want teams to start with an MVP?
> **A:** Because the MVP is only useful after the team understands the problem and validates an offer; otherwise it may just be a small version of the wrong idea.
>
> **Q:** What three things should an MVP do in *Running Lean*?
> **A:** It should create value, deliver value, and capture value for the business.
>
> **Q:** How does the book's MVP differ from a prototype?
> **A:** A prototype explores or demonstrates an idea, while an MVP is the smallest product meant to validate real customer demand and business value.
>
> **Q:** What does the MVP help a team avoid?
> **A:** It helps avoid overbuilding and the build trap by forcing early evidence about whether customers care.
>
> **Q:** Where does the MVP sit in Maurya's process?
> **A:** It comes after deconstructing the idea, testing the model, and validating the offer; it is not the first step.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> A clear MVP definition keeps teams from building too much too soon. It focuses effort on the riskiest assumptions, speeds learning, and helps a team reach traction with less waste.
