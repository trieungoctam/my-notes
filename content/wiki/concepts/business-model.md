# Business Model

A **business model** is the system that explains how a venture **creates value**, **delivers value**, and **captures value** from customers. In *Running Lean*, Ash Maurya treats the business model not as background context, but as the startup's **real product**: the thing founders must design, test, refine, and eventually scale.

The key implication is that a startup does not begin with a finished product; it begins with a set of assumptions about how the venture will work. Those assumptions should be made visible, stress-tested, and iterated into something that works before time and money run out.

## What the concept means

A business model answers a practical set of questions:

- Who are the customers?
- What problem are they trying to solve?
- What solution do they want?
- How will they discover it?
- How will the business make money?
- What costs will it incur?
- What metrics show progress?
- What advantage, if any, is hard to copy?

Maurya's framing is that these questions belong together. A business model is not just a revenue plan or a marketing plan. It is an interconnected system of assumptions that must fit together.

The [[concepts/lean-canvas]] is the main tool used in the book to represent this system on one page. It makes the model visible so it can be tested, discussed, and revised.

## Why it matters

The book argues that many startups fail because they spend too much time building a solution before proving that the business model works. In a world where products are easier to build and competition is intense, success depends on finding a model that creates traction before resources run out.

That shifts the startup question from:

> "Can we build it?"

to:

> "Should we build it?"

The business model is therefore the object of design, validation, and learning.

## Business model as a system of assumptions

A business model is more than a feature list or a pricing decision. It is a set of mutually dependent parts:

- **Customer segments** and **early adopters** define who the model serves.
- **Problem** and **existing alternatives** define the pain in the market.
- **Unique value proposition** explains why the offering is different and worth attention.
- **Solution** sketches the smallest way to deliver value.
- **Channels** show how customers are reached.
- **Revenue streams** and **cost structure** define viability.
- **Key metrics** measure progress.
- **Unfair advantage** suggests what might be hard to copy.

Because the parts are interdependent, changing one part often changes the others. That is why the model must be refined iteratively rather than assumed correct from the start. This is closely related to [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] and [[concepts/traction]].

## The book's perspective on business models

In *Running Lean*, the business model is the startup's first design challenge. The founder should:

1. Sketch the model quickly.
2. Identify the riskiest assumptions.
3. Validate those assumptions in stages.
4. Iterate based on customer feedback and evidence.

This is very different from the traditional business-plan approach, which often asks founders to write long forecasts and detailed roadmaps before they have real evidence. Maurya's approach favors short cycles, customer learning, and a bias toward action.

## Business model, traction, and the product

One of the strongest claims in the source document is that **traction is evidence of a working business model**. Investors do not primarily fund ideas or even solutions; they fund evidence that the market is responding.

That means the business model must be designed with traction in mind:

- It identifies a specific early adopter.
- It defines a compelling problem/solution match.
- It includes a path to first revenue.
- It can be tested without fully building the final product.

This ties the business model to [[concepts/traction]], [[concepts/problem-solution-fit]], and [[concepts/mvp]].

## Different kinds of business models

The document also shows that business models come in different archetypes:

- **Direct** models, where users and customers are the same.
- **Multisided** models, where users and customers differ.
- **Marketplace** models, where buyers and sellers are distinct sides.

The implication is that you should model each business story separately if combining them would blur the assumptions. A good business model is specific enough to be tested.

## The model in practice: design, validation, growth

The book breaks the business model journey into three major parts:

### Part 1: Design
Design means turning a big idea into a structured model. This includes deconstructing the idea on a Lean Canvas and making the model explicit.

### Part 2: Validation
Validation means using evidence to test desirability, viability, and feasibility. Instead of guessing, the founder uses interviews, offers, Fermi estimates, and 90-day cycles to see whether the model works.

### Part 3: Growth
Growth means taking a validated model and finding a scalable engine for acquisition and retention. Maurya uses the metaphor of a rocket ship: boosters help with liftoff, but a sustainable growth rocket is needed for the long journey.

This progression shows that the business model is not static; it evolves from initial design to repeatable value delivery to scalable growth.

## Business model and customer progress

Later chapters expand the concept beyond initial acquisition. A business model must not only attract customers, but also help them succeed repeatedly. That means the model must support:

- activation and the first aha moment
- retention and habit formation
- referral and word of mouth
- customer progress toward desired outcomes

In other words, the business model ultimately has to work as a **customer-making system**, not just a sales system.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A business model is the full plan for how a company works: who it helps, what it helps them do, how it reaches them, how it makes money, and how it keeps going. In *Running Lean*, you don't assume that plan is right-you write it down and test it early.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> A business model is like planning a restaurant. It is not just the recipe. You also need the customers, the location, the menu price, the costs, the way people find you, and the reason they come back.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> **Myth:** A business model is just pricing. **Reality:** Pricing is only one part of a larger system.
>
> **Myth:** A good product automatically means a good business. **Reality:** The market, timing, channels, and economics still have to work.
>
> **Myth:** The business model should be fully known before starting. **Reality:** It begins as a set of assumptions that should be validated and revised.
>
> **Myth:** Growth is separate from the business model. **Reality:** In the book, scalable growth is an extension of a working business model.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** Why does *Running Lean* treat the business model as the product?
> **A:** Because the startup succeeds or fails based on the whole system of value creation, delivery, and capture-not just the feature set.
>
> **Q:** What is the difference between a solution and a business model?
> **A:** A solution is the thing you build; a business model includes the customer, problem, channels, pricing, costs, metrics, and growth logic around it.
>
> **Q:** Why is the Lean Canvas useful?
> **A:** It makes assumptions visible on one page so the team can prioritize and test the riskiest ones.
>
> **Q:** What does traction tell you about a business model?
> **A:** It shows that customers are responding in a way that suggests the model may work.
>
> **Q:** Why does the book connect business models to growth rockets?
> **A:** Because a working business model still needs a sustainable growth loop to scale.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> Understanding the business model helps founders avoid building the wrong thing efficiently. It improves decision-making, focuses validation on the riskiest assumptions, and increases the odds of reaching product/market fit and then scaling sustainably.

## Deep Study Sections

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A business model is the story of how a business works in real life: who it helps, what problem it solves, how people find it, how it earns money, and how it grows. In *Running Lean*, the important idea is that this story should be tested instead of guessed.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> Think of a business model like launching a food truck. You need to know what you serve, who buys it, where they find you, what it costs, what they pay, and how you'll keep getting customers.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> **Myth:** A business model is the same as a product idea. **Reality:** The product is only one part of the model; customer, pricing, channels, and costs matter too.
>
> **Myth:** If you build a great product, the business model will take care of itself. **Reality:** Great products can still fail if the market or economics do not work.
>
> **Myth:** Business models should be fully specified before testing. **Reality:** They are assumptions that improve through validation.
>
> **Myth:** Growth is separate from the model. **Reality:** A working model needs a sustainable growth loop.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** Why does the book say the business model is the product?
> **A:** Because the startup's success depends on the whole system, not just the feature set.
>
> **Q:** What makes a business model different from a business plan?
> **A:** A business model is a concise, testable set of assumptions; a business plan is usually a longer, more static document.
>
> **Q:** How does the Lean Canvas support business model thinking?
> **A:** It compresses the model onto one page so the team can see dependencies, prioritize risks, and iterate quickly.
>
> **Q:** Why is traction important when evaluating a business model?
> **A:** Traction shows that customers are responding, which is evidence that the model may be working.
>
> **Q:** Why might one company need more than one canvas?
> **A:** If it serves different actors or uses different revenue logic, separate canvases can keep the assumptions clear.
>
> **Q:** Why does growth appear later in the book's framework?
> **A:** Because growth becomes meaningful only after the model can repeatedly create value and retain customers.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> If you understand the business model, you can test the riskiest assumptions early instead of spending months building something that won't sell. That makes it easier to reach traction, adjust quickly, and avoid the build trap.
