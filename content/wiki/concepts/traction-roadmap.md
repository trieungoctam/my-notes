# Traction Roadmap

A **traction roadmap** is a staged growth map that turns a long-term business goal into concrete customer milestones over time. In *Running Lean*, it is the planning instrument that connects [[concepts/fermi-estimate]] viability work, [[concepts/customer-factory]] metrics, [[concepts/right-action-right-time]] execution, launch sequencing, post-launch customer progress, and eventual growth rockets inside [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] cycles.

It is not just a forecast. It is a **decision tool** that helps a team test whether a business model can plausibly produce a target outcome and, just as importantly, what must be validated first to get there.

## What it does

The traction roadmap converts an abstract business ambition into a **time-based customer-growth trajectory**. Instead of treating growth as a vague aspiration, it makes growth explicit by showing how the business should move from early validation to repeatable traction, then to product/market fit, and finally to scale.

Maurya uses it to complement the [[concepts/customer-factory]] and to set the sequence of goals inside a 90-day cycle. In practice, it helps founders avoid the trap of building a product first and hoping growth appears later.

In *Running Lean, 3rd Edition*, the roadmap also becomes the bridge between the business model and the operating cadence of the company. It helps teams decide:

- how many customers they need
- when they need them
- what growth rate is plausible
- which traction milestones come first
- which risks matter now versus later
- what must be validated before scale becomes realistic

> [!note] Forecast vs. operating instrument
> In many startup conversations, a roadmap sounds like a simple forecast of future growth. *Running Lean* treats it more strictly: the roadmap is not a promise that growth will happen on schedule, but a way to test whether the business model can plausibly produce that growth and which assumptions must be validated first.

## Core ideas

### 1. Connects minimum success criteria to customer counts

The roadmap begins with the [[concepts/minimum-success-criteria|minimum success criteria]]-for example, a target ARR in three years. From there, it estimates how many active customers are needed to support that goal. This makes the growth target concrete instead of aspirational.

### 2. Breaks growth into stages

The roadmap divides the journey into phases such as:

- **Now**: problem/solution fit
- **Next**: product/market fit
- **Later**: scale

Each stage has different traction expectations and different risks.

### 3. Prioritizes the right risks at the right time

The roadmap is not just about numbers. It helps decide what to work on now versus later. Early stages focus on demand-side validation, while later stages shift toward launch discipline, retention, and scaling.

### 4. Supports a now-next-later rollout plan

The roadmap is used to build a [[concepts/right-action-right-time]] plan. This avoids premature optimization by making sure the team focuses on the most urgent constraints first.

### 5. Works with 90-day cycles

In the book's operating system, the traction roadmap informs the goal for each 90-day cycle. The roadmap is then translated into campaigns, sprints, and measurable customer-factory metrics. It becomes the link between long-term ambition and short-term execution.

### 6. Creates a review baseline

In later chapters, the traction roadmap is also the reference point for a [[concepts/90-day-cycle-review]]: the team compares actual traction against the planned roadmap and uses that evidence to decide whether to **persevere, pivot, or pause**.

### 7. Extends into launch and post-launch growth

The roadmap does not stop at MVP launch. In the launch phase it supports:

- batch rollout planning
- early access sequencing
- capacity management for a Concierge MVP
- continued customer-factory measurement

After launch, it also helps teams think about how the product should create repeatable customer progress, retention, and habit formation. That means the roadmap is still useful once the product is live: it helps connect early traction to the next stage of making happy customers.

### 8. Leads into growth rockets

In the growth phase, the roadmap becomes the way to choose and validate a scalable channel or growth loop. Maurya's rocket metaphor treats the roadmap as the guidance system for selecting the next stage of growth rather than chasing every channel at once.

## How it works in the book

In the book's example, Steve initially imagines a linear path to success, but the traction roadmap reveals that his original plan would require far too many customers too soon. Once he revises his assumptions, he sees that the roadmap should be **exponential rather than linear**, with early milestones much smaller than the long-term goal.

The roadmap helps him determine:

- the number of leads needed
- expected conversion rates
- churn and retention assumptions
- the likely customer count at 3 months, 12 months, 24 months, and 36 months
- what counts as problem/solution fit, product/market fit, and scale

This makes the roadmap both a planning tool and a diagnostic tool.

The later chapters add an important nuance: the roadmap also drives the sequencing of validation work. Steve does not simply chase growth; he uses the roadmap to decide when to run problem discovery, when to deliver an offer, when to build an MVP, and how to stage launch and customer success.

In the 90-day cycle review, that same roadmap becomes the scorecard. The team plots actual traction against the roadmap, reviews the business model snapshots from the start of the cycle, and uses the gap between planned and actual outcomes to inform the next decision.

## Relationship to other concepts

The traction roadmap sits between business model design and execution:

- [[concepts/lean-canvas]] defines the model
- [[concepts/fermi-estimate]] tests whether the model can work on paper
- [[concepts/customer-factory]] describes how traction is created
- [[concepts/mafia-offer]] and [[concepts/concierge-mvp]] are tactical ways to get early traction
- [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] supplies the cycle of modeling, prioritizing, and testing
- [[concepts/traction]] is the outcome the roadmap is trying to make observable and measurable
- [[concepts/90-day-cycle-review]] uses the roadmap as evidence for the next step
- [[concepts/happy-customers]] extends the roadmap beyond acquisition into retention and habit formation
- [[concepts/growth-rocket]] extends the roadmap beyond product/market fit into scalable, sustainable growth loops
- [[concepts/minimum-success-criteria]] sets the endpoint that the roadmap must plausibly support

It is also deeply tied to [[concepts/traction]] itself: the roadmap does not merely forecast growth, it frames the learning journey needed to earn growth.

## Why it matters

The traction roadmap prevents founders from confusing a big vision with a workable path. It forces clarity about what growth should look like, when the business must prove itself, and which milestones matter before scale becomes realistic.

Without it, teams may overbuild, overhire, or overpromise. With it, they can align around measurable milestones and decide whether to persevere, pivot, or pause.

It also helps teams avoid the classic mistake of trying to scale before they have validated demand. In the book's logic, the roadmap turns growth into a staged sequence of evidence, not a leap of faith.

It also keeps the team from stopping at acquisition. A good roadmap keeps attention on activation, retention, and repeatable customer progress so the business can become the new status quo for customers.

Finally, *Find Your Growth Rocket* extends the same roadmap logic into the growth phase: once a business has repeatable traction, the roadmap helps the team identify the right scalable channel or growth loop to double down on next.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A traction roadmap is a startup growth map that shows how many customers you need, when you need them, and what milestones come first.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like planning a long hike with checkpoints and supply stops instead of just saying you'll reach the mountain someday.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: A traction roadmap is just a revenue forecast. Reality: It is a staged learning-and-growth plan.
> Myth: It should focus on scale from day one. Reality: It starts with validation and only later shifts to scale.
> Myth: Once written, it should stay fixed. Reality: It should change as the team learns more.
> Myth: It guarantees success. Reality: It only tests whether success is plausible and what evidence is needed next.
> Myth: It ends at launch. Reality: It continues into retention, habit formation, and growth loops.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** Why does the traction roadmap start with minimum success criteria?
> **A:** Because it works backward from a meaningful business outcome to test whether the idea can realistically get there.
>
> **Q:** How is a traction roadmap different from a product roadmap?
> **A:** A product roadmap lists features or releases; a traction roadmap lists growth milestones and validation stages.
>
> **Q:** What risks does it help prioritize?
> **A:** It helps teams focus on the riskiest assumptions and the most important customer-growth milestones first.
>
> **Q:** Why is it useful in early-stage startups?
> **A:** It keeps the team from trying to scale too early and helps them focus on the most important milestones first.
>
> **Q:** How does it connect to the customer factory?
> **A:** The roadmap sets the growth goals, and the customer factory explains how customers are created through the funnel.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> It turns vague ambition into a testable sequence of milestones, helping teams avoid premature scaling, focus on the right risks, and decide when to persevere, pivot, or pause. It also extends beyond launch into retention, happy customers, and growth rockets.
