# Pre-build Tests That Package a Promise and Secure Real Customer Commitment

Pre-build tests are **validation experiments run before a full MVP exists** to see whether customers care enough to commit. In *Running Lean*, Ash Maurya frames them as the bridge between [[concepts/customer-discovery]], [[concepts/solution-design]], and building: instead of guessing at demand, teams package a believable promise, deliver it to early adopters, and use real commitment as evidence.

At the center of this concept is a simple claim: **customers buy a promise of something better, not a finished product**. A pre-build test turns that promise into something testable, usually through a combination of:

- a clear [[concepts/lean-canvas|UVP]]
- a demo or prototype
- a call to action
- a fair price anchored against existing alternatives

In the later chapters of *Running Lean*, these tests also become the foundation for launch, repeatable sales, and eventually a scalable [[concepts/growth-loop|growth loop]]. Early commitment is not just a validation signal; it becomes the seed of a durable customer engine.

## What Pre-Build Tests Validate

Pre-build tests are designed to answer the earliest, most important validation questions:

- Is the problem painful enough to cause a switch?
- Does the promise feel meaningfully better than the [[concepts/continuous-innovation|existing alternative]]?
- Will early adopters take a concrete next step?
- Can the business model support the price and economics?
- Can the team translate customer commitment into a launch-ready path?
- Can early commitment later sustain a repeatable acquisition engine?

They are especially useful before a full [[concepts/traction-roadmap]] or MVP is built, because they surface whether demand exists at all and which parts of the model need adjustment. In the later growth stages, the same logic helps teams see whether the initial offer can evolve into a sustainable engine rather than a one-off sale.

## Core Offer Campaign Types

The book lists several offer recipes, each with different trade-offs between scale and conversion:

- **Smoke test** - tease the offer to collect interest or email addresses
- **Landing page** - present a specific CTA and measure signups
- **Webinar** - educate and attract prospects
- **Preorder** - collect advance sales
- **Crowdfunding** - fund the idea through public commitments
- **Direct sales** - pitch prospects one-on-one and close deals
- **Mafia offer** - a high-touch, highly targeted offer built from discovery interviews

Maurya positions these as different tools for different jobs, but strongly recommends starting with the **mafia offer** for early validation because it produces the most learning and the strongest signal. Later, the same offer logic can be reused to support batch launches, staged rollout, and even the early seeds of a [[concepts/growth-rocket]].

## Why the Mafia Offer Is Special

A mafia offer is an offer customers cannot refuse-not because they are manipulated, but because the offer is highly relevant and compelling. It is built from deep customer understanding and has three main properties:

1. It nails the customer's top problems
2. It demonstrates a believable solution
3. It gives a clear path to getting started

Unlike a broad marketing campaign, the mafia offer is intentionally narrow and high-touch. It is meant to produce **repeatable sales on a small scale**, not immediate scale.

It also becomes a critical transition point in later chapters: once the team secures early commitments, they can use those customers to validate the MVP, shape launch, and begin building repeatable demand.

## The Learning Logic Behind Offer Campaigns

Offer campaigns are not just about selling. They are about **testing assumptions in the real world**. The book emphasizes that if you pitch a landing page or direct offer too early, you may fail without knowing why, because the offer contains many untested variables.

That is why Maurya recommends a sequence like this:

1. [[concepts/customer-discovery]] to understand the problem
2. Solution design to determine what to promise
3. Offer assembly to package that promise
4. Offer delivery to test demand and pricing
5. Only then, build the MVP

This makes offer campaigns a central part of [[concepts/continuous-innovation]]: they convert research into evidence. They also help a team learn whether its initial promise can eventually feed a repeatable customer engine rather than a one-off sale.

## Offer Campaigns as a Gate to MVP Building

The main point of an early offer campaign is not to "launch" in the traditional sense. It is to secure enough tangible commitment to justify building. Depending on the model, commitment may take forms such as:

- preorders
- trial signups
- demo bookings
- letters of intent
- advance payments

In this sense, the offer campaign helps determine whether the team has achieved **problem/solution fit** before moving on to building and scaling. The later growth chapters extend this logic: early commitments are not merely proof of desire, but the first proof that a business model can be repeated, retained, and expanded.

## From Offer Delivery to Launch Preparation

The later chapters in *Running Lean* extend the role of offer campaigns beyond early validation. Once a team has a compelling offer and a small batch of committed customers, the next challenge is not "get attention at all costs," but **prepare to launch without losing the learning loop**.

Maurya recommends:

- keeping the [[concepts/customer-factory|customer factory]] running while the MVP is being built
- rolling out the product in **batches**
- focusing on the **best early adopters** first
- using launch work to preserve speed, learning, and focus
- avoiding premature optimization and big-bang publicity

This shifts offer campaigns from a one-time validation trick into an ongoing operational discipline. In the launch phase, they become a way to create repeatable delivery, not just commit a few early buyers.

## Trade-offs Between Campaign Types

Maurya maps offer types by two key dimensions:

- **customer conversion rate**
- **scalability / reach**

This creates an important trade-off:

- high-reach methods such as smoke tests may generate weak commitment
- high-conversion methods such as mafia offers reach fewer people but teach more

For early validation, the book favors **maximum learning per unit time**, even if the method is not scalable yet. Later, as the business grows, the same learning can be used to identify the scalable channel or growth loop that matters most.

> [!note]
> In the launch and growth phases, the book adds a second balancing act: teams should not stop customer acquisition entirely just because they have early customers. The customer factory has to keep running, but launch work should be staged so the team can learn, deliver value, and scale deliberately.

## How Pre-Build Tests Connect to Growth

One of the newer insights from the later chapters is that pre-build tests are not just a gate to MVP creation; they are the earliest expression of the future growth machine.

If the team can:

- secure commitment before building
- deliver value quickly
- make customers happy
- and observe repeatable behavior

then those first commitments can eventually point toward a sustainable [[concepts/growth-loop|growth loop]]. In *Running Lean*, growth is not treated as an afterthought; it is the result of identifying the right sequence of commitments, delivery, and repeatable customer behavior.

## How Offer Campaigns Connect to the Rest of the Framework

Offer campaigns sit inside the broader validation workflow of:

- [[concepts/lean-canvas]] for modeling the business
- [[concepts/traction-roadmap]] for sizing the opportunity
- [[concepts/customer-forces]] for understanding switching behavior
- [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] for identifying the bigger context
- [[concepts/mafia-offer]] for packaging the promise
- [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] for running evidence-based cycles
- [[concepts/problem-solution-fit]] for deciding whether to build
- [[concepts/customer-factory]] for tracking the conversion pipeline
- [[concepts/growth-rocket]] for understanding how early demand can become scalable growth

They are the practical mechanism that turns a theory of the business into a measurable market test.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> Before building the full product, you make a small promise and see if customers will commit to it.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like taking pre-orders for a meal after showing the menu and tasting samples, before opening the full restaurant.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: Offer campaigns are the same as advertising. Reality: They are experiments to test commitment, not just awareness.
> Myth: The best offer campaign is the one with the biggest audience. Reality: Early on, the best offer is often the one that teaches fastest and converts most strongly.
> Myth: If customers say yes once, the job is done. Reality: Early commitments are only the start; the team still has to deliver value and build repeatability.
> Myth: Offer campaigns replace customer discovery. Reality: They build on discovery and use it to package a better promise.
