# Pitch Worldviews

Pitch worldviews are the different mental frames audiences use to judge the same business model. In _Running Lean_, Ash Maurya argues that founders should not pitch the same way to everyone: investors, customers, advisors, and later growth-oriented stakeholders each care about different evidence, different risks, and different outcomes. A strong pitch translates one underlying business model into the language that matters most to the listener. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]], pp.210-228]

Pitch worldviews are part of the broader practice of [[concepts/pitching]] and sit alongside [[concepts/traction]], [[concepts/value-based-pricing]], [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], and [[concepts/problem-discovery]] as a way to communicate progress under uncertainty.

> [!note]
> Chapter 8 adds an important nuance to the customer worldview: the best customer pitch is usually rooted in discovery, not explanation. Maurya argues that founders should understand the customer's switching trigger, existing alternatives, friction, and desired outcome before trying to persuade them. That means worldview-aware pitching is built on [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] and [[concepts/customer-forces]], not just on product features.

> [!note]
> Chapter 14 adds a growth-stage lens: once a business shows repeatability and a clear happy-customer loop, the pitch must increasingly address a scalable growth rocket, sustainable growth loops, and the transition from early traction to product/market fit and beyond. Investors and internal stakeholders then care not only about the current model, but also about how it compounds. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-14-Find-Your-Growth-Rocket]], pp.467-487]

## Core Idea

Maurya identifies three main worldviews, then extends the practical pitching system across the startup lifecycle:

- **Advisor worldview**: cares about learning, clarity, and honest feedback rather than persuasion.
- **Customer worldview**: cares about whether the product solves a real problem and delivers a desired outcome.
- **Investor worldview**: cares about market size, monetization, [[concepts/traction|traction]], and defensibility.
- **Growth-stage stakeholder worldview**: cares about repeatability, scalable acquisition, and the sustainability of growth loops.

The key insight is that the same startup story must be reframed depending on who is hearing it and what decision they are making. Advisors want a sharper model; customers want relief from a real problem; investors want evidence of a business that can scale; growth-stage stakeholders want proof that the growth engine can compound.

## Why Worldview Matters

A pitch fails when it leads with the wrong things for the wrong audience. For example:

- Customers do not care about your financial model first; they care about their problem and what changes in their life if they switch.
- Investors do not care about your features first; they care about whether your business can scale and return capital.
- Advisors do not need a hard sell; they need enough context to give useful feedback.
- Growth-focused stakeholders do not just want traction snapshots; they want to know whether the growth engine is repeatable and sustainable.

This is why Maurya recommends starting with a **learning frame** rather than a pure sales frame. That lowers the stakes, improves dialogue, and helps founders refine the model before asking for funding or commitment.

## The Three Worldviews

### Advisor worldview

Advisors care about helping founders think better. The goal is not to persuade them immediately but to invite critique, identify blind spots, and build trust. Maurya recommends using a conversational, exploratory tone and asking for feedback rather than funding.

This worldview is especially useful early on because it helps the founder discover what parts of the model are still weak.

### Customer worldview

Customers are not buying a company narrative; they are evaluating whether the product helps them achieve a better outcome. For this worldview, the pitch should focus on:

- the customer's triggering event
- the existing alternative
- the problem or pain
- the unique value proposition
- the desired outcome

This is the worldview that connects most directly to [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] and [[concepts/customer-forces]]. The pitch should show why a customer would switch from the old way to the new way.

Chapter 8 deepens this further: the best customer pitch is often built from a **problem discovery conversation**. Rather than starting with your solution, you should learn how people currently use existing alternatives, what frustrations they encounter, what job they are trying to get done, and what pushes them to switch. That insight becomes the foundation for a more credible pitch.

### Investor worldview

Investors look for evidence that the business can become large and repeatable. The pitch should emphasize:

- the size of the opportunity
- the revenue model
- the [[concepts/traction]] story
- the [[concepts/unfair-advantage|unfair advantage]]

In this worldview, traction is the clearest signal that the model is working. Maurya notes that investors are usually not persuaded by abstract enthusiasm; they want to see momentum.

### Growth-stage stakeholder worldview

Once the business is showing repeatability, the pitch must begin to answer a new question: not just "Does it work?" but "Can it keep working and scale?" Chapter 14 reframes this as a search for a **growth rocket**-a sustainable growth loop powered by revenue, content/data, or referrals.

For this worldview, the pitch should emphasize:

- repeatable acquisition
- retention and the happy customer loop
- sustainability of the growth engine
- evidence that one growth loop can compound over time
- the path from early traction to product/market fit and scale

This lens matters because later-stage stakeholders, especially investors and executives, need to understand how the current model becomes a scalable one.

## How the Concept Fits Into the Pitch Process

Maurya suggests a progression:

1. Start with a **learning frame** for advisors.
2. Refine the narrative with customer feedback.
3. Prepare an investor-specific pitch once traction and clarity improve.
4. As repeatability emerges, extend the pitch toward the growth rocket and sustainable scale story.

This means pitch worldviews are not separate presentations so much as different lenses on the same business model. The founder's job is to choose the right lens for the audience and stage.

## Structure of a Worldview-Aware Pitch

The book's pitch structure maps to what each audience wants to know:

- **Why now?** What has changed?
- **What's at stake?** Why does this matter?
- **What's broken?** What is wrong with the current way?
- **The fix**: How does the solution help?
- **Your moat**: What makes this defensible?
- **How you make money**: What is the business model?
- **Your key milestones**: What traction shows progress?
- **Current progress**: What have you already proven?
- **The team**: Who can execute?
- **The ask**: What do you need next?
- **The growth engine**: How does this scale sustainably over time?

The emphasis changes depending on whether the listener is an investor, customer, advisor, or growth-stage stakeholder.

## Practical Implications

Pitch worldviews turn pitching into a form of audience design. Instead of asking, "How do I explain everything I know?" the founder asks, "What does this audience need to hear to make a good decision?"

That shift matters because it prevents common startup mistakes:

- overloading customers with strategy
- pitching investors with vague product enthusiasm
- asking advisors for validation instead of feedback
- confusing early traction with a scalable growth engine

In practice, worldview-aware pitching helps founders get better meetings, better feedback, better buy-in, and eventually a more credible growth story.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> Different people judge your startup differently: advisors want honest learning, customers want a better result, investors want proof it can grow, and growth-stage stakeholders want proof the growth engine can keep going.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like selling the same house to three people: the architect wants feedback on the design, the family wants to know if it fits their life, and the bank wants to know if it will hold value and scale as an investment.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: One pitch works for everyone. Reality: investors, customers, advisors, and growth-stage stakeholders each need different framing.
> Myth: Pitching is only for fundraising. Reality: you also pitch to customers, co-founders, advisors, and later to growth partners or investors.
> Myth: Start by describing the product. Reality: often start with the problem, trigger, outcome, and stage-appropriate evidence.
> Myth: Traction alone is enough for the investor story. Reality: as the company matures, investors also care about repeatability and scalable growth loops.
> Myth: Advisors are just mini-investors. Reality: advisors are mainly for learning, critique, and model-sharpening.
