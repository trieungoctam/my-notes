# Business Model Archetypes

Business model archetypes are recurring patterns for how a company creates, delivers, and captures value. In *Running Lean*, Ash Maurya uses them as a practical way to keep one Lean Canvas focused on one coherent business story instead of mixing incompatible assumptions. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-1-Deconstruct-Your-Idea-on-a-Lean-Canvas]], pp. 99-107]

Maurya's later chapters show that archetypes are more than a classification system. They shape how you identify the customer, what counts as an existing alternative, what switching trigger might cause adoption, how monetization works, how traction is measured, how rollout should be staged, and how you learn from customers. In other words, archetypes influence [[concepts/problem-first-thinking]], [[concepts/desirability-testing]], [[concepts/fermi-estimate]], [[concepts/traction-roadmap]], [[concepts/business-model-pitch]], [[concepts/customer-factory-blueprint]], and [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] all at once. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp. 108-132; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]], pp. 179-209; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp. 210-228; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]], pp. 229-237; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp. 300-345]

> [!note]
> Maurya does not treat archetypes as rigid categories that determine the company's future forever. He uses them as starting points for modeling and validation, then expects founders to split, refine, or reframe canvases as the idea becomes clearer.

## Core Idea

Maurya identifies three basic business model archetypes:

1. **Direct** - users are also customers.
2. **Multisided** - users and customers are different groups.
3. **Marketplace** - buyers and sellers interact through the platform.

The main value of the archetype lens is not taxonomy; it is focus. It helps you define one coherent business story per [[concepts/lean-canvas]], choose the right validation questions, and avoid confusing one model's assumptions with another's.

> [!note]
> Maurya repeatedly shows that the same startup idea can hide multiple business stories. The right move is usually to split canvases by archetype rather than force one canvas to explain everything.

## Why Archetypes Matter

A Lean Canvas should capture **one business model story at a time**. If you try to combine multiple archetypes in one canvas, the result becomes blurry: the customer changes, the problem changes, the channels change, and the economics change.

Archetypes help you answer questions like:

- Who pays?
- Who uses the product?
- Who receives the value?
- What currency is exchanged - money, attention, engagement, or something else?
- What needs to be validated first?

The desirability chapter adds an important refinement: the answers should be grounded in the customer's current situation and the old solution they already use. A strong archetype definition makes it easier to identify the real job-to-be-done and the switching trigger that motivates change. This connects archetypes to [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] and [[concepts/customer-forces-model]]. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp. 108-132]

The viability chapter shows the numerical side: once the model type is clear, you can estimate how many customers you need, what they pay, what churn looks like, and whether the model can realistically reach your minimum success criteria. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172]

The feasibility chapter adds a rollout dimension. Different archetypes can require different stage-based launches because the hardest risks may be on the demand side, supply side, or both. That makes archetypes useful not only for modeling the idea, but for deciding how to stage the path to launch. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]], pp. 179-209]

The communication chapter extends the same logic into pitching: different audiences care about different parts of the archetype. Investors care most about market size, traction, margins, and defensibility; customers care about the problem, promise, and price; advisors care about learning and feedback. Archetype clarity therefore also improves how you frame your story for each worldview. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp. 210-228]

> [!note]
> The validation section reinforces archetypes in practice: once a team starts running experiments, it becomes obvious that different business models need different bottlenecks fixed and different validation loops. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]], pp. 229-237]

## The Three Archetypes

### 1. Direct Business Models

In a direct model, the user and customer are the same person or organization. This is the simplest and most common archetype.

**Example from the source:** Starbucks

- Customer: coffee drinker
- Problem: few choices for freshly brewed, high-quality coffee
- Value: a "third place" between work and home
- Revenue: coffee sales

In a direct model, the Lean Canvas should usually represent the total addressable market as a single customer segment, while the **early adopters** box narrows the starting segment.

Direct models also benefit from desirability stress testing because the problem can be framed against a familiar existing alternative: what customers are currently doing to get the job done. The more clearly that old solution is broken by a switching trigger, the easier it becomes to explain why the new product should matter. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp. 108-132]

From a viability perspective, direct models are often the easiest to model with the customer factory because acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral all happen within one customer relationship. But even here, acquisition cost, retention, and price must align with the minimum success criteria. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172]

### 2. Multisided Business Models

In a multisided model, the business serves two different groups:

- **users** who consume the product or service
- **customers** who pay for access to those users or to some derivative value created by them

**Example from the source:** Facebook

- Users: college students
- Customers: advertisers
- User value: connect and share with friends
- Customer value: reach a highly segmented, active audience

This archetype requires modeling the business from both perspectives. Users may not pay directly, but they still produce value that customers pay to access.

This is closely related to [[concepts/network-effects]] and [[concepts/traction]], since high user engagement can make the customer-side value stronger.

The desirability chapter reinforces a key warning for multisided models: you cannot define the user side in isolation from the customer side. If the customer side is driven by a different job-to-be-done than the user side, you need to surface both clearly on the canvas and validate each side separately. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp. 108-132]

In viability terms, multisided models often require a careful distinction between user traction and monetization traction. A platform can look healthy on one side while failing on the other, so the customer factory logic has to be adapted to show how user engagement becomes monetizable value. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172]

### 3. Marketplace Business Models

Marketplace models are a more specific kind of multisided model with **buyers** and **sellers**.

**Example from the source:** Airbnb

- Buyers: guests looking for rooms
- Sellers: hosts with available space
- Marketplace role: connects both sides and enables exchange

Marketplaces are trickier than direct models because both sides must be activated. The business must solve the "chicken-and-egg" problem of attracting supply and demand at the same time.

The desirability framing matters here too: marketplace founders must understand the switching trigger for both sides. Buyers and sellers often have different existing alternatives, different friction points, and different reasons to switch. A useful archetype definition therefore reduces the chance of mixing buyer and seller assumptions on the same canvas. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp. 108-132]

Viability is especially sensitive for marketplaces because the model often depends on reaching a critical mass of both supply and demand. Acquisition, retention, and referral are usually more complex, and the minimum success criteria can be harder to meet unless the marketplace gains strong network effects or a very efficient matching system. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172]

## Archetypes and Lean Canvas Design

Maurya's key rule is: **model one business story per Lean Canvas**.

If your idea contains multiple archetypes, split it into separate canvases. This prevents the canvas from becoming too broad and helps you identify which model has the strongest fit.

This is especially important when your idea seems to serve multiple groups. For example, a product may appear to target both consumers and businesses, or both users and advertisers. In that case, each group may require a different problem statement, different channels, and different metrics.

The desirability chapter sharpens this further: if you start with a category like AR/VR, you can accidentally force multiple customer stories into the same canvas. Maurya shows that Steve's first canvases are too solution-centered, and that the real work is to split and reframe them around distinct customer jobs - such as helping developers get billable work, helping retailers sell more furniture, or helping architects visualize homes. Archetype clarity therefore supports better canvas design and better problem framing. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp. 108-132; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp. 300-345]

The viability chapter adds that splitting canvases is not only about clarity but also about economics. Different archetypes imply different price points, churn rates, acquisition burdens, and minimum success criteria. If one model cannot plausibly reach the target on paper, it may deserve a separate canvas or be discarded entirely. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172]

The feasibility chapter adds one more reason to split: if a model's rollout path is fundamentally different, it likely needs a separate launch strategy too. A model that can start with direct outreach and a simple MVP is not the same as one that must stage supply, demand, and infrastructure before launch. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]], pp. 179-209]

The communication chapter adds a final layer: archetype clarity helps you tailor your pitch. Investors want market size, traction, margins, and defensibility; customers want the problem, promise, and price; advisors want to understand what you have learned and what you are testing next. If your archetype is fuzzy, your pitch will be fuzzy too. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp. 210-228]

> [!note]
> In the validation section, archetype thinking becomes more concrete: once teams start testing, they discover that bottlenecks, metrics, and experiments often differ depending on whether the model is direct, multisided, or marketplace. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-II-Validation]], pp. 229-237]

## Relationship to Validation

Archetype clarity improves validation because each model has different risks:

- **Direct models** often hinge on whether customers want the product and will pay.
- **Multisided models** often require proving engagement on one side and monetization on the other.
- **Marketplace models** often need proof that both sides can be acquired and matched efficiently.

This matters because risk prioritization depends on knowing what kind of model you are testing.

The desirability chapter shows that validation starts with the customer's current solution, the switching trigger, and the promised better outcome. Archetypes determine which side of the model you should interview first and which alternative you are trying to displace. That makes archetype choice foundational to [[concepts/risk-prioritization]] and [[concepts/customer-discovery]]. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-2-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Desirability]], pp. 108-132]

The viability chapter shows the same idea in numerical form: once the archetype is clear, you can estimate how many customers, what price, what churn, and what referral rate are required to make the model work. That makes archetypes foundational to [[concepts/customer-factory-blueprint]] and [[concepts/fermi-estimate]]. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172]

The feasibility chapter adds a final layer: archetype clarity helps you decide whether to use a now-next-later rollout plan, a Wizard-of-Oz MVP, or some other staged launch pattern. In other words, the archetype informs not just what you build, but how you sequence the build. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]], pp. 179-209]

## Common Uses in the Book

In the source document, Maurya uses archetypes to help Steve realize that his original canvas contains multiple overlapping business models. That realization leads him to split the idea into more specific variants instead of trying to solve everything on one canvas.

This is a practical example of how archetype thinking supports [[concepts/problem-solution-fit]] and [[concepts/innovation-trinity]] by clarifying the structure of the business before deeper testing. In Chapter 2, the same logic is extended into desirability testing: the customer's current alternative, the switching trigger, and the desired outcome all need to fit the archetype you are actually pursuing.

Chapter 3 then shows why this matters economically: once you know the archetype, you can ask whether the model can produce enough active customers, revenue, and retention to reach the minimum success criteria. Chapter 4 completes the picture by showing that the archetype also affects how you stage the rollout and which risks you tackle first. Chapter 5 adds that archetype clarity improves pitch framing because investors, customers, and advisors each care about different parts of the story. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-3-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Viability]], pp. 133-172; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-4-Stress-Test-Your-Idea-for-Feasibility]], pp. 179-209; Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-5-Communicate-Your-Idea-Clearly-and-Concisely]], pp. 210-228]

Chapter 8 adds a new, deeper layer: archetypes should be grounded in jobs-based customer stories, not just market labels. The homebuilder and AR/VR examples show that different customers can share a broad category yet have distinct triggering events, desired outcomes, and existing alternatives. This means archetypes are not just about who the company serves in theory; they are about how real customer journeys cluster in practice. [Source: [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-8-Understand-Your-Customers-Better-Than-They-Do]], pp. 300-345]

## Summary

Business model archetypes are a simplification tool for startup strategy. They help founders distinguish between direct, multisided, and marketplace models, choose the right customer perspective, and keep each Lean Canvas focused on one coherent business story.

The deeper lesson from the desirability, viability, feasibility, communication, validation, and customer-discovery chapters is that archetypes are only useful when they are anchored in the customer's actual job, current workaround, reason to switch, economics, rollout path, and audience worldview. A clean archetype without those elements is still too abstract to validate.

## Deep Study

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A business model archetype is the basic shape of how a startup works. Some businesses sell directly to the person who uses the product, some sell to one group while another group uses it, and some connect buyers and sellers.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like choosing the format of an event. A direct model is a ticketed concert where one audience pays one organizer. A multisided model is a conference where attendees and sponsors both matter. A marketplace is a bazaar where buyers and sellers meet and exchange value.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: Archetypes are just labels. Reality: They change customer definition, problem framing, pricing, channels, metrics, and rollout strategy.
> Myth: One Lean Canvas can cover everything. Reality: Maurya recommends splitting different business stories into separate canvases.
> Myth: User and customer are always the same. Reality: In multisided and marketplace models, they often differ.
> Myth: Archetype choice only matters early on. Reality: It affects desirability, viability, feasibility, validation, and pitching.
> Myth: Broad customer categories are enough. Reality: Chapter 8 shows that jobs-based segmentation usually gives a much sharper archetype than generic labels alone.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding
> **Q:** What are the three archetypes Maurya describes?  
> **A:** Direct, multisided, and marketplace.
>
> **Q:** Why does archetype clarity matter for desirability testing?  
> **A:** It helps identify the right customer, the existing alternative, and the switching trigger.
>
> **Q:** Why do marketplace models need separate buyer and seller thinking?  
> **A:** Because both sides must be acquired and matched, and they often have different needs and alternatives.
>
> **Q:** How do archetypes affect viability?  
> **A:** They shape pricing, churn, acquisition load, and the customer count needed to hit minimum success criteria.
>
> **Q:** How do jobs-based customer segments refine archetypes?  
> **A:** They replace vague market labels with real customer stories built around triggers, outcomes, and current alternatives.

> [!info]- Why It Matters
> Archetypes keep founders from mixing incompatible assumptions and help them choose the right business model story, validation method, rollout plan, and pitch frame. That makes it easier to move from idea to traction without wasting time on the wrong model.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> A business model archetype is the basic shape of how a startup works: direct, multisided, or marketplace.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> It's like choosing the format of an event: a direct model is one audience paying one organizer, a multisided model is attendees plus sponsors, and a marketplace is buyers meeting sellers.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: Archetypes are just labels. Reality: They change customer definition, problem framing, pricing, channels, metrics, and rollout strategy.
> Myth: One Lean Canvas can cover everything. Reality: Maurya recommends splitting different business stories into separate canvases.
> Myth: User and customer are always the same. Reality: In multisided and marketplace models, they often differ.
> Myth: Archetype choice only matters early on. Reality: It affects desirability, viability, feasibility, validation, and pitching.
> Myth: Broad customer categories are enough. Reality: Chapter 8 shows that jobs-based segmentation usually gives a much sharper archetype than generic labels alone.
