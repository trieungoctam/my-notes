# Customer Jobs-to-be-Done

Customer jobs-to-be-done (JTBD) is the idea that people do not buy products for their features; they "hire" them to accomplish a specific job in a specific situation. The real unit of analysis is not the product, but the outcome the customer is trying to achieve.

In [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], JTBD is the bridge between customer discovery and product design. It helps founders uncover the real problem behind a surface request, identify the trigger that starts a search, understand why a customer switches from one solution to another, and choose a launch path that matches how the job actually gets done.

## Core idea

A job-to-be-done starts with a **triggering event**. Something changes in the customer's world, creating a need or want. That trigger pushes them to look for a way to achieve a desired outcome. They then evaluate existing alternatives, pick one, and use it until the job is done well enough-or until a new trigger causes them to switch.

Maurya emphasizes that JTBD is not just about the functional task. The deeper opportunity is often in the **bigger context**: what the customer really wants to accomplish, improve, express, avoid, or become.

## Why JTBD matters

JTBD prevents founders from getting trapped in solution thinking.

If you ask, "What features should we build?" you may end up optimizing around your own idea. If you ask, "What job is the customer trying to get done, and why are current alternatives failing?" you discover more durable opportunities.

This is especially important in early-stage innovation, where [[concepts/innovators-bias]] can cause teams to invent problems to justify an existing solution. JTBD forces the opposite: start with the customer's current reality, then work outward from there.

Maurya also extends this into the launch phase. Once you start rolling out an MVP, JTBD keeps you focused on the job customers are actually hiring the product to do, which is why launch should be staged, measured through the [[concepts/customer-forces]] lens, and supported by a customer factory that keeps acquiring and learning from the right users.

## The switching story

Running Lean frames innovation as a switch from an old way to a new way.

A useful JTBD story looks like this:
- a customer encounters a triggering event
- the current solution no longer feels good enough
- they compare alternatives
- they choose a new solution
- they expect a better outcome

That is why JTBD is tightly connected to the [[concepts/customer-forces]] model:
- **push** = dissatisfaction or urgency created by the trigger
- **pull** = attraction of the new solution
- **inertia** = comfort with the old solution
- **friction** = anxiety or difficulty with change

## Functional benefit vs. bigger context

Maurya distinguishes between:

- **functional outcome**: the immediate task or utility
- **bigger context**: the deeper life or business goal behind the task

For example:
- a drill bit is not really about making a hole
- a hole is not really about a hole
- the bigger context may be hanging art, decorating a home, or expressing identity

This matters because competition exists in the bigger context, not just inside your product category. A drill bit may compete with Command Strips, not only with other drill bits.

Launch strategy follows the same logic: your product must be positioned around the bigger outcome customers care about, then introduced in batches so you can see whether the job is actually being hired at a small scale before you try to scale it.

> [!note]- JTBD and launch readiness
> In the later launch phase of Running Lean, JTBD shows up in a practical way: keep the customer factory running, roll out in batches, and watch whether early adopters consistently "hire" the product for the intended job. This makes JTBD not just a discovery tool, but a launch and retention lens as well. ## How to discover a job-to-be-done

In the book's process, JTBD is discovered through interviews rather than surveys. The recommended approach is:

1. Start from a **recent use** of an existing alternative.
2. Work backward to the **first thought** and **switching trigger**.
3. Ask what led the customer to choose that alternative.
4. Ask what happened during early use and recurring use.
5. Look for friction, workarounds, and gaps between promised and actual outcomes.
6. Move from surface behavior to the deeper desired outcome.

This turns customer discovery into a timeline of real events rather than a list of guessed preferences.

## Good JTBD questions

Examples of JTBD-oriented questions include:
- What happened that made you start looking for a solution?
- What were you using before?
- Why did you switch?
- What did you hope would be different?
- What was frustrating about the old way?
- What does success look like for you?

These questions help reveal the job, the forces behind change, and the outcome the customer is hiring the product to achieve.

## Connection to product strategy

JTBD is not just a research technique; it is a strategic filter. It helps you decide:
- which customer segment to target
- which existing alternatives you are truly competing against
- what outcome your UVP should promise
- how to prioritize features and offers
- whether the opportunity is big enough to matter
- how to sequence rollout and launch so the product reaches customers when they are most ready to switch

When used well, JTBD keeps the team focused on [[concepts/traction]] and customer value instead of vanity features.

## Key takeaways

- Customers hire products to get a job done.
- Jobs begin with triggers and switching events.
- The real opportunity is often in the bigger context, not the surface feature.
- Existing alternatives are the true competition.
- JTBD is best discovered through interviews, not surveys.
- Understanding the job helps you craft a stronger UVP, offer, rollout plan, and launch strategy.

> [!tip]- ELI5
> People buy products because they need help getting a job done, not just because they like features.

> [!tip]- Real-World Analogy
> A ladder is hired to reach a high place; the ladder is not the goal, the job is.

> [!warning]- Common Misconceptions
> Myth: JTBD is about product features. Reality: It is about the outcome the customer wants.
> Myth: Customers can always tell you their problem directly. Reality: You often need to uncover it by tracing triggers and workarounds.
> Myth: The product category is the competition. Reality: The real competition is whatever customers use now, even outside your category.
> Myth: JTBD is only for research. Reality: It also shapes launch timing, rollout, retention, and pricing.
