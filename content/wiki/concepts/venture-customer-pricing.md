# Venture Customer Pricing

Venture Customer Pricing is the practice of setting early-stage prices based on customer value, the cost of existing alternatives, and the venture's minimum success criteria-not on internal build cost alone.

It is a core part of [[concepts/traction]] and [[concepts/viability]] in [[summaries/Running Lean, 3rd Edition  Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works Ash Maurya z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk-12-Get-Ready-to-Launch]]. In Maurya's framework, pricing is not a late-stage detail; it is one of the riskiest assumptions and one of the strongest levers for making a venture model work.

The bonus material sharpens this further: early-stage pricing should also respect the true cost of your time, the ROI of delegation versus doing it yourself, and the need to preserve time for [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] work. In other words, pricing is not only about what customers will pay; it is also about what your venture can sustainably afford in attention, effort, and runway.

## Core idea

Early pricing should answer four questions:

1. **Can the business model hit its minimum success criteria?**
2. **Does the price make sense relative to the customer's current alternative?**
3. **Does the price match the value promised in the [[concepts/unique-value-proposition]]?**
4. **Does the price justify the use of your time and other scarce resources?**

Maurya repeatedly warns against cost-based pricing. Customers do not care how much it cost you to build the product; they care about the problem they are trying to solve and the outcome they want. That means pricing should be anchored against:

- the **value of the outcome** the product creates,
- the **cost/time/effort** of existing alternatives,
- the **segment** you are trying to serve,
- and the **return on time** the venture consumes.

## Why it matters in Running Lean

Pricing appears in the Lean Canvas, but it becomes especially important in the viability stress test. Steve's first pricing guess of $50/month looked plausible, yet his Fermi estimate showed that it would not support his target of $10M ARR in three years. He had to revisit pricing as a lever and consider whether the market could bear $250/month or even $500/month.

This is a key lesson: **price is part of the product**, and pricing choices shape the rest of the business model. A different price changes how many customers are needed, which customers are viable, and how the venture can scale.

The appendix extends this logic by arguing that time is your scarcest resource, so any work you do should be judged by ROI. If you can buy speed, learning, or leverage cheaper than the opportunity cost of doing it yourself, you usually should. That makes pricing decisions closely connected to [[concepts/bootstrapping]], delegation, and [[concepts/flow]].

## How the concept works

### 1. Anchor against existing alternatives

Customers compare your offer to what they already use. A good price sits between:

- the **floor**: what the current alternative costs in time, money, or effort
- the **ceiling**: the value the customer expects to receive from your offer

This is why Maurya encourages founders to identify the true competition and the real switching trigger before settling on pricing.

### 2. Use pricing to test segment fit

Price is also a segmentation tool. A product at $50/month and a product at $500/month often serve different customers, even if the features are similar. That means pricing helps define [[concepts/customer-segmentation]] and can reveal whether the venture is targeting the right early adopters.

### 3. Match pricing to the business model math

In the book's Fermi estimate, pricing is one of the main inputs for determining how many customers are needed to hit the minimum success criteria. If the required customer count is too high, the venture can fix the model by:

- raising price,
- increasing usage frequency,
- targeting a higher-value segment,
- or redefining the problem being solved.

### 4. Tie price to the offer, not just the software

During offer delivery, the customer is not buying code; they are buying a promise of better results. That is why pricing belongs in the [[concepts/mafia-offer]] and [[concepts/customer-story-pitch]] as well as on the canvas.

### 5. Treat your time as an economic input

The appendix makes a strong bootstrapper's point: sweat equity is not free. Your time has an opportunity cost, and in a startup it should be valued with risk and payback in mind. This means pricing should also support:

- faster learning,
- less wasted effort,
- and a better allocation of founder time.

If a lower-priced path forces you to spend excessive time on non-core work, the "cheap" option may actually be expensive.

## Key patterns from the source

### Pricing is a viability lever

Maurya treats pricing as one of the first levers to check when a business model fails a viability test. If the numbers do not work on paper, the venture needs a stronger price point or a stronger value proposition.

### Pricing is tied to the switching story

The best price is easier to defend when the product is clearly displacing an existing alternative. If the new solution is dramatically better at getting a job done, a premium price becomes easier to justify.

### Pricing should be fair, not arbitrary

The book distinguishes fair pricing from "what the founder feels like charging." Fair pricing is grounded in the customer's current spend, the promised outcome, and the market segment's willingness to pay.

### Pricing can reveal the real market

Steve's journey shows that low pricing can hide a weak model. A higher price can force clarity about whether the venture is actually solving a big enough problem for a big enough customer.

### Pricing should reward speed and leverage

The bonus material adds an operational twist: if hiring, buying tools, or paying for help produces more learning per hour than doing the work yourself, then the pricing and resource model should support that choice. Good venture pricing helps preserve founder time for the highest-leverage tasks.
