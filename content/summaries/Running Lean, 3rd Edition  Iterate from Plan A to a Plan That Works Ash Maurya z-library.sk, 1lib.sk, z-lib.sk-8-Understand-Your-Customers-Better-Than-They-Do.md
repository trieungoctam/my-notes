# Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Summary

Ash Maurya's *Running Lean* presents a systematic approach for turning an initial idea into a business model that works before resources run out. The book argues that early-stage entrepreneurs should not start by building a product first, but by modeling the business, testing the riskiest assumptions, and iterating through staged validation until they reach product/market fit.

## Core thesis

The central claim is that the world has changed: products are easier to build, but competition is much higher, customers have more choices, and investors care more about [[concepts/traction]] than intellectual property. In this environment, the winning move is a [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] mindset: continuously model, prioritize, and test the business model rather than following a build-first or investor-first playbook.

> The book's repeated warning is simple: **don't build something nobody wants**.

## Main framework: Continuous Innovation

Maurya frames entrepreneurship as a three-part loop:

1. **Model** - deconstruct the idea into a business model.
2. **Prioritize** - identify and attack the riskiest assumptions first.
3. **Test** - run small, fast experiments and learn from evidence.

This is supported by a set of 10 mindsets, including:
- your business model is the product
- love the problem, not the solution
- traction is the goal
- right action, right time
- tackle risks in stages
- constraints are a gift
- hold yourself externally accountable
- make many small bets
- make evidence-based decisions
- breakthroughs come from unexpected outcomes

## Part 1: Design

The design phase starts with the [[concepts/lean-canvas]] as a one-page business model blueprint. The book strongly discourages traditional business plans because they are slow, unread, and based on unknowable assumptions.

### Lean Canvas emphasis

The Lean Canvas is used to capture:
- customer segments
- problems
- unique value proposition
- solution
- channels
- revenue streams
- cost structure
- key metrics
- unfair advantage
- existing alternatives
- early adopters

A key design principle is that the solution box should be small relative to the canvas overall: entrepreneurs tend to overfocus on solutions and underfocus on the riskiest parts of the model.

### Model archetypes

The book distinguishes among three business model types:
- **direct** models
- **multisided** models
- **marketplace** models

It shows how each requires a different canvas perspective. For example, Facebook is modeled from both user and advertiser perspectives, while Airbnb is modeled from both guest and host perspectives.

### Avoiding the Goldilocks problem

Maurya warns against making the first canvas too broad or too narrow. The solution is to split a "big idea" into multiple focused canvas variants and compare them. This is especially important when the idea mixes multiple customer segments or multiple business model archetypes.

## Part 1: validation prelude

Before testing in the market, the book urges founders to stress test the idea through three lenses:

- **desirability** - do customers want this?
- **viability** - can you monetize this?
- **feasibility** - can you build this?

These are framed as the [[concepts/innovation-trinity]] of a workable startup.

## Part 2: Desirability

Desirability is about whether customers actually want the thing. The book's key insight is that customers don't care about your solution; they care about desired outcomes and the obstacles that block them.

### Innovator's Bias vs Innovator's Gift

A major theme is the danger of [[concepts/innovators-bias]]: founders unconsciously invent problems that justify the solution they already want to build. The antidote is the [[concepts/innovators-gift]]:

- new problems come from old solutions
- innovation causes a switch from an old way to a better new way
- the best opportunities appear when an old solution breaks or becomes inadequate

### Jobs-to-be-done and switching triggers

The book uses [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] to explain how customer demand forms around triggers and desired outcomes. A job starts with a trigger, often an expectation violation, which creates a push toward change.

It then introduces the [[concepts/customer-forces]] model:
- **push** = motivation to leave the current state
- **pull** = attraction of the new solution
- **inertia** = comfort with the old way
- **friction** = resistance to trying the new way

Successful innovation happens when push + pull outweigh inertia + friction.

### Bigger context

The book argues that strong products are built by moving beyond the immediate product context into the bigger context of customer outcomes. Examples like drill bits, paintings, and music formats show that people rarely want the feature itself; they want the larger job done.

## Part 3: Viability

Viability asks whether the business can become economically large enough to matter. The book replaces heavy financial forecasting with a quick Fermi estimate and a target called [[concepts/minimum-success-criteria]] (MSC).

### Fermi estimate and traction

Instead of projecting vague upside, founders should calculate whether the model can reach a realistic target within three years. Key ideas:
- revenue and profit are trailing indicators
- [[concepts/traction]] is a leading indicator of a working business model
- traction is the rate at which the business model captures monetizable value

### Customer factory

Maurya uses a [[concepts/customer-factory]] metaphor to define traction as throughput. The customer lifecycle is broken into:
- acquisition
- activation
- retention
- revenue
- referral

These steps help founders estimate how many customers, leads, conversion rates, and churn levels are needed to hit the MSC.

### Pricing as a lever

A major viability insight is that pricing is often underutilized. The book encourages value-based pricing anchored against:
- the cost of current alternatives
- the value delivered by the UVP

If the model fails, founders can revisit:
- pricing
- the problem being solved
- the customer segment
- or, if necessary, the target goal itself

## Part 4: Feasibility

Feasibility asks whether the product can be built and rolled out in a manageable way. The answer is not a product roadmap, but a [[concepts/traction-roadmap]].

### Traction roadmap and now-next-later

Maurya recommends a stage-based rollout plan:
- **Now** - problem/solution fit
- **Next** - product/market fit
- **Later** - scale

The roadmap helps founders focus on the right risks at the right time. Early on, the goal is not growth optimization but learning and demand validation.

### Right action, right time

The book warns against premature optimization, such as:
- hiring too early
- optimizing for scale before demand exists
- building a full product before validating the offer

The right approach is to prioritize the current bottleneck and move through 90-day cycles.

## Part 5: Communication and pitching

The final major theme is communication. To get buy-in from investors, advisors, cofounders, or customers, founders need to tell a coherent story that matches the audience's worldview.

### Worldviews

The book distinguishes three audience frames:
- **investor worldview**: market size, traction, margins, defensibility
- **customer worldview**: problem, promise, value, price
- **advisor worldview**: learning, feedback, and external accountability

### Business model pitch deck

Maurya provides a 10-slide pitch structure that mirrors the validation sequence:
- why now
- what's at stake
- what's broken
- the fix
- your moat
- how you make money
- key milestones
- current progress
- team
- ask

The pitch should be framed as a learning conversation first, not a hard sell.

## Part 6: 90-day cycles and experimentation

The book's operational engine is the 90-day cycle. Each cycle includes:
- modeling
- prioritizing
- testing
- review

This is broken into two-week sprints and backed by ceremonies such as kickoff, planning, standups, sprint reviews, and cycle reviews.

### Effective experiments

The book gives a strong methodology for experiments:
- declare expected outcomes upfront
- make hypotheses falsifiable
- use ranges, not false precision
- use time boxes
- measure actions, not words
- use control groups where possible
- treat qualitative discovery and quantitative traction as complementary

## Part 7: Problem discovery and customer understanding

The latter chapters focus on customer discovery interviews. Maurya argues that surveys and focus groups are usually too shallow for early-stage discovery because they assume you already know the right questions and answers.

### Interview approach

The recommended approach is:
- interview people face to face when possible
- avoid pitching
- anchor on a recent event or existing alternative
- reconstruct the timeline of the job being done
- ask about facts, not hypotheticals
- focus on the switch, friction, and outcome
- search for patterns across 10-30 interviews

### Customer Forces Canvas

Insights from interviews are captured on the [[concepts/customer-forces]] canvas, which organizes findings into:
- push
- pull
- inertia
- friction
- next summit

These stories are then grouped into jobs-based customer segments.

## Key recurring examples

The book uses several repeated case studies to illustrate the framework:
- Steve, a founder with an AR/VR idea, who repeatedly falls into build-first thinking
- Larry, who instead starts with traction, problem focus, and staged validation
- a homebuilder uncovering seasonal triggers for moving to a new house
- drill bits displaced by alternatives that solve the bigger job
- Tesla, which used staged rollout and a Wizard-of-Oz-style MVP
- the Lean Canvas itself, which was improved through the same process it teaches

## Overall takeaway

The book's core message is that startups should not be treated as execution plans, but as systems for learning. A startup's first job is not building the product; it is discovering a business model that can capture customer value repeatably and scalably.

The practical path is:

1. model the idea
2. stress test desirability, viability, and feasibility
3. run 90-day validation cycles
4. interview customers deeply
5. build only after proving demand
6. communicate the story clearly enough to earn buy-in

In short, *Running Lean* teaches how to move from Plan A to a plan that works-before time, money, or momentum run out.
