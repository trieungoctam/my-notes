# Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Summary

Ash Maurya's *Running Lean* presents a systematic approach for turning an initial idea into a working business model by iterating from **Plan A** toward a plan that works. The book argues that startup success is less about having a perfect vision and more about rigorously testing assumptions, learning quickly, and focusing on the riskiest uncertainties first.

## Core Thesis

The central claim is that modern startups operate in a new world of lower build costs, higher competition, and greater customer choice. In this environment, teams should not start with a build-first or investor-first mindset. Instead, they should adopt a **traction-first** approach that prioritizes learning, customer validation, and evidence-based decision-making.

The book's framework is built around the idea that:

- the **business model is the product**
- the goal is **traction**, not just output
- **problems come before solutions**
- teams should test the **riskiest assumptions** first
- progress comes through repeated **Model → Prioritize → Test** cycles

## The Continuous Innovation Framework

The book introduces the [[concepts/continuous-innovation]] perspective as a "framework of frameworks," combining ideas from Lean Startup, Design Thinking, Business Model Design, jobs-to-be-done, and systems thinking.

The framework is organized into three main phases:

1. **Design** - deconstruct the idea into a business model
2. **Validation** - test the model with evidence, not faith
3. **Growth** - build repeatable traction and prepare to scale

### Supporting Models

Two important models recur throughout the book:

- **Lean Canvas** - a one-page business model sketch used to capture assumptions quickly
- **Customer Factory** - a systems view of how a business turns visitors into customers through acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral

Together, these models help teams think about both the structure of the business and the dynamics of growth.

## Lean Canvas and Business Model Design

The Lean Canvas replaces heavyweight business plans with a fast, one-page model that can be created in about 20 minutes. It forces clarity by breaking the idea into key elements:

- Customer Segments
- Problem
- Unique Value Proposition
- Solution
- Channels
- Revenue Streams
- Cost Structure
- Key Metrics
- Unfair Advantage
- Existing Alternatives

A major theme is that the **solution box should not dominate the canvas**. The book emphasizes that founders are usually most attached to the solution, while the riskiest assumptions are often elsewhere.

The book also advises:

- sketch canvases individually first to avoid [[concepts/groupthink]]
- keep a single business model story per canvas
- split overly broad ideas into multiple canvases when needed
- distinguish **customers** from **users**
- keep the first canvas simple and current, not speculative

## Desirability: Start with Problems, Not Solutions

The book argues that customers do not care about features; they care about outcomes. The key to desirability is identifying a problem worth solving that is already familiar to a customer because of some old or inadequate alternative.

This is framed through the [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]] lens and the idea that innovation is about causing a **switch** from an existing alternative to a better new one.

Key ideas include:

- **Innovator's Bias** - the tendency to invent or exaggerate problems to justify a favored solution
- **Innovator's Gift** - new problems come from old solutions; innovation starts by breaking the old way
- **switching trigger** - the event that makes a customer reconsider the status quo
- **push / pull / inertia / friction** - the four forces that shape switching behavior

The desirability process uses a **Lean(er) Canvas** focused on:

- Customer Segments
- Problem
- Unique Value Proposition

This is where teams look for:

- dissatisfaction with current outcomes
- friction while using existing alternatives
- friction in selecting solutions
- emotional as well as functional reasons to switch

## Viability: Model the Economics Before Building

The viability chapter argues that investors and founders should think in terms of a viable business model, not just a working product. The book recommends using a **Fermi estimate** instead of detailed financial forecasts at the early stage.

### Minimum Success Criteria

A key step is defining **minimum success criteria (MSC)**-a rough but meaningful target for what success must look like within about three years. This is often framed as annual recurring revenue (ARR) or an equivalent business milestone.

The MSC is used to test whether the idea can plausibly support the desired level of business growth.

### Customer Factory and Traction

Traction is defined as the rate at which a business model captures monetizable value from customers. The book breaks traction into five key customer-factory steps:

1. Acquisition
2. Activation
3. Retention
4. Revenue
5. Referral

The customer factory helps founders reason about:

- how customers enter the system
- how they become engaged
- how they stay
- how money is made
- how growth compounds through referrals

### Key Viability Levers

The book emphasizes that if a model fails the viability test, there are only a few ways to fix it:

- raise pricing
- target a bigger or more frequent problem
- choose a different customer segment
- improve retention
- improve conversion rates
- revise the MSC if necessary

This is framed as an economic stress test of the business model.

## Feasibility: Use a Traction Roadmap, Not a Product Roadmap

The book distinguishes **feasibility** from long-range product planning. Rather than assuming you know what you will build for the next 18-24 months, the recommendation is to create a **traction roadmap** that maps milestones from now to scale.

A traction roadmap translates the MSC into staged milestones over time, typically using a **10x growth model** and a [[concepts/hockey-stick-growth]] assumption.

The roadmap typically spans:

- **Now** - problem/solution fit
- **Next** - product/market fit
- **Later** - scale

The point is not to rush into building, but to identify the right action at the right time.

## 90-Day Cycles and Team Process

A major operational idea in the book is the **90-day cycle**. Each cycle includes:

- modeling
- prioritizing
- testing
- review

These cycles are designed to keep teams externally accountable and focused on the current constraint.

### Principles of the Cycle

- align on goals, assumptions, and constraints
- prioritize the riskiest assumptions
- place many small bets
- make evidence-based decisions
- use short feedback loops
- treat uncertainty as a reason for experimentation, not delay

The book also encourages a minimum viable team with complementary skills, often described as:

- hacker
- designer
- hustler

## Customer Discovery and the Customer Forces Model

A major validation tool in the book is the **problem discovery interview**, which is used to understand how customers currently solve a job and what causes them to switch.

The book strongly discourages relying on surveys or focus groups for early discovery. Instead, it advocates:

- one-on-one interviews
- open-ended questions
- reconstruction of the customer timeline
- focusing on actual past behavior, not hypothetical intent

The result of these interviews is summarized in the **Customer Forces Canvas**, which captures:

- Push - motivation for progress
- Pull - attraction of the chosen solution
- Inertia - resistance from habits or the status quo
- Friction - resistance to adopting the new solution
- Actual outcome - what happened
- Next summit - what the customer does next

This supports [[concepts/customer-forces]] and [[concepts/customer-discovery]] as cross-document themes.

## Jobs-Based Segmentation

The book argues that customer segmentation should be based on jobs, not demographics alone. The best early adopters are not just "who" the customer is, but **when** they are open to switching.

Jobs-based segmentation focuses on:

- triggering event
- chosen existing alternative
- desired outcome
- switching trigger

This makes the target segment more behaviorally meaningful and helps teams find people who are actually in motion toward a new solution.

## Solution Design and MVP Strategy

Once a big enough problem is found, the next step is designing a solution that causes a switch. The book defines MVP more narrowly and strategically than many startup guides.

The MVP is not just the smallest thing you can build; it is the smallest thing that can:

- deliver value
- create a compelling promise
- fit the business model
- be launched quickly enough to matter

### The 5 P's of MVP

- **Problem**
- **Promise**
- **Price**
- **People**
- **Packaging**

The book also introduces several MVP validation recipes:

- **Concierge MVP** - deliver value manually as a service before automating
- **Wizard-of-Oz MVP** - fake the product with manual or existing tools behind the scenes
- **Foot-in-the-Door MVP** - deliver the smallest useful entry point to get inside the customer's world

The goal is to launch fast, learn quickly, and replace manual work with automation only after learning.

## Offer Delivery and the "Mafia Offer"

Before building the full MVP, the book recommends delivering a **mafia offer**: an offer customers cannot refuse because it is highly compelling, concrete, and tied to a real problem.

A mafia offer combines:

- a clear UVP
- a demo
- a call to action
- a fair price anchored to value and existing alternatives

This stage is crucial because the book argues that customers buy a **promise of something better**, not a finished product.

The pitch process is designed to:

- test desirability
- validate willingness to commit
- uncover objections
- refine the offer
- secure tangible commitments before building

## Story-Based Pitching

The book recommends structuring pitches as a customer story rather than a product feature dump. The pitch should be framed for different audiences:

- **customers** care about the problem and the promise
- **investors** care about traction, market size, margins, and defensibility
- **advisors** are best approached through a learning frame rather than a sales frame

A good pitch is organized into four acts:

1. Setup - big context and switching trigger
2. Confrontation - break the old way
3. Resolution - demonstrate the better new way
4. Call-to-action - ask for the switch

This is tied to the [[concepts/hero's-journey]] style structure and the idea that your customer is the hero, your competition is the villain, and you are the guide.

## Practical Takeaways

The book's practical message is consistent throughout:

- don't start by building
- don't rely on intuition alone
- don't overinvest in plans nobody can validate
- start with problems and existing alternatives
- use rapid models to expose risk
- test assumptions in the right order
- iterate through small, focused cycles
- optimize for learning speed

## Overall Contribution

*Running Lean* offers a disciplined operating system for early-stage entrepreneurship. It is especially focused on helping founders and product teams avoid the build trap, uncover real customer pain, validate economic viability, and launch with a strategy that can be tested before it is fully built.
