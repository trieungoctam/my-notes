# Running Lean, 3rd Edition - Preface & Introduction Summary

Ash Maurya frames this edition of *Running Lean* around a central claim: in today's fast-moving, low-barrier, highly competitive market, the old build-first or investor-first startup playbook is too risky. The book's contribution is a systematic alternative: a **Continuous Innovation Framework** for moving from an initial idea to a business model that works through repeated cycles of modeling, prioritizing, and testing.

## Core argument

The introduction contrasts two founders, Steve and Larry, to show that success depends less on talent or technical skill and more on **mindset** and **process**.

- **Steve** follows a build-first path: he spends time perfecting a product, seeks funding without traction, and gets trapped in a cycle of delayed launches and weak validation.
- **Larry** follows a traction-first path: he starts with the problem, validates demand, defines an MVP only after discovering customer needs, and grows through staged experiments.

The lesson is that, under conditions of extreme uncertainty, the main challenge is not "Can we build it?" but **"Should we build it?"**

## Main concepts introduced

### 1. The new world of entrepreneurship

Maurya argues that the market has changed:

- It is **cheaper and easier to build products**.
- That has produced **more competitors** and **more customer choice**.
- Investors now care less about intellectual property and more about **traction**.

In this environment, product quality alone is not enough. A viable business must demonstrate that real customers want it.

### 2. Traction as evidence of a working business model

Traction is defined as evidence that people beyond the founders care about the idea. It is not merely early usage or publicity; it is proof that the underlying business model can work.

This reframes the startup goal:

- Not just building a solution.
- Not just raising money.
- But proving a **repeatable and scalable business model**.

### 3. The build trap

The book warns against the [[build trap]]: the tendency to keep adding features and polishing a product while never validating whether the market wants it.

Symptoms include:

- endless feature chasing,
- overreliance on assumptions,
- low retention,
- "killer feature" thinking,
- and building something nobody wants.

### 4. Problem-first thinking

A major shift in the book is from solution obsession to **problem discovery**:

- Customers care about their problems, not your solution.
- A strong product begins with a well-understood customer problem.
- The right sequence is: **understand problem → define solution → craft offer → build solution**.

This is contrasted with the common mistake of defining an MVP before understanding demand.

### 5. MVP redefined

Maurya's MVP is not "the first thing you can ship." Instead, it is the **smallest solution that creates, delivers, and captures customer value**.

Important implication:

- An MVP should come *after* problem discovery and offer validation.
- Launching a rough product too early without customer understanding can still fail.

### 6. Demo-Sell-Build vs Build-Demo-Sell

A key process reversal in the book is:

- Traditional: **Build → Demo → Sell**
- Maurya's approach: **Demo → Sell → Build**

This sequence reduces risk by validating demand before investing heavily in development.

### 7. Model-Prioritize-Test and 90-day cycles

The Continuous Innovation Framework is organized around recurring cycles:

1. **Model** the business.
2. **Prioritize** the riskiest assumptions.
3. **Test** them with fast experiments.

These cycles run on a 90-day cadence and are designed to keep teams externally accountable, focused, and learning quickly.

### 8. Risks shift over time

One of the book's strongest claims is that the riskiest assumption changes as a business evolves.

- Early on, customer and market risks are more important than technical risks.
- Teams should focus on the **riskiest parts of the business model first**, not what is easiest.

This is a central principle of [[risk prioritization]] and [[business model validation]].

### 9. Small bets and evidence-based decisions

Instead of making large bets, Maurya recommends:

- many small experiments,
- fast learning loops,
- and decisions grounded in evidence.

This helps teams avoid catastrophic failures and course-correct early.

## Key frameworks and tools referenced

The introduction previews several tools that are central to the book:

- [[Lean Canvas]]
- Customer interview scripts
- Traction roadmap
- Customer Forces Canvas
- Business modeling templates
- Small, staged validation experiments

These tools support the broader [[continuous innovation]] approach.

## What the story of Steve and Larry teaches

### Steve's path

Steve represents the common but risky path:

- gets attached to a big idea,
- builds in isolation,
- delays launch,
- seeks funding before traction,
- misreads investor rejection,
- and eventually discovers competitors have moved ahead.

### Larry's path

Larry represents the new approach:

- starts with the problem,
- validates early,
- defines a compelling offer,
- gets paying customers before building fully,
- recruits a team with traction,
- and iterates toward product/market fit.

The contrast shows that the difference is not raw skill but **mindset**:

- Steve = artist/maker mindset
- Larry = innovator mindset

## The 10 mindsets previewed

Maurya introduces ten mindsets that support the framework:

### Model
1. Your business model is the product.
2. Love the problem, not your solution.
3. Traction is the goal.

### Prioritize
4. Right action, right time.
5. Tackle your riskiest assumptions in stages.
6. Constraints are a gift.
7. Hold yourself externally accountable.

### Test
8. Place many small bets.
9. Make evidence-based decisions.
10. Breakthrough requires unexpected outcomes.

These mindsets are presented as the foundation for the rest of the book.

## Structure of the book previewed here

The introduction explains the book's three-part structure:

### Part 1: Design
Focuses on deconstructing Plan A into a business model and stress testing it.

### Part 2: Validation
Focuses on validating the model with evidence, especially via customer interviews and the Demo-Sell-Build process.

### Part 3: Growth
Focuses on building an MVP, rolling out in stages, and moving toward product/market fit.

## Why it matters

The introduction argues that modern entrepreneurship requires a new operating system:

- ideas must be validated, not just imagined;
- products must be grounded in customer problems;
- teams must learn quickly and continuously;
- and success depends on finding a repeatable business model under uncertainty.

The overarching promise of the book is that the path from idea to product/market fit does not have to be random or chaotic. With the right process, entrepreneurs can systematically move from [[Plan A]] to [[product/market fit]].
