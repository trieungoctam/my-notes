# Summary of *Running Lean, 3rd Edition* - Preface, Introduction, Part I, Chapter 1, and Chapter 2 (pages 6-132)

## Main idea
This document argues that startups should not begin by building a product first, but by **deconstructing an idea into a business model**, then **stress testing the riskiest assumptions** before investing heavily in development. The core message is that in today's environment, success depends less on having a perfect vision and more on rapidly finding a **plan that works** through iterative learning.

## Key contributions
- Introduces the **Continuous Innovation Framework** as a system for navigating extreme uncertainty.
- Reframes the startup challenge from **build-first** to **traction-first**.
- Presents the **Lean Canvas** as a 1-page model for capturing and testing a business model.
- Explains the difference between **customers** and **users**, and why early adopters matter.
- Defines a practical sequence for validation: **desirability**, **viability**, and **feasibility**.
- Introduces the **Innovator's Gift**: new opportunities come from breaking old solutions, not inventing imaginary problems.

## Core narrative: Steve vs. Larry
The introduction uses two contrasting founders:

- **Steve** follows a traditional **build-first** path. He spends months building in isolation, struggles to recruit support, raises little traction, and falls into the **build trap**.
- **Larry** follows a **traction-first** path. He starts with problems, tests assumptions in stages, gets paying customers early, and builds momentum before scaling.

This contrast illustrates that the key difference is not talent or effort, but **mindset**. Larry succeeds because he focuses on the riskiest assumptions first and treats the business model itself as the product. This theme connects strongly to [[concepts/continuous-innovation]], [[concepts/traction]], and [[concepts/problem-first-thinking]].

## Continuous Innovation Framework
The book's framework combines ideas from Lean Startup, Design Thinking, Business Model Design, jobs-to-be-done, Systems Thinking, and Behavior Design into a unified process.

### The three stages
1. **Model**
   - Deconstruct the idea into a business model.
   - Use the Lean Canvas.
2. **Prioritize**
   - Identify the riskiest assumptions.
   - Focus on what matters most, not what is easiest.
3. **Test**
   - Run small experiments.
   - Learn quickly and revise the model.

### Associated mindsets
The introduction lists 10 mindsets that support the framework, including:
- Your business model is the product.
- Love the problem, not your solution.
- Traction is the goal.
- Right action, right time.
- Tackle your riskiest assumptions in stages.
- Constraints are a gift.
- Hold yourself externally accountable.
- Place many small bets.
- Make evidence-based decisions.
- Breakthrough requires unexpected outcomes.

These mindsets support a broader concept page around [[concepts/mindset-shifts]] and [[concepts/risk-prioritization]].

## The Lean Canvas
The Lean Canvas is presented as a lightweight alternative to the traditional business plan.

### Why it matters
- A business plan is too slow, too long, and mostly guesswork.
- A Lean Canvas can be completed in about **20 minutes**.
- It turns vague ideas into explicit assumptions that can be tested.
- It is especially useful under conditions of uncertainty.

### Canvas structure
The main boxes include:
- Problem
- Solution
- Unique Value Proposition
- Customer Segments
- Early Adopters
- Channels
- Revenue Streams
- Cost Structure
- Key Metrics
- Unfair Advantage
- Existing Alternatives
- High-Level Concept

The book emphasizes that the **Solution** box should remain small relative to the rest, because founders tend to over-focus on solutions and under-focus on the rest of the model. This connects to [[concepts/lean-canvas]] and [[concepts/business-model-design]].

## Business model archetypes
The text distinguishes three broad model types:

- **Direct**: users are customers
- **Multisided**: users and customers are different groups
- **Marketplace**: buyers and sellers interact through the platform

This distinction is important because different archetypes require different canvases and validation strategies. The document gives examples using Starbucks, Facebook, and Airbnb.

## Refining the canvas
The first Lean Canvas is only a starting point. The book warns against two common errors:

- **Too broad**: trying to model too many customer stories on one canvas
- **Too narrow**: getting stuck on a local maximum and missing a better opportunity

The recommended solution is to **split one big idea into multiple specific variants** and test them separately. This is a key part of [[concepts/idea-varianting]] and [[concepts/local-maxima-in-innovation]].

## Desirability stress test
Chapter 2 focuses on whether customers actually want the idea.

### Main principle
Customers do not buy solutions; they hire products to achieve desired outcomes. Therefore, the job is to uncover the customer's real problem in the context of their current alternatives.

### Innovator's Bias
Founders often unconsciously distort customer problems to justify the solution they already want to build. This is called **Innovator's Bias**.

### Innovator's Gift
The antidote is the **Innovator's Gift**:
- New problems come from old solutions.
- Innovation happens when a new solution causes people to switch from an existing alternative.
- The right opportunity is not a made-up problem, but a real pain in an established workflow.

This aligns strongly with [[concepts/innovators-bias]], [[concepts/jobs-to-be-done]], and [[concepts/switching-triggers]].

## Jobs-to-be-done and switching triggers
The chapter explains that:
- All jobs start with a **trigger**.
- A **switching trigger** includes an expectation violation that makes the old solution feel inadequate.
- People resist change due to **inertia** and **friction**.
- A new solution must create enough **pull** to overcome those forces.

The formula presented is:
**PUSH + PULL > INERTIA + FRICTION**

This is central to [[concepts/customer-forces-model]] and [[concepts/jtbd]].

## Bigger context vs. solution context
A major conceptual distinction in the chapter is between:

- **Solution context**: features and benefits of the product itself
- **Bigger context**: the customer's desired outcome or job

The book argues that products should be framed in the bigger context because that is where people decide whether a switch is worthwhile. This supports [[concepts/outcomes-vs-features]] and [[concepts/bigger-context-thinking]].

## Practical process for desirability testing
To stress test desirability, the book recommends revisiting the Lean(er) Canvas in a specific order:

1. Customer Segments
2. Early Adopters
3. Existing Alternatives
4. Problem
5. Unique Value Proposition

Key guidance:
- Keep customer segments simple.
- Make early adopters specific, especially by identifying switching triggers.
- Define competition broadly as existing alternatives, not just direct competitors.
- Describe problems relative to those alternatives.
- Anchor the UVP in a compelling reason to switch.

This is a practical framework for [[concepts/desirability-testing]].

## Example: Steve's AR/VR idea
Steve initially frames his startup around AR/VR itself, but through the Lean Canvas process he starts to see the problem with that framing.

### What he learns
- His early adopters are too broad when described only as "software developers."
- His solution is still centered on the AR/VR category rather than the customer's real job.
- The true value is not "AR/VR apps" but what those apps help customers achieve:
  - developers want billable work
  - retailers want more sales
  - architects want better visualization

This is the chapter's clearest example of moving from **solution thinking** to **problem-first, outcome-based thinking**.

## Structural roadmap of the book
The book is organized into three parts:
- **Part 1: Design** - deconstruct and stress test the business model
- **Part 2: Validation** - test the model with interviews and experiments
- **Part 3: Growth** - launch, iterate, and move toward product/market fit

The overall journey is described as moving from **Plan A** through iteration toward **a plan that works**, not by intuition alone, but by a disciplined process of learning.

## Bottom line
The document argues that startup success comes from systematically discovering a real customer problem, modeling the business around it, and testing the riskiest assumptions early. Instead of building in hopes of traction, founders should seek traction as evidence that they are building the right thing.
